# Huffman Prairie Flying Field

Huffman Prairie Flying Field is a low, grassy field in Greene County, Ohio, about eight miles east of Dayton, where Wilbur and Orville Wright did the unglamorous work that turned a few short hops into a usable flying machine.[^s1][^s3] Their December 1903 flights at Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, had shown that a powered, piloted airplane could leave the ground; at Huffman Prairie in 1904 and 1905 they learned to turn, circle, bank and stay aloft for more than half an hour, producing what the National Park Service calls the world's first practical airplane, the Wright Flyer III.[^s2][^s3] Today the field lies inside Wright-Patterson Air Force Base and is one unit of Dayton Aviation Heritage National Historical Park.[^s1][^s4]

## History

Long before it was a flying field, the land was wet bottomland. The Park Service notes that Paleo-Indian people may have passed through around 11,000 BCE, that Adena burial mounds nearby date to about 1000 BCE, and that the Miami and later the Shawnee settled the region in the 18th century.[^s2] By the late 1800s it was a pasture for horses and cows, too wet for crops; Orville had visited it as a high-school student on plant-collecting trips with his biology teacher.[^s7]

After returning from North Carolina, the brothers wanted a field close to home in Dayton. The banker Torrence Huffman let them use his pasture rent-free, so long as they moved his cattle out of the way, and it sat beside Simms Station on an interurban line that locals nicknamed the "Damned Slow and Uncertain."[^s1][^s3] Wilbur described the field to the engineer Octave Chanute in June 1904 as an old swamp dotted with grassy hummocks and fenced with barbed wire.[^s2][^s7]

The first season was humbling. A press demonstration on May 23, 1904, failed when the engine misfired, and the reporters went away convinced there was little to report.[^s5] Ohio's light winds made take-offs hard, so on September 7 the brothers began launching from a catapult — a tall derrick with a falling weight.[^s2][^s5] On September 15 they made the first controlled turns in a powered airplane, and on September 20 they flew the first complete circle; the beekeeping-supply maker Amos Root, who watched, published the first eyewitness account of such a flight in his trade journal *Gleanings in Bee Culture*.[^s3][^s5] The 1904 season added up to 105 starts but under an hour of flying — the Park Service counts 49 minutes.[^s1][^s5]

In 1905 they rebuilt the machine as the Flyer III. A crash on July 14 that threw Orville through the upper wing pushed them to enlarge the elevator and move it farther forward, enlarge the rudder and give the pilot separate control of it.[^s6] The changes worked: on October 5, 1905, Wilbur flew about 24 miles in some 39 minutes, longer than all their 1903 and 1904 flying combined.[^s1][^s6] Then they stopped. The War Department had turned down their offer, and the brothers feared that public flights would let rivals copy the design before their patent and a sale were secure, so they hid the dismantled Flyer and did not fly again for nearly three years.[^s6]

The Wrights came back in 1910. Their new Wright Company used the field to test almost every model it built, to base its exhibition team and to run the Wright School of Aviation, which trained 119 students between 1910 and 1916, among them Henry "Hap" Arnold, who later led the U.S. Army Air Corps.[^s1][^s3] In November 1910 a Wright pilot flew a bolt of silk from the field to Columbus, an early commercial air-freight flight.[^s3] In 1917, as the United States entered World War I, the Army leased the surrounding land for a Signal Corps aviation school named Wilbur Wright Field; that installation grew into Wright-Patterson Air Force Base.[^s2][^s3]

## Significance

Huffman Prairie is where flight became practical rather than merely possible: the control methods, the endurance and the dependable launching that the Wrights worked out there are what made the airplane usable.[^s2][^s3] The site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1990, and Congress made it part of Dayton Aviation Heritage National Historical Park in the Dayton Aviation Heritage Preservation Act, signed October 16, 1992, which leaves decisions about the land with the Secretary of Defense in consultation with the Secretary of the Interior.[^s2][^s4] The field is also an Ohio Natural Landmark, one of the state's few surviving prairie remnants; the Park Service gives it 84 acres, while older records describe a pasture of about 100 acres.[^s2][^s3]
