  myth: The Battle of New Orleans was fought after the War of 1812 was already over, so it was pointless.
  fact: The Treaty of Ghent was signed on December 24, 1814, but hostilities were to stop only after both governments ratified it, and the United States did not do so until February 1815; the war was still legally on when the battle was fought on January 8, 1815.

review:
  - question: Why did the United States declare war on Britain in 1812, and why do historians argue about it?
    answer: Madison and Congress cited British impressment of American sailors and British limits on neutral trade, alongside British support for Native resistance on the frontier. Most historians treat these maritime grievances as the core cause, but some have argued that the real aim was to seize Canada, while others stress national honor and British hostility to the young republic.
  - question: How could the war end in a treaty that changed no borders and yet still have large consequences?
    answer: The Treaty of Ghent restored prewar territory and did not mention impressment, but the war shattered Tecumseh's confederacy and the Creek Red Sticks, cost the Creeks millions of acres at Fort Jackson, left Native nations without British support, made Andrew Jackson a national hero and helped finish off the Federalist Party.
  - question: What choices did the war present to enslaved African Americans?
    answer: Some Black men joined U.S. forces hoping to earn freedom, while about 4,000 enslaved people in the Chesapeake escaped to British ships after Cochrane's 1814 proclamation; about 200 served as Colonial Marines, and Britain later resettled most in Canada's Maritime provinces or Trinidad.
  - question: Was the Battle of New Orleans fought after the war ended?
    answer: No. It came after the treaty was signed but before the United States ratified it in February 1815, and the treaty required fighting to continue until ratification.

# War of 1812

The War of 1812 was fought between the United States and Great Britain, together with Britain's Canadian colonies and allied Native nations, from June 1812 until early 1815.[^s3][^s2] Americans went to war over the Royal Navy's seizure of sailors from American ships and British limits on neutral trade; they fought on the Great Lakes, in the Chesapeake, on the Gulf Coast and at sea; and they made peace on terms that restored the prewar map.[^s1][^s10] For the Native nations of the Great Lakes and the Southeast, though, the war was a decisive defeat.[^s2]

## Background

Britain was locked in war with Napoleonic France, and both powers restricted American trade. Britain's blockade of neutral shipping and its practice of "impressing" sailors off American ships into the Royal Navy were the grievances the United States cited most.[^s1][^s4] Impressment weighed most heavily on President James Madison.[^s2] In Congress, a group of mostly young Republicans from the South and West known as the War Hawks, led by House Speaker Henry Clay and including John C. Calhoun, pressed for war and saw conquering Canada as a way to force British concessions.[^s4]

On the frontier, the Shawnee leader Tecumseh and his brother Tenskwatawa built a Native alliance centered on Prophetstown, founded in 1808 on the Wabash, to defend Native homelands against American settlement.[^s9] Britain supplied that confederacy, and William Henry Harrison's fight with it at Tippecanoe intensified American calls for war.[^s2]

Madison sent Congress a list of British offenses on June 1, 1812. The House voted for war on June 4, 79 to 49, and the Senate on June 17, 19 to 13; no Federalist voted yes.[^s2][^s4] Madison signed the declaration on June 18.[^s3] Britain had just rescinded its trade-restricting Orders in Council, but the news reached Washington too late.[^s2]

## What happened

The invasions of Canada failed. General William Hull surrendered Detroit and all of Michigan Territory on August 16, 1812, and in January 1813 British, Canadian and Native forces routed a Kentucky army at the River Raisin, where wounded prisoners were killed the next day.[^s11] Tecumseh, given command of Native forces by British general Isaac Brock, was killed at the Battle of the Thames on October 5, 1813, weeks after Oliver Hazard Perry's squadron won the Battle of Lake Erie.[^s9][^s10][^s2] At sea, USS *Constitution* and other frigates won single-ship duels, but the Royal Navy blockaded much of the coast.[^s10]

In the Southeast, the war merged with a conflict inside the Creek (Muscogee) Nation. Andrew Jackson defeated the Red Stick faction at Horseshoe Bend on March 27, 1814, and that August imposed the Treaty of Fort Jackson, which took more than 21 million acres — including nearly eight million from Creeks who had fought on the American side.[^s6]

In 1814, with Napoleon defeated, Britain took the offensive. British troops burned the Capitol and the President's house in Washington on August 24–25.[^s1][^s10] Fort McHenry withstood a 25-hour naval bombardment at Baltimore on September 13–14, inspiring Francis Scott Key's poem "The Star-Spangled Banner," which Congress recognized as the national anthem in 1931.[^s7][^s10] On September 11, a U.S. naval victory on Lake Champlain led a larger British land force to abandon its attack at Plattsburgh, New York.[^s10]

Enslaved African Americans made their own choices. After Vice Admiral Alexander Cochrane's proclamation of April 2, 1814, about 4,000 people in the Chesapeake gained freedom by reaching the British; about 200 men served as Colonial Marines, and Britain later resettled most of the refugees in Canada's Maritime provinces or Trinidad. Other Black men joined U.S. forces hoping to earn their freedom.[^s8]

## Aftermath and legacy

Commissioners signed the Treaty of Ghent on December 24, 1814. It restored captured territory, returned prisoners and promised Native nations their 1811 rights and possessions, but it said nothing about impressment or neutral trade.[^s1][^s13] On January 8, 1815, American forces repulsed a British assault of about 6,000 troops at New Orleans, and the United States ratified the treaty in February 1815.[^s2][^s5] With Napoleon gone, impressment ended in practice.[^s2]

The Native nations were the clearest losers: their confederacies were broken and Britain no longer backed them.[^s2][^s12] The United States ignored the treaty's Article IX promise as it applied to the Creek cession.[^s6] Lewis Cass later used the River Raisin to argue for removal and, as Jackson's secretary of war from 1831, oversaw the Indian Removal Act's implementation.[^s11] At home, New England Federalists who had opposed the war met at the Hartford Convention; their complaints sounded disloyal after the peace, and the party never recovered.[^s2] Jackson's fame soared.[^s2]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians have argued about the war's causes for two centuries. The standard view, associated with Bradford Perkins, Reginald Horsman, Donald Hickey and J. C. A. Stagg, holds that maritime grievances — not a wish to annex Canada or to crush Native resistance — drove the declaration.[^s13] Andrew Lambert counters that Republicans used those grievances as a pretext to seize British territory, while Stagg sees the invasion of Canada as a strategy forced on a country with a small navy; Nicole Eustace emphasizes honor and patriotic emotion.[^s12]

Who won is also debated. Many Americans called it a "Second War of Independence," and Republicans presented the peace as a victory.[^s12][^s13] Lambert argues Britain won because its blockade forced the United States to accept the prewar status quo; Troy Bickham argues Britain lost because American sovereignty was respected; Stagg names the Canadians winners and Native Americans the clear losers.[^s12]

## Connections

- The war was **influenced by** British **impressment** and **occurred during** the **early republic**.[^s4][^s2]
- It was fought in **Canada**, **Washington, D.C.**, **Baltimore** and **New Orleans**.[^s11][^s1][^s7][^s5]
- It was **led** by **James Madison**, **William Henry Harrison** and **Andrew Jackson** on the American side and by **Tecumseh** among Britain's Native allies, and it devastated the **Shawnee** and the **Muscogee (Creek) Nation**.[^s2][^s9][^s6]
- It **led to** the **Treaty of Ghent**, the **Treaty of Fort Jackson**, "**The Star-Spangled Banner**," the **Hartford Convention** and, more distantly, arguments used for the **Indian Removal Act**.[^s1][^s6][^s7][^s2][^s11]
