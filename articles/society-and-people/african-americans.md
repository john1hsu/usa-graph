# African Americans

African Americans, also called Black Americans, are Americans whose origins lie among the peoples of sub-Saharan Africa. The 2020 census counted 41.1 million people who chose Black as their only race (12.4 percent of the population) and 46.9 million who chose it alone or together with another race (14.2 percent).[^s1] Most descend from Africans brought to North America in bondage, while a growing share trace their families to more recent migration from the Caribbean and Africa.[^s2][^s5]

## History

The documented story in English North America begins on August 25, 1619, when the privateer *White Lion* reached Old Point Comfort, Virginia, carrying Africans seized from a Spanish slave ship. They came from the kingdom of Ndongo, in present-day Angola, and colonists bought about 32 of them in exchange for food. Virginia law did not yet define slavery, and their legal status is unclear, but they had been captured and sold against their will. A child named William, born to Isabella and Antoney, is the first known child of African descent in English North America.[^s4]

Over the next two centuries an estimated 388,000 Africans survived the Atlantic crossing and landed in British North America and the United States, a small fraction of the transatlantic trade. In the Chesapeake the enslaved population became self-sustaining through births by about 1730, so the Black population grew mainly by natural increase, and children were born into slavery.[^s5] That history, and the ways enslaved people resisted it, is told in the article on slavery in the United States.

Free Black communities built institutions of their own. Prince Hall founded African Lodge No. 459 in Boston in 1784.[^s7] In Philadelphia, Richard Allen, born enslaved in 1760, bought his freedom in 1780; after he was confined to segregated services at St. George's Methodist Church, he and Absalom Jones founded the Free African Society in 1787. Allen built Mother Bethel in 1793, and in 1816 a Philadelphia meeting formed the African Methodist Episcopal Church and elected him bishop.[^s6] The Grand United Order of Odd Fellows, organized in 1843, is described as the oldest African American mutual aid society.[^s7]

During the Civil War about 179,000 Black men, roughly a tenth of the Union Army, served after the War Department created the Bureau of Colored Troops in May 1863, and about 19,000 more served in the Navy; nearly 40,000 Black soldiers died, most of them from disease.[^s9] In 1862 Elizabeth Keckly and other Black women in Washington, D.C., founded the Contraband Relief Association to supply food, clothing and schooling to newly freed people, and after the war freed families formed burial societies that pooled dues to pay for funerals.[^s7] Higher education grew as well: Cheyney University in Pennsylvania, founded in 1837, was the first historically Black college, and Fisk (1866) and Howard (1867) opened soon after emancipation.[^s8]

Between about 1910 and 1970, some six million Black Southerners moved north and west in the Great Migration, escaping racial violence and Jim Crow and seeking work and schooling. Early migrants went to New York, Chicago, Detroit and Pittsburgh; after World War II many went to Oakland, Los Angeles and other western cities. In their new homes they met restrictive covenants and redlining that produced segregated neighborhoods and widened the racial wealth gap.[^s10]

How the government names the community has changed too. The census added "Free Colored Persons" in 1820, used "Mulatto" in 1850 and reintroduced it in 1910, paired "Negro" with "Black" from 1900, added "Black" to the "Negro" category in 1970, and adopted "Black or African American" in 2000 under federal standards issued in 1997.[^s3]

## Today

The 2020 census showed a community that is both rooted and diverse. The Black alone count was 38.9 million (12.6 percent) in 2010 and 41.1 million in 2020, while the multiracial "in combination" count reached 46.9 million.[^s1] When people wrote in a detailed origin, "African American" was the most common answer, given by 53.7 percent of the Black alone population; Jamaican and Haitian were the largest Caribbean groups, each 2.2 percent of the Black alone or in combination population, and Nigerian, Ethiopian, Somali and Ghanaian were the largest sub-Saharan African groups. Texas, Georgia and Florida had the largest African American populations, and Cook County, Illinois, home to Chicago, counted 773,963 people who reported African American, more than any other county.[^s2]

Institutions founded in earlier centuries remain central. In 2020 there were 101 accredited HBCUs, about 3 percent of the nation's four-year colleges.[^s8] The AME Church, born of protest against segregated worship in Philadelphia, traces its founding to the 1816 meeting that made Allen its first bishop.[^s6]

## Connections

Black Americans were participants in the Great Migration, which moved about six million people out of the South between 1910 and 1970.[^s10] They took part in the American Civil War as soldiers and sailors who fought for the Union and for emancipation.[^s9] The descendants of people enslaved in the United States contrast with Black immigrants and their children from the Caribbean and Africa, who share the census category but report distinct national origins such as Jamaican, Haitian or Nigerian.[^s2] African Americans' story requires the history of slavery in the United States, the institution that brought most of their ancestors to North America.[^s5] Their independent churches, colleges and mutual aid societies link to the African Methodist Episcopal Church and to historically Black colleges and universities.[^s6][^s8]
