# Liberty Bell

The Liberty Bell is a bronze bell, cracked and long silent, displayed in Independence National Historical Park in Philadelphia. It was made to hang in the steeple of the Pennsylvania State House, the building now called Independence Hall, and was known for most of its first century simply as the State House bell.[^s1][^s3] Its fame as a symbol of American freedom came later, and from an unexpected direction: abolitionists who read its inscription as an indictment of slavery.[^s2]

## Origins

On November 1, 1751, the superintendents of the State House ordered a bell of about 2,000 pounds from London, specifying a verse from Leviticus for its rim.[^s4] The National Park Service credits Isaac Norris, Speaker of the Pennsylvania Assembly, with the order, and suggests the verse may have marked the fiftieth anniversary of William Penn's 1701 Charter of Privileges.[^s1] The bell, cast at Thomas Lester's Whitechapel foundry, cracked on its first test strike after arriving in 1752. Two Philadelphia metalworkers, John Pass and John Stow, melted it down and recast it, and the date 1753 appears on its face.[^s4][^s1] It is about 70 percent copper and 25 percent tin, with traces of other metals.[^s1]

The inscription reads "Proclaim Liberty Throughout All the Land Unto All the Inhabitants thereof," citing Leviticus 25:10, a passage about the Jubilee year in which property was returned and enslaved people freed.[^s1] For decades the bell did ordinary civic work, calling legislators to session and opening courts.[^s4] In the imperial crisis it rang in protest when a ship carrying stamped paper for the Stamp Act arrived in 1765, and it summoned crowds against the Townshend duties, the Tea Act and the Coercive Acts.[^s3] When the British occupied Philadelphia in 1777, residents hid the bell under the floor of the Zion Reformed Church in Allentown so it would not be melted for munitions; it came back in 1778.[^s4][^s3]

## Meaning and practice

The name "Liberty Bell" first appears in print in the February 1835 *Anti-Slavery Record*, published in New York by the American Anti-Slavery Society. A writer who climbed the State House tower read the inscription as a prophecy and said its peals were "a mockery" while about one-sixth of the population was enslaved.[^s2] Abolitionists in New York and Boston repeated the image, and the name slowly spread.[^s3][^s1]

The bell's crack came in the 1840s. A hairline split was drilled out in February 1846, before Washington's Birthday, in an attempt to restore its tone; a second fissure opened, and the bell has not been rung in the ordinary way since.[^s1] A year later the journalist George Lippard published "Ring, Grandfather, Ring," a fictional story of an old bell-ringer pealing the news of independence on July 4, 1776. Retold as fact, it bound the bell to the Declaration in popular memory.[^s3][^s4]

Between 1885 and 1915 the bell traveled by rail to seven expositions, among them New Orleans (1885), Chicago (1893) and San Francisco (1915), each journey widening its audience while worsening the crack.[^s3][^s4] Historian Charlene Mires notes that African Americans were largely absent from the celebrations along these routes, which coincided with a period of segregation and racial violence.[^s6] During World War I, the government made the bell the emblem of Liberty Loan drives that raised more than $17 billion.[^s5]

Other movements claimed it too. Pennsylvania suffragists commissioned a replica, the Justice Bell, which toured all 67 counties in 1915 with its clapper chained; they rang it on Independence Square on September 25, 1920, after the Nineteenth Amendment.[^s3][^s1] Richard R. Wright Sr., a banker born into slavery, began the annual commemoration at the bell that became National Freedom Day, and civil rights demonstrators held sit-ins and vigils there in the 1960s.[^s6][^s3] The National Park Service took custody in 1948, moved the bell to a pavilion for the 1976 Bicentennial, and in 2003 to a new building at Sixth and Market Streets. That site adjoins the house where President Washington held enslaved Africans in the 1790s, a discovery that pushed the park to present slavery alongside liberty.[^s3][^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

Did the bell ring for independence? The National Park Service states there is no evidence it rang on July 4 or July 8, 1776.[^s1] Gary Nash writes that it is believed to have rung when the Declaration was read publicly on July 8,[^s3] while Mires judges that likelier than July 4 but still unproven, given the steeple's poor condition.[^s6]

When did it crack? A tradition places the break in 1835 at Chief Justice John Marshall's funeral, which the Park Service's own handbook treats as unverified.[^s4] Nash considers 1843 almost certain,[^s3] Stephen Fried notes the first known mention of the crack in 1844,[^s5] and the Park Service gives the early 1840s.[^s1]

When did it become a national icon? Accounts that stress the 1830s emphasize the abolitionist origin of its name and meaning.[^s2][^s3] Fried argues instead that the bell's rise to national glory came only with the 1915 tour and the World War I bond campaigns.[^s5]

## Connections

The bell stands in Philadelphia, in Independence National Historical Park, near Independence Hall, where it hung for nearly two centuries.[^s3] Its name and its meaning were shaped by abolitionism, and the name was introduced in the *Anti-Slavery Record*.[^s2] It came to symbolize the women's suffrage movement through the Justice Bell campaign, and the Nineteenth Amendment's ratification was celebrated by ringing that replica.[^s1] The July 4 legend links it, inaccurately, to the Declaration of Independence.[^s1][^s3]
