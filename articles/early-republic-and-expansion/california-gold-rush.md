# California Gold Rush

The California Gold Rush was the stampede of fortune seekers that followed the discovery of gold on the American River in January 1848. Within a few years it turned a thinly settled former Mexican province into a populous state, carried California into the Union as a free state, and drew migrants from the eastern United States, Latin America, Europe and China.[^s1][^s3] It also brought catastrophe to the Native peoples whose homelands held the gold.[^s8][^s12]

## Background

The Sierra Nevada foothills around the discovery site were the homelands of the Nisenan and foothill Miwok, who had lived in villages along its rivers for thousands of years and called the Coloma valley *Cullumah*; diseases introduced by fur trappers had already devastated them by the late 1830s.[^s2] John Sutter, a Swiss immigrant who arrived in 1839, built an agricultural colony he called New Helvetia (later Sacramento) in the Sacramento Valley, where some Native people worked for him.[^s1][^s2] California was still Mexican territory when the United States seized it during the Mexican–American War, and the peace treaty that confirmed the transfer promised that Mexican citizens' property would be "inviolably respected."[^s4]

## What happened

On January 24, 1848, James Marshall, a carpenter building a sawmill for Sutter on the South Fork of the American River, found a gold nugget at the mill site; he later recalled that the find "made my heart thump."[^s1][^s2] Marshall and Sutter tried to keep the discovery quiet, but word spread, and by July about 4,000 people were digging in the area.[^s2][^s5] On December 5, 1848, President James K. Polk told Congress that the reports "would scarcely command belief" had officers not confirmed them, and asked for a branch mint in California.[^s5]

The great overland migration began in the spring of 1849, when about 25,000 emigrants crossed the plains; an estimated 140,000 came by the California Trail between 1849 and 1854.[^s1] Cholera and other diseases took their toll on the trail, and many livestock were lost.[^s1] California's non-Native population, about 14,000 in 1848, passed 200,000 by 1852, and roughly 300,000 people arrived over the course of the rush.[^s2][^s9][^s11] San Francisco grew from about 500 residents in 1848 to almost 50,000 by 1853.[^s13] The peak year was 1852, when about 67,000 people arrived, 20,000 of them from China.[^s3] Alongside Californios, the newcomers included Mexican and other Latin American miners, Europeans and more than 2,000 African Americans by 1852, some brought as enslaved people; Peter and Nancy Gooch came to Coloma enslaved in 1849, and Nancy later bought her son's freedom.[^s2][^s4]

Very few miners became rich; many went bankrupt or returned home, while merchants charged steep prices for tools, food and news. Samuel Brannan, who sold pans and land, became a millionaire, and Levi Strauss prospered selling work clothes.[^s9][^s11] Marshall himself was driven back to prospecting by the late 1860s and was once stranded penniless in Kansas City.[^s2]

The rush hardened racial lines. In 1850 the legislature charged foreign miners $20 a month, a fee aimed mainly at Latin American miners that white miners used to drive them from rich claims; it was repealed the next year and replaced by a $4 monthly tax.[^s3][^s4] An 1852 tax fell chiefly on Chinese miners, who paid more than $5 million, about a quarter of state revenue, before the tax was voided in 1870; state law also barred Chinese and other people of color from testifying in court.[^s7] Californios lost much of their land to squatters, speculators and lawyers; the Peralta family kept only 700 of its 49,000 East Bay acres.[^s4]

For Native Californians the Gold Rush was a disaster. Miners seized land and water, placer mining destroyed salmon runs, and epidemics of syphilis, cholera, measles and smallpox spread; disease may account for as much as 60 percent of the population loss.[^s1][^s8] In 1848 a government report estimated that half the gold diggers were Native, but they were increasingly forced into labor for whites.[^s8] The 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians let local officials arrest "vagrant" Native people and hire them out, amounting to indentured servitude, and children were taken and "apprenticed" to white masters.[^s4][^s8][^s10] California's Native population fell from about 150,000 in the 1840s to under 30,000 by 1870.[^s4][^s8][^s10]

## Aftermath and legacy

The rush made statehood urgent. Voters ratified a state constitution on November 13, 1849, and on September 9, 1850, California entered the Union as the 31st state, free of slavery, as part of the bargains of the Compromise of 1850; because part of the state lay south of the Missouri Compromise line, its admission had reopened the national quarrel over slavery's expansion.[^s6][^s9] By 1860 the first federal census in California counted about 308,000 residents.[^s3] Over the following fifty years miners took roughly 125 million ounces of gold from the region.[^s2]

Californians pressed Congress for a transcontinental railroad, on which more than 10,000 Chinese workers would labor at one time; the hostility Chinese miners met in the camps fed the movement that led to the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882.[^s3][^s9][^s13] The American Yawp presents the era's lawlessness and abuse of Native peoples and older Mexican communities as the darker side of Manifest Destiny.[^s13] On June 18, 2019, Governor Gavin Newsom apologized on behalf of the State of California for its role in violence, exploitation and dispossession of California Native peoples; the state's Office of Tribal Affairs calls that history genocide and created a Native-led Truth and Healing Council to examine it.[^s12]

## Connections

- **Place:** It took place in California's Sierra Nevada foothills, in the American West.[^s1][^s13]
- **Background:** It followed the Mexican–American War and is often read as part of Manifest Destiny.[^s4][^s13]
- **Native Californians:** Disease, dispossession, the 1850 indenture law and violence cut the Native population by more than four-fifths within a generation.[^s8][^s10][^s12]
- **Consequences:** It led to California's rapid admission under the Compromise of 1850, to the foreign miners' taxes of 1850 and 1852, and to the anti-Chinese politics behind the Chinese Exclusion Act.[^s3][^s4][^s7][^s9]
