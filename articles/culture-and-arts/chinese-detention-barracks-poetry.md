# Chinese detention-barracks poetry

Between 1910 and 1940, Chinese men held at the U.S. Immigration Station on Angel Island in San Francisco Bay wrote poems on the wooden walls of their detention barracks, first in ink and then by carving the characters into the wood.[^s1][^s5] Hundreds survive. Because the immigrants who passed through the station left few firsthand accounts, the National Park Service treats the poems as unusually valuable evidence of what detention under the exclusion laws felt like.[^s1] Judy Yung, one of their translators, calls them the first literary body of work by Chinese in North America.[^s5]

## Background

The station opened on January 21, 1910, chiefly to enforce the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and the anti-Asian immigration laws that followed it.[^s1][^s4] Chinese applicants, including those claiming to be the children of U.S. citizens (often called "paper sons" and "paper daughters"), faced long interrogations, and inconsistent answers could lead to deportation. Detainees waited weeks, months and sometimes years for a decision.[^s4] The poets were mostly Cantonese-speaking villagers from rural southern China, many in their late teens and with limited schooling.[^s5][^s4] According to the Park Service, poems began to appear about ten months after men started living in the barracks.[^s1]

## About the work

Most of the poems follow classical Chinese forms, often four lines of seven characters with rhymed couplets, and draw on ancient stories, historical figures and literary allusions.[^s4][^s6] Their themes include the voyage to America, life on the island, homesickness, impressions of Westerners, anger at their treatment and hopes that China would grow strong.[^s4] Few poets signed or dated their work, likely to avoid punishment; the earliest dated poem yet recorded gives a date equal to October 5, 1911.[^s4][^s3]

The poems survived partly because of efforts to erase them. Officers repeatedly ordered the walls repainted, so detainees carved over their brushed characters. Maintenance crews filled the carvings with putty and paint, which sealed the wood; as the putty shrank and the paint cracked, the poems reappeared.[^s5] Detainees also preserved them on paper: Smiley Jann (Jann Mon Fong) copied 99 poems in 1931, and Tet Yee (Yee Tet Ming) copied 96 in 1932.[^s3]

No poem by a woman is known. Women were held in the administration building, which burned on August 12, 1940; the station moved to the mainland that November.[^s1][^s4] Chinese men were not the only writers: Charles Egan's anthology gathers inscriptions in Japanese, Korean, Russian and other languages alongside the Chinese poems.[^s2]

## Reception and legacy

In May 1970 state park ranger Alexander Weiss found hundreds of poems in the abandoned barracks, which were due to be demolished.[^s3][^s4] Asian American activists, descendants of detainees and volunteers campaigned to save the building and its walls. The restored barracks opened as a museum in 1983, and the station became a National Historic Landmark in 1997.[^s4] A Save America's Treasures grant in 2000 funded further restoration, including the poems.[^s1]

Translation followed preservation. In 1976 the Jann and Yee manuscripts reached researchers, who used them to recover damaged or missing poems.[^s3] Him Mark Lai, Genny Lim and Judy Yung published *Island: Poetry and History of Chinese Immigrants on Angel Island, 1910–1940* in 1980 (some accounts give 1982), translating 135 poems with oral histories; its 2014 second edition annotates 150.[^s5][^s4] With Charles Egan's *Voices of Angel Island* (2020–21), researchers have translated and published 154 Chinese poems or inscriptions, while many others remain incomplete, illegible or untranslated.[^s3][^s2] The Smithsonian counts more than 200 poems discovered and documented.[^s4]

Scholars read the poems in more than one way. Lai described them as largely in the classical style of the Tang dynasty, while R. John Williams has argued that their irregular rhythms and loose constructions sit closer to modern free verse, and that teaching them in English means reckoning with how Ezra Pound's translations shaped American ideas of Chinese poetry.[^s6] Yung stresses what they record, saying "the walls at Angel Island do talk," and the Smithsonian notes that the poems still resonate in debates over immigrant detention.[^s5][^s4]

## Connections

The poems originated in the men's barracks of the Angel Island Immigration Station (`originatedIn`), which is why the station is required background.[^s1] Their anger and despair were shaped by detention under the Chinese Exclusion Act and later anti-Asian laws (`influencedBy`).[^s4] Their forms and allusions draw on classical Tang-dynasty poetry (`influencedBy`), though scholars disagree about how closely.[^s6] They belong to Asian American literature and American poetry, and are related to the history of Chinese Americans, the "paper sons" system, Japanese Americans and immigration to the United States more broadly.[^s4][^s2]
