# The Federalist Papers

The Federalist Papers are 85 essays that Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay wrote under the shared pen name "Publius" between October 1787 and May 1788, addressed to the people of the State of New York and meant to win their support for the Constitution drafted at Philadelphia.[^s1] Collected in two volumes as *The Federalist*, they became the best-known contemporary explanation of how the new government was supposed to work, although how much they mattered in 1788, and how much weight they deserve in reading the Constitution today, are still debated.[^s2][^s6]

## Background

The Constitutional Convention sent its plan to the states on September 17, 1787, and New York's newspapers quickly filled with essays attacking it.[^s1] Hamilton conceived a reply series and recruited Madison and Jay.[^s1] Opponents writing as "Cato" and "Brutus" pressed objections that Publius tried to answer, and Antifederalist writers in turn accused Publius of misrepresenting their views and of being too attached to federal power.[^s2]

The first essay appeared in the *Independent Journal* on October 27, 1787, and the series also ran in the *New-York Packet*, the *Daily Advertiser* and the *New-York Journal*.[^s1] By April 2, 1788, 76 numbers had appeared in the newspapers; the printer John McLean issued the first bound volume, with 36 essays and an unsigned preface by Hamilton, on March 22, 1788, and the second, completing the set of 85, on May 28.[^s1][^s2] Each volume ran to 500 copies, and Hamilton paid more than half the printing cost.[^s2]

The authors' identities were an open secret at best: letters of the time guessed most often at Hamilton, and none of the three publicly named all the authors until after New York had ratified.[^s2] Founders Online credits Hamilton with 51 essays, Madison with 14 and Jay with 5, and treats 15 (Nos. 18–20, 49–58 and 62–63) as disputed, leaning toward Madison without settling the question.[^s1] Jay's small share owed to rheumatism in the winter of 1787, not, as older biographies claimed, to injuries in the 1788 "Doctors' Riot."[^s1]

## What it says

Taken together, the essays set out the weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation, explain the government the Constitution would create and answer the objections of its critics.[^s2] Madison later recalled that they were written in haste, without any fixed division of subjects among the authors.[^s2]

The most famous essay today, No. 10, appeared in the *New York Packet* on November 23, 1787.[^s4] Madison defined a faction as a group, whether a majority or a minority, united by a passion or interest adverse to other citizens' rights or the common good. Because its causes are sown in human nature and in unequal property, he wrote, they cannot be removed without destroying liberty, so a republic must control their effects. A large republic would do this best: "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests," making an oppressive majority harder to form.[^s4]

The essays also defended the Constitution's compromises with slavery. No. 54, printed on February 12, 1788, and attributed to Hamilton or Madison, explained why enslaved people would be counted as three fifths of a person for apportioning House seats and direct taxes.[^s5] Speaking through an imagined Southern voice, it argued that the law treated enslaved people as both property and persons, and it accepted a rule that left each enslaved person "divested of two fifths of the MAN."[^s5]

## Legacy

Contemporary opinion was mixed. Washington thought no work better suited to convince an unbiased mind, and Jefferson called it "the best commentary on the principles of government," but Rufus King found the essays too elaborate and others judged them too long and learned for ordinary readers.[^s2] Copies did travel beyond New York, to Virginia, Massachusetts and other states, often in the hands of ratifying-convention delegates.[^s2]

Over time the collection became a standard textbook of political science and constitutional law, reissued in dozens of editions and several languages.[^s2] Later editions settled the authors' names: an 1810 edition was the first to attribute essays to individual authors, and the 1818 Gideon edition carried Madison's own list.[^s1] Courts, lawyers and scholars have long treated the essays as a guide to the Constitution's meaning.[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

**Did the essays matter in 1788?** The documentary record shows that the convention that met at Poughkeepsie on June 17, 1788, was dominated by Antifederalists, and that New York ratified by 30 to 27 on July 26 only after New Hampshire and Virginia had ratified, which made adoption nearly certain.[^s3] The editors of the ratification documents emphasise that the essays were widely sought and circulated to delegates in several states, while also recording contemporaries who doubted they reached ordinary readers.[^s2]

**How much authority should they carry?** Dan Coenen notes that critics question using *The Federalist* to interpret the Constitution, both because of doubts about relying on background texts at all and because the essays were partisan advocacy written to win a political fight; he examines why courts nonetheless give them weight.[^s6]

**What was Federalist No. 10 about?** No. 10 was not regarded as one of the important numbers until Charles Beard made it the central exhibit of his 1913 argument that the framers acted to protect property. Greg Weiner accepts that Madison was concerned with property but rejects Beard's equation of property rights with greed, and argues that the essay's assumptions about slow-moving, limited government no longer fully hold.[^s7]

## Connections

- **Authors:** Alexander Hamilton planned the series and wrote most of it, with James Madison and John Jay as co-authors.[^s1]
- **New York City and New York:** The essays were printed in the city's newspapers and addressed to the state's voters, whose convention ratified on July 26, 1788.[^s1][^s3]
- **Ratification of the Constitution and the Antifederalists:** The series answered Antifederalist writers and circulated among delegates in several states.[^s2]
- **United States Constitution and Constitutional Convention:** The essays explain and defend the plan the convention proposed on September 17, 1787.[^s1]
- **Three-fifths compromise and slavery:** No. 54 defended counting enslaved people as three fifths of a person.[^s5]
- **Articles of Confederation:** The essays set out the weaknesses of the government the Constitution would replace.[^s2]
