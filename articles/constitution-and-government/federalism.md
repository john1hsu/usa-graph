# Federalism

Federalism is the American answer to a basic question: who governs? The Constitution never uses the word, but it creates what one political science textbook calls "an institutional arrangement that creates two relatively autonomous levels of government," a national government and the states, each able to act directly on the people under the Constitution's authority.[^s2] It differs from a unitary system, where subnational governments depend on the center, and from a confederation, where the center can act only with the states' consent.[^s2] Where exactly the line between nation and states falls has been argued over, and sometimes fought over, ever since.[^s3]

## Origins

Under the Articles of Confederation the states held most power and the national government was weak.[^s2] Article II of the Articles reserved to each state every power not expressly delegated to the United States.[^s1] The Constitution drafted in 1787 replaced that confederation with a stronger national government of enumerated powers.[^s1][^s2]

Defending the plan in Federalist No. 51, published in the New York Packet on February 8, 1788, Publius described America as a compound republic in which the people's power is divided first between two distinct governments and then among separate departments within each. "Hence a double security arises to the rights of the people," the essay concluded, because the governments would check each other while each was also checked from within.[^s4]

When the First Congress drafted the amendment that became the Tenth, it reserved to the states, or to the people, the powers neither delegated to the United States nor prohibited to the states. Both houses refused to copy the Articles' word "expressly"; the House vote was 17 to 32.[^s1] James Madison told Congress that if a power had been granted, Congress could use it even where that touched state laws.[^s1]

## How it works

The Constitution sorts power in several ways. Article I, Section 8 lists Congress's enumerated powers, such as taxing, borrowing, regulating interstate and foreign commerce, coining money and declaring war, and ends with the Necessary and Proper Clause.[^s2] The Tenth Amendment reserves other powers to the states or the people; both levels share concurrent powers such as taxing, borrowing and setting up courts; and Article I, Sections 9 and 10 forbid certain acts to each.[^s2] The Supremacy Clause of Article VI makes valid federal law prevail over conflicting state law, and Article IV requires states to honor one another's acts and court judgments.[^s2]

The balance has shifted over time. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) the Supreme Court upheld implied powers and national supremacy, and in Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) it read the commerce power broadly.[^s3] Kentucky and Virginia had already advanced the idea that states could nullify federal law in 1798, and South Carolina tried it against the tariffs in 1832 until the Force Bill and a compromise tariff ended the crisis in 1833.[^s3] After eleven southern states seceded to protect slavery, the Union victory ended any claimed right of secession, and returning Confederate states had to ratify the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.[^s3]

Dual federalism, a "layer cake" in which each level kept to its own sphere, emerged in the late 1870s; in United States v. E. C. Knight (1895) the Court held that the national government could not regulate manufacturing.[^s3] The Great Depression produced cooperative federalism, a "marble cake" of blended authority, after the Court upheld the National Labor Relations Act in the wake of Franklin Roosevelt's 1937 court-packing proposal.[^s3] The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 gave Washington tools to enforce equal rights against state segregation and disfranchisement, and Great Society grants such as Medicaid are jointly funded and run by the states.[^s3] Presidents Nixon and Reagan promoted a "new federalism" returning programs to the states, and in United States v. Lopez (1995) a 5–4 Court held that a federal ban on guns near schools exceeded the commerce power and would have given Congress a general police power belonging to the states.[^s3][^s7]

Federalism also has a third set of governments. The Constitution gives Congress plenary power over Indian affairs, so states have no authority over tribal governments unless Congress expressly grants it; federally recognized tribes are generally not subordinate to states and often work with them through compacts.[^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

**States' rights and slavery.** Before the Civil War, appeals to state sovereignty were tied to slavery. South Carolina's 1860 secession declaration pointed to "an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery" and complained that thirteen northern states had passed laws that nullified or obstructed the federal fugitive slave acts.[^s5] Slaveholding states thus demanded vigorous federal enforcement for slavery while invoking state sovereignty to leave the Union.[^s5] In the twentieth century, Robert Schapiro writes, the Tenth Amendment became associated with "states' rights" arguments against civil rights.[^s6]

**How much should courts enforce the line?** The Court in United States v. Darby (1941) called the Tenth Amendment a truism, and from the late 1930s to the mid-1970s it largely vanished from constitutional law; since 1992, decisions such as New York v. United States and Printz v. United States have barred Congress from commandeering state governments.[^s6] Schapiro argues that this revival lacks a basis in text or sound policy and that dual federalism's separate spheres no longer match modern reality. Gary Lawson answers that the amendment usefully blocks federal powers inferred from the general nature of government and can serve as a backstop when enumerated powers are read too broadly.[^s6]


## Connections

- **Constitution and Articles of Confederation:** the Constitution replaced the Articles' confederation but kept its principle of reserved state powers, minus "expressly."[^s1][^s2]
- **Federalist Papers and separation of powers:** Federalist No. 51 joined the division between nation and states to the division among branches as a double security for rights.[^s4]
- **McCulloch v. Maryland and United States v. Lopez:** rulings that moved the line toward the nation and back toward the states.[^s3][^s7]
- **Nullification crisis, Civil War and Reconstruction:** crises that ended claims of a right to nullify or secede.[^s3][^s5]
- **New Deal and Voting Rights Act of 1965:** the expansions of the national role that defined cooperative federalism.[^s3]
- **Tribal sovereignty:** Native nations stand outside the state-federal division as governments in their own right.[^s8]
