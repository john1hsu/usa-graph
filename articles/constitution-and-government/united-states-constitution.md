# United States Constitution

The Constitution of the United States is the country's supreme law: a four-page document, signed in Philadelphia on September 17, 1787, that established the government of the United States and has its permanent home at the National Archives.[^s2] Its seven articles create Congress, the presidency and the federal courts and set the rules for how the nation and the states share power.[^s1] Amended twenty-seven times between 1791 and 1992, it remains the framework of American government.[^s5]

## Background

The Federal Convention met in the Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, to revise the Articles of Confederation, the weak first national charter; it lacked a quorum until May 25, 1787, and by mid-June the delegates had decided to write an entirely new frame of government instead.[^s2] Working in secret, they argued over how strong the central government should be, how many representatives each state should have, and who should elect them.[^s2]

The finished text needed the approval of conventions in nine of the thirteen states.[^s1][^s3] Federalists, strongest in New England, wanted a national government able to defend the country, regulate trade and issue a common currency; Anti-Federalists, strongest in the South, feared it would favor the wealthy, overtax farmers and swallow state sovereignty and individual liberty.[^s3] Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay defended the plan in eighty-five newspaper essays signed "Publius," now known as the Federalist Papers.[^s3] Delaware ratified first on December 7, 1787, and New Hampshire's vote on June 21, 1788, made nine; Virginia and New York followed that summer, North Carolina in November 1789, and Rhode Island, which had sent no delegates to Philadelphia, last on May 29, 1790.[^s3][^s7] The framers set March 4, 1789, for the new Congress to meet, though the Senate did not reach a quorum until April 6.[^s6]

## What it says

The Preamble opens "We the People of the United States" and lists the government's purposes, among them a more perfect union, justice, domestic peace, the common defense, the general welfare and liberty.[^s1] Article I gives the legislative powers it grants to a two-chamber Congress and lists them, ending with authority to make all laws necessary and proper for carrying them out; Article II vests the executive power in a president; Article III vests the judicial power in a single Supreme Court and lower courts Congress creates.[^s1][^s10]

These articles separate the government's powers and let each branch check the others. The president can veto bills and Congress can override with two-thirds of both houses; the Senate confirms justices and ambassadors and ratifies treaties; the House can impeach and the Senate tries impeachments; and since *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) the Supreme Court has exercised judicial review.[^s10] Power is also divided between the nation and the states, which keep reserved powers, while Article VI makes the Constitution, federal laws and treaties the supreme law of the land, overriding conflicting state law.[^s10][^s1] Article VI also bars any religious test for federal office, and Article VII set the nine-state threshold for ratification.[^s1]

The original text protected slavery without ever using the word. It counted three-fifths of enslaved people, whom it called simply other persons, in apportioning House seats and direct taxes, forbade Congress to ban their importation before 1808, and required the return of any person held to service or labor who escaped to another state.[^s1] It mentioned Native peoples twice: apportionment left out Indians who were not taxed, and Congress received power to regulate commerce with Indian tribes.[^s1]

Article V sets the amendment process: two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or a convention called at the request of two-thirds of the state legislatures, may propose amendments, which take effect when three-fourths of the states ratify.[^s1][^s5] Thousands of amendments have been proposed and Congress has sent thirty-three to the states, but only twenty-seven have been ratified, and the convention method has never been used.[^s5]

## Legacy

Because the Constitution lacked a list of individual rights, several state conventions ratified only after being promised amendments.[^s3][^s4] The First Congress proposed twelve on September 25, 1789; ten were ratified on December 15, 1791, as the Bill of Rights, and one of the others, on congressional pay, was finally ratified on May 7, 1992, as the Twenty-seventh Amendment.[^s4]

The Constitution has also been remade by later amendments. The Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments, adopted after the Civil War, made the three-fifths clause obsolete and wrote a guarantee of equality into the text, and the National Archives marks the clauses that later amendments changed.[^s7][^s1] Its ratification, as the National Constitution Center stresses, was hard-fought rather than quickly embraced.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians have long argued about why the Constitution was made. In *An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution of the United States* (1913), Charles A. Beard argued that the framers and the supporters of ratification acted mainly to advance their own economic interests, a thesis that soon became orthodoxy.[^s9][^s8] Henry Steele Commager answered in 1958 that Beard's evidence explained how the document was made but not what it says: its central concern is political, dividing power among governments, and it has no property qualifications for voting or office.[^s8] From the late 1960s, Bernard Bailyn, Gordon Wood and others argued that political ideas, especially republicanism, explain the framing better than economic interest; Wood declared Beard's thesis dead, and law professor Stephen Feldman notes that the ideological approach still dominates, though he argues economic interests can still help illuminate the framers' ideas.[^s9]

## Connections

The Constitution was produced by the **Constitutional Convention** of 1787, which understanding it **requires**, and it is one of the **founding documents of the United States**.[^s2] The campaign to ratify it **led to** the **Federalist Papers**, and Anti-Federalist demands for protection of liberties **led to** the **Bill of Rights**.[^s3][^s4] Its compromises **affected** **slavery in the United States** by strengthening slaveholders' representation and protecting the slave trade until 1808 and the recapture of freedom seekers, until the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments **amended** the text.[^s1][^s7] It is often **confused with** the **Declaration of Independence**, the source of the phrase "all men are created equal."[^s7]
