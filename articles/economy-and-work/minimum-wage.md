# Minimum wage

A minimum wage is the lowest hourly pay an employer may legally offer. In the United States it exists at several levels at once: a federal floor set by the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), and state and sometimes local rates layered on top. Employers must pay whichever applicable rate is highest.[^s7] The federal minimum has been $7.25 an hour since July 24, 2009.[^s2]

## Origins

Minimum wage laws began in the states, not in Washington. Massachusetts passed the first in 1912, and it took effect on July 1, 1913.[^s3] The early laws covered women and, often, minors. Organizing women into unions was seen as difficult, so legislated standards were considered justified for them, while the American Federation of Labor under Samuel Gompers opposed legal wage floors for men, preferring that men win gains through collective bargaining; opponents also warned that "the minimum tends to become the maximum."[^s4] By 1923 sixteen states had minimum wage laws for women, and in 1918 Congress passed one for women and minors in the District of Columbia.[^s4]

The courts soon checked these laws. In *Adkins v. Children's Hospital* (1923) a narrowly divided Supreme Court voided the District of Columbia law, and later state laws became largely advisory.[^s4][^s1] During the Depression, the National Recovery Administration's industry codes set wages and hours, but the Court struck down the NRA in *Schechter* (1935).[^s1] Even so, by the end of 1938 twenty-five states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico had minimum wage legislation.[^s3]

The turning point came on March 29, 1937. In *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish*, a 5–4 Court upheld Washington State's minimum wage of $14.50 for a 48-hour week, which Elsie Parrish, a chambermaid at a Wenatchee hotel, had sued to collect, and it expressly overruled *Adkins*.[^s5] Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes wrote that "The Constitution does not speak of freedom of contract."[^s5] Justice Owen Roberts's switch to the side upholding the law improved the odds for a federal measure.[^s1]

Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins had kept draft wage-and-hour bills ready, and on May 24, 1937, President Franklin D. Roosevelt sent one to Congress, asking for "a fair day's pay for a fair day's work."[^s1] Southern Democrats joined Republicans to bottle it up in the House Rules Committee, and labor itself was divided: the AFL's William Green wanted a uniform standard rather than a labor board, while the CIO, after John L. Lewis lost interest, backed the House version through Sidney Hillman.[^s4] After a long fight, Roosevelt signed the FLSA on June 25, 1938.[^s1]

## How it works

The 1938 act set a minimum of 25 cents an hour, effective October 24, 1938, a 44-hour maximum workweek before overtime, and a ban on oppressive child labor.[^s1][^s2] It reached only industries employing about a fifth of the labor force.[^s1] Farmworkers, most transportation workers, intrastate retail, government employees and many small businesses were exempt.[^s4] Wage orders nonetheless raised about 700,000 workers above the 25-cent floor by 1941.[^s4] In *United States v. Darby* (February 3, 1941) the Supreme Court upheld Congress's power to set wage and hour standards for workers producing goods for interstate commerce, overruling *Hammer v. Dagenhart*.[^s6]

Congress raises the federal rate only by amending the law; there is no automatic adjustment. The rate reached 40 cents in 1945, $1.00 in 1956, $3.35 in 1981 and $7.25 in 2009.[^s2] Amendments also widened coverage: in 1961 to large retail and service enterprises, and in 1966 to hospitals, schools, hotels, restaurants and farms, with farmworkers first covered at $1.00 an hour on February 1, 1967.[^s2] Domestic workers were brought in during 1974.[^s11] Special rules remain. Employers may pay workers under 20 a $4.25 rate during their first 90 days, and may pay tipped employees, those receiving more than $30 a month in tips, a cash wage of $2.13 as long as tips bring them to $7.25.[^s2][^s8]

States now shape most pay floors. As of July 1, 2026, thirty states, the District of Columbia, Guam, Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands had minimums above $7.25; thirteen states matched it; and seven states had no minimum wage law or one below the federal rate.[^s7] The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted about 843,000 hourly workers at or below $7.25 in 2024, or 1.0 percent of hourly paid workers, compared with 13.4 percent in 1979. Workers under 25 made up 43 percent of that group, and about two-thirds worked in leisure and hospitality, mostly restaurants (BLS, 2024).[^s9]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians weigh why the 1938 law left out farm and household workers. Phyllis Palmer describes the exclusions as products of central racial and gender inequalities in the act. The scholarship she engages variously stresses southern lawmakers' wish to control a nonwhite farm labor force, opposition from growers and agricultural business, and ideas that placed farm and household work outside the market.[^s11]

The economic effects of raising the minimum remain disputed. A 2019 Congressional Budget Office study of a $15 federal minimum by 2025 illustrates the trade-off at the center of the debate. It estimated raises for about 17 million workers and about 1.3 million people lifted out of poverty. Its median estimate was also about 1.3 million fewer jobs, within a likely range of roughly zero to 3.7 million, so the same policy would raise pay for many workers while possibly costing others their jobs.[^s10]

## Connections

The federal minimum wage was established by the Fair Labor Standards Act, signed by Franklin D. Roosevelt as part of the New Deal response to the Great Depression.[^s1] It was made possible by *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish*, which overruled the freedom-of-contract reasoning of *Adkins v. Children's Hospital*; both cases turned on the due process clause.[^s5][^s1] The labor movement shaped it, from the AFL's early resistance to wage laws for men to the CIO's support in 1938.[^s4] *United States v. Darby* later confirmed Congress's power to enact it, and the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division enforces it.[^s6][^s2]
