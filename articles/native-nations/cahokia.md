# Cahokia

Cahokia was the largest city north of Mexico before European colonization. Built by Native people of the Mississippian culture on the Mississippi River floodplain in southwestern Illinois, across the river from St. Louis, it was first settled around A.D. 700, peaked between about 1050 and 1200, and was abandoned by about 1400.[^s1] The National Park Service calls it the largest archaeological site of the Mississippian culture; about 2,200 acres near Collinsville are protected as Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site, which in 2011 was the only World Heritage Site in the Park Service's Midwest Region.[^s1] The name is not the builders' own: it comes from the Cahokia, an Illinois (Illini) people who lived nearby in the 1700s.[^s2]

## History

**Rise.** Mississippian peoples farmed corn, squash and tobacco, hunted and fished, and built earthen mounds at their towns, some as cemeteries and others as platforms for temples.[^s4] Around 1050 a Cahokia village of perhaps 1,000–2,000 people was rapidly redesigned into a planned city; archaeologist Timothy Pauketat calls this ancient America's "Big Bang," and its cause is unclear.[^s3] Immigrants came from nearby villages and from as far as southern Missouri and northeast Arkansas, and Pauketat estimates the city proper reached about 10,000 people.[^s3] Feasting deposits, including the remains of about 2,000 deer, point to great public gatherings.[^s3]

**The city.** Earthen pyramids ringed a vast central plaza.[^s3] At its peak Cahokia covered more than six square miles and held about 120 mounds.[^s1] Monks Mound, built between about 900 and 1200, rises about 100 feet, covers more than 14 acres and is the largest prehistoric earthen structure in the Western Hemisphere.[^s2] Cahokians also raised about five "woodhenges," post circles aligned with sunrise and sunset at the equinoxes and solstices that most likely served as calendars; one stood where Mound 72, a burial mound for high-status people, was later built.[^s5] Pauketat reports that sacrificial rituals began shortly after 1050.[^s3] Across the river, an East St. Louis center of some 50 mounds was linked to Cahokia by a chain of mounds and villages, and the site of St. Louis held many more.[^s4][^s11]

**Decline.** Decline began after about 1200, and by 1400 the city was empty.[^s1] In its later years people built wooden palisades around the center, suggesting new threats, and residents dispersed, most likely joining other Native communities.[^s2][^s8] Illinois (Illini) people later used the first terrace of Monks Mound in the mid-1700s, near a French chapel.[^s14]

**Destruction and preservation.** When the French founded St. Louis in 1764 the area probably held hundreds of mounds; by 1875 nearly all were gone, leaving the former "Mound City" only Sugarloaf Mound.[^s11] East St. Louis's mounds were leveled for fill, factories and rail yards.[^s4] Cahokia's core survived because Illinois made part of it a state park in 1925; it became a National Historic Landmark on July 19, 1964, a state historic site in 1979 and a World Heritage Site in 1982, and about 70 mounds remain.[^s1][^s2] Excavations for the Interstate 70 bridge from 2009 to 2012 recorded more than 6,000 ancient features at East St. Louis, yet covered only about a tenth of that city.[^s4]

## Significance

Cahokia shows that a planned, socially complex city flourished in North America centuries before Europeans arrived; the Park Service compares its peak population to London's at the time.[^s1] The Osage, Chickasaw and Peoria trace ancestry to its builders, and nations expelled from Illinois under the Indian Removal Act of 1830 keep a strong interest in it.[^s13] The Osage Nation bought Sugarloaf Mound in August 2009, and in November 2015 the national NAGPRA Review Committee unanimously found the Osage culturally affiliated with the Mississippian people.[^s11][^s12]

Cahokia also matters for how Americans once denied it. In the 1800s many white writers credited the mounds to a vanished "lost race" — Israelites, Europeans or Atlanteans — rather than to Native people.[^s9][^s10] The story cast Indians as usurpers whose removal was deserved, and Andrew Jackson invoked a vanished race while defending Indian removal in 1830.[^s10] Cyrus Thomas's 1894 study for the Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology concluded that American Indians built the mounds, ending the myth among scholars.[^s9][^s10]

## Debates and interpretations

**How big was it?** The Park Service gives 10,000 to 20,000 people at the peak, while Britannica notes estimates ranging from 8,000 to 40,000.[^s1][^s2] Pauketat, who reads Cahokia as a true city born in a sudden "Big Bang," estimates about 10,000 in the city itself, not counting suburbs and satellite towns.[^s3]

**Why was it abandoned?** A long-favoured 1993 theory by Neal Lopinot and William Woods held that cutting upland trees caused erosion and floods that ruined crops.[^s8] In 2015 Samuel Munoz's team used lake sediments to argue that Cahokia rose in a flood-free period and declined after a major Mississippi flood around 1200, without ruling out drought.[^s6] In 2021 Caitlin Rankin's study in *Geoarchaeology* found the Cahokia Creek floodplain surface stable from Mississippian times to the 1800s, with no physical evidence of the expected flooding.[^s7] Others point to factionalism, warfare, drought or disease, and Tristram Kidder warns against single-cause explanations; Rankin and Pauketat urge reading Cahokians' land use through Indigenous practices rather than a Western "ecocide" model.[^s8]

## Connections

Cahokia lies in southwestern Illinois (`locatedIn`) beside the Mississippi River, whose floods may have shaped its rise and fall.[^s1][^s6] It was the largest center of the Mississippian culture (`partOf`) and is a key site for archaeology (`studiedIn`), as the Illinois State Archaeological Survey's regional work shows.[^s1][^s4] It is tied to the East St. Louis mound center, to Sugarloaf Mound and the Osage Nation, to the Mound Builder myth, and to the Indian Removal Act that drove its descendants from Illinois.[^s4][^s11][^s10][^s13]
