# Lunar Laser Ranging Experiment

The Lunar Laser Ranging Experiment is the longest-lived scientific legacy of the first Moon landing. In July 1969 the Apollo 11 crew set a flat panel of 100 glass prisms on the Sea of Tranquility, and observatories on Earth began timing laser pulses bounced off it.[^s3][^s4] More than half a century later it is the only Apollo experiment still returning data from the Moon, and it has turned the Earth–Moon distance into one of the most precisely measured lengths in science.[^s8][^s3]

## Background

The idea grew out of discussions around 1960 in a Princeton research group studying gravitation, whose members wanted a way to track the Moon's orbit precisely enough to test theories of gravity.[^s2] The key device is a corner cube, a prism that sends light straight back toward its source wherever it comes from.[^s7] The Apollo 11 array held 100 fused-silica corner cubes, each 3.8 cm across, in an aluminum panel 46 cm square; it needs no power.[^s3] The experiment's 1969 science team, the LURE group, included C. O. Alley of the University of Maryland, P. L. Bender, R. H. Dicke of Princeton, J. E. Faller of Wesleyan University and D. T. Wilkinson, and NASA's agreement with Lick Observatory in California came only in February 1969.[^s2][^s8]

## What happened

During their moonwalk, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin deployed the array about 20 feet from the lunar module, with Aldrin setting it out alongside the seismic experiment.[^s2][^s4] The exact landing spot was uncertain, so four American teams searched for the reflector — two at Lick, one at McDonald Observatory in Texas and one on Haleakalā in Hawaii — while Soviet scientists fired lasers at the Moon too. The U.S. sponsors pressed the teams not to let the Soviets reach the mirrors first.[^s2][^s8]

Lick's effort combined scientists from Wesleyan University, NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center and the observatory with a crew from the laser maker KORAD.[^s1][^s2] When parts failed days before the first detection, KORAD's field operations manager, Hildreth "Hal" Walker Jr., drove about 350 miles to Santa Monica for replacements.[^s8] Walker, an African American engineer from Louisiana who had been shut out of film-industry work by racial barriers, led the team that adapted the ruby laser.[^s6][^s2]

On the night of August 1, 1969, the 120-inch telescope at Lick fired giant-pulse ruby-laser shots every 30 seconds and detected the returns about 2.5 seconds later.[^s1] The beam had spread to a spot about 3.2 km wide at the Moon, so only one or two photons came back per shot. That first night fixed the distance to within about 7.5 metres; on August 3, a faster-firing laser recorded 98 measurements and narrowed it to about 5 metres.[^s1] McDonald Observatory followed with its own measurements from August 20.[^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

Apollo 14 and Apollo 15 left two more U.S. arrays in 1971, the Apollo 15 panel holding 300 prisms.[^s4][^s2] The Soviet Union landed reflectors on its Lunokhod rovers in 1970 and 1973, the second one built in France.[^s2][^s3] Building on the experiment's success, NASA approved a new generation of lunar reflectors to allow sharper tests of relativity, and APOLLO now ranges to six working reflectors, including NASA's next-generation reflector NGLR-1.[^s4][^s5] By 1999, ranging from McDonald Observatory and from Grasse in France was accurate to about 2 cm, better than one part in 10 billion of the average 385,000-km distance.[^s3] Since 2005 the APOLLO project at Apache Point Observatory in New Mexico, led by Tom Murphy of UC San Diego, has used a 3.5-metre telescope to reach about a millimetre.[^s5]

The results reach well beyond the Moon. Ranging shows the Moon receding about 3.8 cm a year because of tidal effects on its orbit, and reveals that the Moon has a fluid core and flexes by up to 15 cm a month under Earth's tides.[^s4] It has tracked tiny changes in the length of Earth's day and in Earth's shape as land rebounds from Ice Age glaciers, and the drift of the observatories themselves on moving crustal plates.[^s3][^s7] It also tests Einstein's theory of gravity: the data show the Sun pulling on Earth and Moon alike despite their different compositions.[^s4][^s5] The laser equipment from Lick is now displayed at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, and an IEEE milestone plaque was dedicated at Lick on the 50th anniversary of the first return.[^s6][^s2]

## Connections

The experiment **resulted from** **Apollo 11**, whose crew placed the first array, and it is **part of** the wider **Apollo program**, since later Apollo crews added reflectors.[^s4] It **occurred in** **California**, where Lick Observatory recorded the first returns, and in **Texas**, where McDonald Observatory carried on ranging for decades.[^s1][^s3] It **occurred during** the **Cold War**, when even the first laser return was a contest with Soviet teams, and Soviet Lunokhod reflectors later joined the network.[^s2] Its success **led to** NASA's **next-generation lunar retroreflectors**, meant to improve on the 1969 design.[^s4][^s5]
