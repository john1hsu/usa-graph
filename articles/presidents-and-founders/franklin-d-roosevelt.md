# Franklin D. Roosevelt

Franklin Delano Roosevelt (1882–1945), the 32nd president, was a Democrat who took office on March 4, 1933, and served until his death on April 12, 1945, the longest presidency in U.S. history.[^s11][^s3] Elected four times, he led the country through the Great Depression and most of World War II, and the Miller Center's William Leuchtenburg judges that he changed American society and politics more than any president except Lincoln.[^s3][^s4]

## Life and work

Roosevelt was born on January 30, 1882, in Hyde Park, New York, and attended Groton and Harvard.[^s4][^s1] He married Eleanor Roosevelt on March 17, 1905, won a seat in the New York State Senate in 1910, and served as assistant secretary of the Navy from 1913 to 1920.[^s4] In 1920 the Democrats nominated him for vice president on a ticket headed by James Cox, which lost.[^s4][^s1]

In August 1921, at Campobello, New Brunswick, he was stricken with polio and never again walked unaided.[^s4] He spent much of the 1920s on rehabilitation, first visiting Warm Springs, Georgia, in 1924 and forming the Georgia Warm Springs Foundation in 1927.[^s4][^s1] He was elected governor of New York in 1928 and re-elected in 1930, and in 1932 he defeated Herbert Hoover for the presidency.[^s4]

His inaugural address assured a frightened nation that it had "nothing to fear but fear itself."[^s1] A bank holiday began on March 6, 1933, and in the "Hundred Days" special session Congress passed emergency banking legislation, the Agricultural Adjustment Act and the law creating the FDIC, while Roosevelt set up the Civilian Conservation Corps.[^s2][^s4] On March 12 he gave the first of his radio fireside chats.[^s4] A "Second New Deal" in 1935 brought the WPA work-relief program, the Wagner Act guaranteeing unions' collective-bargaining rights, and the Social Security Act, signed on August 14, 1935.[^s2][^s4]

After winning a second term in 1936, he asked Congress in February 1937 to add up to six justices to the Supreme Court, which had struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act; the plan failed, though the Court then began upholding New Deal laws.[^s2] A recession beginning in fall 1937, linked to spending cuts, cost about 4 million jobs.[^s2] The 1939 Reorganization Act created the Executive Office of the President.[^s2]

As war spread abroad, he pledged in December 1940 that the country would be an "arsenal of democracy," set out the Four Freedoms in January 1941, and signed Lend-Lease that March.[^s4] On June 25, 1941, under pressure from A. Philip Randolph's threatened march on Washington, he issued Executive Order 8802, barring discrimination in defense industries and government; the National Archives calls it the first presidential directive on race since Reconstruction.[^s8] After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, he asked Congress on December 8, 1941, to declare war.[^s4] On February 19, 1942, he signed Executive Order 9066, under which about 122,000 people of Japanese ancestry, nearly 70,000 of them U.S. citizens, were forcibly removed and incarcerated.[^s7] He met Churchill and Stalin at Tehran and Yalta, signed the G.I. Bill in June 1944, won a fourth term over Thomas E. Dewey, and died at Warm Springs on April 12, 1945, of a cerebral hemorrhage.[^s4][^s1]

## Legacy

The New Deal gave Washington a lasting role in the economy and in citizens' welfare, from union rights and the 1938 minimum wage to old-age and unemployment insurance.[^s3] Its benefits were unequal: Social Security excluded agricultural and domestic workers, many of them African American or Mexican American, and Leuchtenburg notes that the New Deal did nothing to secure Black Americans' voting rights.[^s2][^s3] Fair-employment enforcement under Executive Order 8802 was often weak, especially in the South.[^s8] In 1988 Congress apologized for the wartime incarceration and provided $20,000 to each person who had been incarcerated.[^s7]

Roosevelt broke George Washington's two-term tradition, and concern over his almost thirteen years in office helped produce the Twenty-second Amendment, ratified on February 27, 1951.[^s10]

Writers long cast his disability either as an obstacle he overcame or, after Hugh Gallagher's 1985 *FDR's Splendid Deception*, as a secret he hid.[^s5][^s6] The National Park Service now stresses how he reshaped his surroundings with ramps, customized wheelchairs and adapted cars.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

**The New Deal and recovery.** Leuchtenburg argues that the New Deal relieved suffering but did not end the Depression: unemployment exceeded 19 percent in 1939, and only wartime mobilization ended the slump.[^s2][^s3] He reports that many economists later judged deficit spending sound in principle but the 1938 relief program too small.[^s2]

**The Holocaust.** David Wyman's *The Abandonment of the Jews* (1984) argues that political expediency shaped Roosevelt's response and that he turned away from a great moral challenge; the War Refugee Board came only in January 1944, after Treasury officials documented State Department obstruction.[^s9] Richard Breitman and Allan Lichtman, in *FDR and the Jews* (2013), concede early failures but argue that he was the first president to intervene, at least part of the time, on behalf of Jews abroad, and that resistance to Nazism would have been far weaker without his leadership; the Board is credited with saving at least 200,000 Jews.[^s9] Whether the Allies should have bombed the rail lines to Auschwitz remains part of the debate.[^s9]

## Connections

Roosevelt **held the offices** of assistant secretary of the Navy, governor of New York and president of the United States.[^s4] He was a **member of** the Democratic Party.[^s11] As commander in chief he **took part in** World War II from the declaration of war on Japan until his death.[^s4] His four elections **led to** the Twenty-second Amendment, which wrote the two-term limit into the Constitution.[^s10]
