# Theodore Roosevelt

Theodore Roosevelt (1858–1919) was the 26th U.S. president. Sworn in on September 14, 1901, at age 42 after William McKinley was assassinated, he remains the youngest person to become president.[^s1] He served until 1909; the Miller Center calls him the first modern president for expanding executive power and appealing directly to the public through the press.[^s2][^s3]

## Life and work

Roosevelt was born on October 27, 1858, at 28 East 20th Street in New York City and graduated from Harvard in June 1880.[^s1] After a term in the New York State Assembly, in 1884 his mother and his first wife, Alice Hathaway Lee, died in the same house on the same day, and he spent much of the next two years ranching in the Dakota Badlands, which he had first visited in September 1883.[^s2][^s4] He later served on the Civil Service Commission (1889–1895), as a New York City police commissioner and as assistant secretary of the Navy.[^s1]

When the Spanish-American War began in 1898, Roosevelt volunteered to help lead the 1st U.S. Volunteer Cavalry, the "Rough Riders."[^s2] On July 1, 1898, the regiment fought for the San Juan Heights in Cuba beside the Black "Buffalo Soldiers" of the 9th and 10th Cavalry; troopers of the 10th and the Rough Riders reached the summit together and forced a Spanish retreat.[^s5] War fame made him governor of New York and, in 1900, McKinley's Republican running mate.[^s1]

As president, Roosevelt set out to regulate big business rather than abolish it.[^s3] In 1902 his Justice Department sued the Northern Securities Company, a railroad combination led by J. P. Morgan, under the Sherman Antitrust Act, and the Supreme Court ruled for the government in 1904.[^s3] In the fall of 1902 he intervened in the Pennsylvania anthracite coal strike: when owners refused to negotiate he threatened to seize the mines, and both sides agreed to a commission's arbitration, a settlement he called a "square deal."[^s3] He backed the Hepburn Act on railroad rates and, after reading Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle*, the Meat Inspection Act and Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906.[^s3]

The National Park Service counts about 230 million acres of public land protected during his presidency, including 150 national forests, 51 federal bird reserves, 4 national game preserves, 5 national parks and 18 national monuments proclaimed under the Antiquities Act he signed on June 8, 1906; the Miller Center gives a lower total of nearly 200 million acres.[^s4][^s2] Abroad, he led the effort to secure rights to build the Panama Canal and announced the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine, casting the United States as the hemisphere's "policeman."[^s2] He won the 1906 Nobel Peace Prize for helping end the Russo-Japanese War, the first statesman to receive it.[^s1][^s9]

Re-elected in a landslide in 1904, Roosevelt kept a pledge not to run in 1908, a promise he later regretted.[^s2] In 1912 he lost the Republican nomination to William Howard Taft and led his supporters into the new Progressive ("Bull Moose") Party.[^s7] On October 14, 1912, he was shot in Milwaukee before a speech; his folded speech and spectacle case slowed the bullet.[^s1] He won 88 electoral votes and 4,119,207 popular votes, ahead of Taft but behind the winner, Woodrow Wilson, who took 435 electoral votes.[^s7] After a 1914 expedition down an uncharted Brazilian river, and the death of his youngest son, Quentin, in World War I, he died at Oyster Bay, New York, on January 6, 1919.[^s1][^s2][^s9]

## Legacy

In October 1901 he invited Booker T. Washington to dine at the White House, refused to apologize when the invitation drew heavy criticism in the Southern press, and appointed some Black Southerners to federal posts, but he did little to resist the disfranchisement of Black voters.[^s3] After a shooting in Brownsville, Texas, on August 13, 1906, was blamed on Black soldiers of the 25th Infantry, Roosevelt discharged 167 of them (the Miller Center gives about 160) without honor and without a trial, even though their white officers said the men had been in their barracks; Congress reversed the discharges in 1972 after new research found the soldiers innocent.[^s6][^s3] He also later belittled the Black troopers who fought beside him in Cuba, a slight the 10th Cavalry's Presley Holliday publicly rebutted.[^s5]

Six National Park Service sites honor him.[^s4] The Miller Center calls him the nation's first environmentalist president.[^s2] Grand Canyon National Park rangers have also noted that he excluded Indigenous voices from that work and held eugenic beliefs in the superiority of people of white European descent, while his own histories of the West told the conquest of Native peoples as a national triumph.[^s10][^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

Sidney Milkis of the Miller Center presents him as a reformer but not a radical: Roosevelt challenged limited government in favor of regulation for social and economic justice, believing reform was needed to head off socialism, and his racial record was a "mixed bag" that reflected his era.[^s2][^s3] Gary Gerstle argues instead that Roosevelt embodied two clashing traditions of American nationalism: a civic creed that welcomed European immigrants of every faith and class, and a racial nationalism that excluded Black and Asian Americans and, in *The Winning of the West*, told the wars against Native peoples as the forging of an American race.[^s8] As evidence that the racial strand often won, Gerstle cites Roosevelt's 1912 backing of excluding Black Southern delegates to court white voters.[^s8]

## Connections

Roosevelt **held the office** of president of the United States from 1901 to 1909, having been nominated for vice president as a **member of** the Republican Party in 1900.[^s1] In 1912 he became a **member of**, and the leader of, the Progressive Party.[^s7] He **took part in** the Spanish-American War as a Rough Rider officer and in the **anthracite coal strike of 1902** as the mediator who forced arbitration.[^s5][^s3] He **affected** the national park system: when the National Park Service was created in 1916, seven years after he left office, 23 of its 35 sites were ones he had helped create.[^s4]
