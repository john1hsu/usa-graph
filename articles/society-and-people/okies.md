# Okies

"Okies" is the name given to the poor migrants from Oklahoma, Arkansas, Texas and Missouri who moved west to California and Arizona during the Great Depression, and to the community they formed there. The word started as an ordinary shortening of the state's name, but in 1930s California it was applied to every destitute Southwestern newcomer and came to carry the force of an ethnic slur.[^s2][^s1] Their story — told in *The Grapes of Wrath*, in Dorothea Lange's photographs and in Woody Guthrie's songs — became one of the defining images of the Depression, though historians have shown it was both larger and more varied than the famous version.[^s1][^s3]

## History

**Roots of the migration.** Southwesterners had been moving to California in large numbers since about 1910, so many 1930s migrants followed relatives who had gone before.[^s1] The Depression turned that stream into a flood. Although journalists popularized the label "Dust Bowl refugees", the Dust Bowl counties themselves were thinly populated and sent relatively few people; most migrants came from the eastern parts of their states, which had drought and hard times but little dust.[^s3] In Oklahoma more left the cotton country of the southeast than the Panhandle. They were pushed out by a long farm depression, foreclosures that cost about a tenth of farmers their land between 1931 and 1933, a tenancy system that covered more than 60 percent of Oklahoma farmers, and mechanization and federal crop-reduction programs that idled land and displaced tenants.[^s1] At least half of the migrants had lived in towns or cities rather than on farms.[^s3]

**Numbers.** Roughly 300,000 to 400,000 people from the four states settled in California during the 1930s, about 95 percent of them white; Black Southwesterners mostly moved to Northern cities until the Second World War.[^s3] Oklahoma's own population fell by 59,606 between the 1930 and 1940 censuses, and its net migration loss may have reached 440,000.[^s1] The movement began in earnest in 1935 and peaked in 1937 and 1938. Families drove three days or more down Route 66; at the Barstow junction about 38 percent turned toward Los Angeles, while others went north into the San Joaquin Valley, which took in more than 70,000 Southwesterners between 1935 and 1940.[^s1] Up to 37,000 more went to Arizona's cotton fields in 1937 and 1938.[^s1]

**Reception.** Many Californians treated the newcomers as a threat to wages and relief budgets. In 1936 the Los Angeles police set up a "bum blockade" at the state line to turn back poor arrivals until lawsuits were threatened, and newcomers had to wait a year or more before they could get relief.[^s1][^s3] In the valleys, migrants worked in cotton and fruit for low wages, lived in tents and ditch-bank shantytowns, and displaced many Mexican and Filipino farmworkers while pushing wages down. The federal Farm Security Administration built model camps, growers' groups such as the Associated Farmers fought unionization, and many migrants proved reluctant strikers.[^s1][^s3]

**The law against bringing in the poor.** California's Indigent Act of 1933 made it a misdemeanor to bring a poor nonresident into the state, and in 1939 county prosecutors charged more than two dozen people under it.[^s3] One was Fred Edwards, who in December 1939 drove to Spur, Texas, to fetch his unemployed brother-in-law Frank Duncan, a former Works Progress Administration worker, and received a suspended six-month jail sentence.[^s5] On November 24, 1941, in *Edwards v. California*, the Supreme Court struck the law down. Justice James Byrnes held that it unconstitutionally burdened interstate commerce, quoting Justice Cardozo that the states "must sink or swim together"; Justices Douglas, Black and Murphy, and separately Justice Jackson, would have grounded the right to move between states in the privileges and immunities of national citizenship.[^s5]

## Today

The Second World War defense boom gave many migrants steady jobs, and many of them and their children rose economically after the war, though poverty researchers still described Okies in the San Joaquin Valley as disadvantaged into the 1970s.[^s3] Together with a larger wartime influx, more than a million people from the four states moved to California in the 1930s and 1940s, and their "Little Oklahomas" became a lasting part of the state. They brought evangelical Protestantism, especially Baptist and Pentecostal churches still common in the Central Valley, and a love of country music that shaped California's country scene from Bob Wills to Merle Haggard.[^s4][^s1] Oklahoma itself tried to reclaim the word: on May 6, 1968, Governor Dewey Bartlett launched an "OKIE" campaign, read as "Oklahoma, Key to Intelligence and Enterprise", which ended after he lost re-election in 1970.[^s2]

How the migration is remembered remains a subject of study. James Gregory notes that the sympathy the white migrants won in the 1930s was not extended to Mexican and Asian farmworkers enduring similar hardship, and that later retellings sometimes cast white poverty as nobler than poverty elsewhere.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Origins:** The migrants came from Oklahoma, Arkansas, Texas and Missouri, with Oklahomans the largest single group among the poorest arrivals.[^s1]
- **Settlement:** They settled in California's cities and farm valleys, and in smaller numbers in Arizona.[^s3][^s1]
- **Dust Bowl and Great Depression:** The migration is often conflated with the Dust Bowl, but drought, debt, tenancy and mechanization across the region were the main forces behind it.[^s1][^s3]
- **Edwards v. California:** The prosecutions of people who brought migrant relatives into California produced the 1941 ruling that states may not bar poor Americans from entering.[^s5]
- **The Grapes of Wrath:** Steinbeck's 1939 novel about the Joad family made the migration famous and deepened the stigma of the word "Okie".[^s2][^s1]
- **Country music:** Migrant communities helped make California a center of country music.[^s4]
