# Desegregation of Little Rock Central High School

In September 1957 nine Black teenagers set out to attend Little Rock Central High School, the all-white flagship of the Arkansas capital's school system. Governor Orval Faubus used the Arkansas National Guard to keep them out, a mob gathered when they finally entered, and President Dwight D. Eisenhower sent the 101st Airborne Division to escort them in. Broadcast worldwide on the young medium of television, the standoff tested whether a state could defy federal court orders carrying out *Brown v. Board of Education*.[^s1][^s3]

## Background

After the Supreme Court declared school segregation unconstitutional in 1954, the Little Rock School Board said it would comply, and in May 1955 it adopted a gradual plan named for Superintendent Virgil Blossom. It began with token desegregation at Central in 1957 and let students transfer out of schools where their race was in the minority, which kept Horace Mann High School predominantly Black.[^s2][^s7] An NAACP suit in 1956 led the federal courts to uphold the plan and make it mandatory. Resistance organized too: the Capital Citizens' Council formed in 1956, Arkansas's whole congressional delegation signed the Southern Manifesto, and in August 1957 the segregationist Mothers' League of Central High won a state injunction that federal judge Ronald Davies voided the next day.[^s2]

Daisy Bates, president of the Arkansas Conference of NAACP branches and co-publisher with her husband L. C. Bates of the *Arkansas State Press*, emerged as the leading public voice for desegregation and worked closely with the students. She and the students' families faced intimidation, including attacks on the Bates home.[^s9]

## What happened

On September 2, 1957, Faubus announced on television that he had called out the Guard, saying he acted to prevent violence. On September 4 guardsmen turned the Black students away. Fifteen-year-old Elizabeth Eckford, arriving alone, walked past a jeering crowd, and photographs of her ordeal ran around the world.[^s1][^s2] The Park Service counts ten students in the first attempt; Jane Hill withdrew after her family received threats.[^s1] A September 14 meeting with Eisenhower at Newport, Rhode Island, settled nothing. On September 20 Judge Davies ordered the governor to stop interfering, and Faubus withdrew the Guard.[^s2]

On Monday, September 23, the students slipped into the building while a mob of more than 1,000 white protesters gathered outside and beat four Black reporters; police removed the students by lunchtime. Mayor Woodrow Wilson Mann asked Washington for help.[^s2] Eisenhower issued Proclamation 3204 ordering the crowd to disperse, and on September 24 signed Executive Order 10730, which federalized the Arkansas National Guard and authorized the Secretary of Defense to use the armed forces; about 1,000 paratroopers of the 101st Airborne were sent.[^s3] In an address to the nation on September 24 he said, "Mob rule cannot be allowed to override the decisions of our courts."[^s4]

On September 25 soldiers escorted the Little Rock Nine — Minnijean Brown, Elizabeth Eckford, Ernest Green, Thelma Mothershed, Melba Pattillo, Gloria Ray, Terrence Roberts, Jefferson Thomas and Carlotta Walls — through the front door.[^s1][^s7] The paratroopers left in November, but federalized guardsmen stayed all year while the students endured verbal and physical harassment. More than 100 white students were suspended and four expelled; Minnijean Brown was expelled in February 1958 after she retaliated, and finished high school in New York. In May 1958 Ernest Green, the only senior, became Central's first Black graduate.[^s1][^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

The school board then asked to postpone desegregation. In *Cooper v. Aaron*, decided on September 12, 1958, and explained in an opinion of September 29 signed by all nine justices, the Supreme Court refused. Relying on the Supremacy Clause and *Marbury v. Madison*, it held that no state official may "war against the Constitution" by nullifying federal court orders.[^s5]

Faubus answered with Act 4, signed the same day as the Court's order, and closed all four Little Rock high schools from September 15, 1958. On September 27 voters chose closure by three to one. The "Lost Year" displaced 3,665 students — 2,915 white and 750 Black, half of the latter finding no schooling at all. After the board fired 44 teachers and administrators in May 1959, the STOP campaign recalled three segregationist board members, and the schools reopened on August 12, 1959.[^s6] Faubus, meanwhile, won four more terms and served six in all.[^s8]

The Nine have since been honored with statues at the Arkansas State Capitol (2005) and a street renamed Little Rock Nine Way (2022), and the school is preserved as Little Rock Central High School National Historic Site.[^s7][^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

**Eisenhower's role.** The Park Service notes that Eisenhower privately thought civil rights advocates were moving too fast, preferred gradual change and state handling of the matter, and avoided humiliating Faubus; in this reading he acted to defend the courts' authority, not as an integration crusader. The Miller Center assessment the Park Service cites adds that, however uncomfortable, he would not let state officials defy the Supreme Court.[^s4]

**Faubus's motives.** Roy Reed's biographical entry describes Faubus before 1957 as a racial moderate who hired Black state employees and backed abolition of the poll tax, and argues that he calculated a moderate could not win re-election in 1958. Some observers have insisted that pandering to white supremacists was out of character for him, and many voters saw him as standing up to federal power.[^s8]

**The Cold War dimension.** An exhibit by UA Little Rock's Center for Arkansas History and Culture, drawing on scholars such as Mary Dudziak and Azza Layton, emphasizes that Soviet newspapers seized on Little Rock to portray American democracy as a façade and that U.S. officials such as John Foster Dulles and Henry Cabot Lodge warned it was hurting American influence abroad; in this view international pressure shaped Washington's response.[^s10] Eisenhower himself cited damage to the nation's prestige abroad.[^s4]

## Connections

The crisis **occurred in** Little Rock, Arkansas,[^s1] and **occurred during** the Cold War, which shaped how it was seen abroad.[^s10] It was **influenced by** *Brown v. Board of Education*, whose mandate the Blossom Plan set out to carry out, and it unfolded alongside the massive resistance signalled by the Southern Manifesto.[^s2] It **led to** Executive Order 10730, which framed the intervention as removing an obstruction of justice;[^s3] to *Cooper v. Aaron*;[^s5] and to the closing of the city's high schools in the Lost Year.[^s6]
