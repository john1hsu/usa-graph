# Cherokee Nation v. Georgia

*Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* was the 1831 Supreme Court case in which the Cherokee Nation, led by Principal Chief John Ross, sued the state of Georgia to stop it from enforcing state laws inside Cherokee territory.[^s3][^s5] On March 18, 1831, the Court denied the injunction and dismissed the suit, holding that it had no jurisdiction because the Cherokee Nation was not a foreign state under the Constitution.[^s3][^s1] The opinions are printed in the official U.S. Reports at 30 U.S. (5 Peters) 1.[^s2] Chief Justice John Marshall's opinion nonetheless called the Cherokees a distinct political society and used the phrase "domestic dependent nations," language that still anchors federal Indian law.[^s1][^s8]

## Background

By the 1820s the Cherokee Nation held its homeland in what is now north Georgia and neighbouring states under treaties with the United States, and in 1827 it adopted a written constitution modeled on the federal one.[^s5] Georgia answered by declaring the Cherokee government abolished in 1828 and placing Cherokee citizens under state law; the state annexed Cherokee lands, abolished Cherokee courts and laws, and set up a process to distribute the land to white citizens.[^s5][^s4] The pressure sharpened in 1828, when white Americans found gold on Cherokee land around Dahlonega and Auraria and miners poured in.[^s6] On May 28, 1830, President Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act, which authorized removal treaties but did not itself remove anyone.[^s6]

When negotiations with the Jackson administration failed, the Nation hired William Wirt, U.S. attorney general from 1817 to 1829, who chose to sue Georgia directly in the Supreme Court under its original jurisdiction rather than trust the state courts.[^s7][^s3] Georgia refused to appear.[^s7] The Nation's supplemental bill told the justices that Georgia had hanged a Cherokee man, Corn Tassel, even after the Chief Justice had allowed a writ of error in his case.[^s1]

## The decision

The motion was set for hearing on March 5, 1831, in the January Term, and the judgment entered on March 18 denied the injunction and dismissed the bill.[^s1][^s3] The National Archives preserves the signed judgment in the Supreme Court's original-jurisdiction case files.[^s3] Marshall, writing for a divided Court, accepted that the Cherokee Nation was a state in the sense of a distinct political society able to govern itself, but held that it was not a foreign state that Article III allows to sue a state.[^s1] He reasoned that the commerce clause names foreign nations, the several states and the Indian tribes as separate categories, and he described the tribes as "domestic dependent nations" whose relation to the United States resembled that of a ward to his guardian.[^s1][^s7] He added that the question of title to Cherokee land might be decided in a proper case with proper parties.[^s1]

The justices split three ways.[^s1] Justice Johnson doubted the Cherokees were a state at all and saw the claim as political rather than judicial, while Justice Baldwin concurred in dismissal on the ground that there was no proper plaintiff.[^s1] Justice Thompson, joined by Justice Story, dissented: he would have held the Nation a foreign state, found Georgia's laws in violation of its treaties and the 1802 federal trade and intercourse act, and granted the injunction.[^s1]

## Legacy

The case is the second of the Marshall Trilogy, the three decisions that form the foundation of federal Indian law.[^s6][^s8] Marshall's hint about a proper case was realized in 1832, when missionaries convicted under Georgia's license law appealed; in *Worcester v. Georgia* the Court held that Georgia's laws had no force within the Cherokee Nation.[^s7][^s4][^s5] Georgia ignored that ruling and Jackson did not enforce it.[^s4][^s6] The line "John Marshall has made his decision, now let him enforce it" is only rumored to be Jackson's, and it concerned *Worcester*, not this case.[^s7] His documented reaction, in a letter to John Coffee, was that the decision had "fell still born."[^s4]

The Nation's legal victories did not stop removal. In 1835 a small group of Cherokees without authority under Cherokee law signed the Treaty of New Echota, which the Senate ratified in March 1836 over Ross's objections.[^s6][^s5] In May 1838 President Martin Van Buren ordered General Winfield Scott to remove the remaining Cherokees, and the Cherokee Nation records that some 4,000 people, about a quarter of its population, died in the camps or on the Trail of Tears.[^s6][^s5]

The decision's double edge endures. Its recognition of tribes as self-governing nations whose sovereignty predates the United States supports tribal self-government today, while its guardian-and-ward language became a root of the federal trust responsibility, the federal duty to protect tribal lands and provide services in return for the land tribes gave up.[^s8] The Cherokee Nation, rebuilt at Tahlequah in present-day Oklahoma, continues to exercise its treaty rights and sovereign authority.[^s5]
