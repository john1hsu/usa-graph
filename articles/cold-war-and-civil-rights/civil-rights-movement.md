# Civil rights movement

The civil rights movement was the organized struggle of Black Americans and their allies to tear down legal segregation, end racial discrimination and win the full citizenship promised by the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. Its most visible phase, often dated from the *Brown v. Board of Education* decision in 1954 to the late 1960s, brought boycotts, sit-ins, Freedom Rides and mass marches, and it pushed Congress to pass landmark civil rights laws.[^s1][^s2] Historians debate where to draw its boundaries, and the movement was far larger than any single leader or campaign.[^s10][^s11]

## Origins

Black Americans had resisted slavery and Jim Crow for generations, and the groundwork for the postwar movement was laid in the 1940s. In June 1941 A. Philip Randolph's threat of a march on Washington led President Franklin D. Roosevelt to issue Executive Order 8802, barring discrimination in defense industries with federal contracts. Students in Chicago founded the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) in 1942 to practice nonviolent direct action, and in July 1948 President Harry S. Truman ordered equal treatment in the armed forces.[^s3] Meanwhile civil rights lawyers, including the NAACP's Thurgood Marshall, won Supreme Court cases against the all-white primary in 1944, segregation on interstate buses in 1946 and court enforcement of racially restrictive housing covenants in 1948; the NAACP's legal strategy against segregated schools culminated in *Brown*.[^s3][^s1]

## Goals and methods

The movement sought an end to segregated schools, buses, lunch counters and public facilities; protection of the right to vote; and fair access to jobs and housing. The 1963 March on Washington put these goals in one list, calling for a comprehensive civil rights bill, voting protections, school desegregation, job training and a federal ban on employment discrimination.[^s4]

Its methods ranged from lawsuits to mass nonviolent protest. After Rosa Parks's arrest on December 1, 1955, Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama, formed the Montgomery Improvement Association and boycotted the city's buses. In January 1957 more than sixty ministers formed the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC). On February 1, 1960, four North Carolina A&T students sat down at a whites-only Woolworth's lunch counter in Greensboro, and sit-ins spread across the South within weeks; Clara Luper and Oklahoma City's NAACP Youth Council had begun sit-ins as early as 1958. In April 1960 Ella Baker, a veteran NAACP organizer and SCLC co-founder, convened the student conference at Shaw University that created the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC). Diane Nash helped lead the Nashville sit-ins and later the SNCC students who resumed the Freedom Rides, and Gloria Richardson led the Cambridge Nonviolent Action Committee in Maryland.[^s1]

Activists faced violent resistance. A bus carrying CORE's Freedom Riders was firebombed near Anniston, Alabama, in May 1961, and more than 300 riders were jailed in Mississippi that summer. In Birmingham in May 1963 police turned fire hoses and dogs on marching young people, and the televised images shocked the nation. Medgar Evers, the NAACP's Mississippi field secretary, was murdered at his home hours after President John F. Kennedy's televised civil rights address in June 1963. During Mississippi Freedom Summer in 1964, local Klansmen murdered the civil rights workers James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner.[^s1][^s2] On March 7, 1965, state troopers and local officers attacked about 600 voting-rights marchers on Selma's Edmund Pettus Bridge; two weeks later about 3,200 people set out on a march to Montgomery, arriving 25,000 strong.[^s5]

The movement also reached beyond the South. In 1966 King and SCLC joined Chicago activists against de facto segregation in housing, schools and jobs; marchers in white neighborhoods met violent crowds, and King was struck by a rock. The campaign won an open-housing agreement in August 1966, but in March 1967 King said city agencies had reneged on it.[^s7]

## Outcomes

The pressure produced landmark laws. After a Senate filibuster broken by a 71–29 cloture vote, Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act on July 2, 1964, banning discrimination in public accommodations, public facilities, federally funded programs and employment.[^s2] The Voting Rights Act, signed on August 6, 1965, outlawed literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters; about 250,000 new Black voters registered by the end of 1965.[^s6] One week after King's assassination in Memphis on April 4, 1968, Johnson signed the Fair Housing Act.[^s9]

The movement also changed internally. In June 1966 Stokely Carmichael of SNCC made "Black Power" a public slogan; SNCC and CORE moved away from nonviolence, while King, SCLC and the NAACP rejected the slogan, which King nonetheless called "a cry of pain."[^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on the events but disagree about the movement's boundaries. Jacquelyn Dowd Hall argues that the familiar account, a Southern, decade-long story running from *Brown* to the voting laws of 1964 and 1965 and focused on non-economic goals, "distorts and suppresses as much as it reveals."[^s10] She describes instead a long movement that began in the late 1930s, linked racial and economic justice from the start and treated racism as a national rather than only a Southern problem.[^s12] Sundiata Cha-Jua and Clarence Lang counter that stretching the movement so far blurs chronology, leaves key terms undefined, and erases differences of place and between civil rights and Black Power campaigns, yielding what they call a "largely ahistorical and placeless chronicle."[^s11]

## Connections

The movement arose from and was led above all by Black Americans organizing in their own churches, colleges and communities.[^s1] Its demands, voiced at the March on Washington, shaped the Civil Rights Act of 1964,[^s4] and the attack at Selma helped persuade Johnson and Congress to pass the Voting Rights Act of 1965.[^s6] Its campaigns targeted racial segregation in public life, which the 1964 act outlawed in public accommodations and facilities.[^s2] Its turn in 1966 helped give rise to the Black Power movement.[^s8] It built on the legal victory in *Brown v. Board of Education* and includes the Montgomery bus boycott and the Selma to Montgomery marches; it is best understood against the background of Jim Crow laws.[^s1][^s5]
