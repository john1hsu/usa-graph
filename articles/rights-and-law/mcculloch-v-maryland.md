# McCulloch v. Maryland

*McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) is the Supreme Court decision that answered two questions at the heart of American federalism: whether Congress may use powers the Constitution does not list word for word, and whether a state may tax an institution the national government has created. In a unanimous opinion by Chief Justice John Marshall, the Court said yes to the first and no to the second.[^s1][^s2] It remains the leading authority on the Necessary and Proper Clause.[^s3]

## Background

Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States in 1816, partly to rein in the unregulated paper money issued by state banks. President James Madison signed the act in April 1816, and the bank opened in Philadelphia in January 1817 with a twenty-year charter and $35 million in capital.[^s2][^s4] Its early management overextended credit and then contracted it sharply, helping to bring on a financial panic and a deflationary depression that its policies made worse.[^s4]

The constitutional question was older than the bank. When the First Congress chartered a national bank, the idea was, as Marshall later put it, resisted first in open debate and then in the executive cabinet, where Thomas Jefferson and Alexander Hamilton disagreed and President George Washington sided with Hamilton.[^s1][^s8] The Constitutional Convention had declined to give Congress an express power to create corporations.[^s8]

On February 11, 1818, Maryland passed a law requiring any bank operating in the state without a charter from its legislature to issue notes only on stamped paper, with fees from 10 cents on a $5 note to $20 on a $1,000 note, or else pay $15,000 a year in advance.[^s1] James W. McCulloch, cashier of the bank's Baltimore branch, issued unstamped notes, and Maryland sued him to collect. The state's courts ruled against him, and the Court of Appeals of Maryland affirmed, so he took the case to the Supreme Court.[^s1][^s2] Counsel for the bank included Daniel Webster, Attorney General William Wirt and William Pinkney; Luther Martin argued for Maryland.[^s3]

## The decision

The Court announced its decision on March 6, 1819, the date recorded in its minutes.[^s2] On the first question, Marshall acknowledged that all agree the national government has only the powers enumerated in the Constitution, but said such a government must have means to carry them out. He noted that the Tenth Amendment, unlike the Articles of Confederation, omits the word "expressly," and he argued that a constitution cannot spell out every detail: "we must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding."[^s1] Reading "necessary" as "absolutely necessary," he said, would ignore that the word admits of degrees, and that Article I, Section 10 uses "absolutely necessary" where the framers wanted that stricter sense.[^s1][^s3] His test: if the end is legitimate and within the Constitution's scope, any appropriate means plainly adapted to it, not prohibited and consistent with the Constitution's letter and spirit, is constitutional. A national bank was such a means for the powers to tax, borrow, regulate commerce and support armies.[^s1][^s3]

On the second question, Marshall argued that the Constitution was ratified by the people in state conventions rather than by the states as sovereigns, so the national government is truly a government of the people and supreme within its sphere.[^s1] He accepted as undeniable the argument that the power to tax involves the power to destroy, and so could defeat the power to create; a single state could not tax the operations of an institution created by the people of the whole Union. Maryland's tax was void, though the Court said a state could still tax the bank's real estate, or Marylanders' shares in it, like other property.[^s1][^s2]

## Legacy

The ruling drew sharp criticism. In June 1819 Virginia judge Spencer Roane published four essays signed "Hampden" in the *Richmond Enquirer* attacking it, and sent them to Madison, calling the issue as important as independence itself.[^s7] Madison replied that the Court's rule of construction was "as broad and as pliant" as few of the Constitution's supporters had expected, and warned that letting ends and means shift at the legislature's will could erase the limits set by enumeration.[^s5] In 1832 President Andrew Jackson vetoed the bank's recharter, insisting that the Supreme Court's authority must not control Congress or the President, that every officer swears to support the Constitution as he understands it, and that whether the bank's features were necessary and proper was for them to judge.[^s6][^s9] The bank's federal charter lapsed in 1836.[^s4]

In the courts, the decision endured. *Osborn v. Bank of the United States* (1824) extended it, and twentieth- and twenty-first-century cases relied on it to shield federally authorized operations from state taxation and state licensing rules.[^s3]

## Debates and interpretations

Scholars still weigh the decision's importance. Harvard law professor Mark Tushnet has called it more important than *Marbury v. Madison* because it underwrote expansive views of national power, while also noting that most of the work in modern cases is done by broad readings of Congress's substantive powers, such as the commerce power, rather than by the Necessary and Proper Clause itself.[^s8] He has described the dispute as one between a Federalist and later Whig vision of national authority and the Jeffersonian and Jacksonian views of its critics, a framing that Madison's and Roane's letters and Jackson's veto illustrate from the other side.[^s5][^s6][^s8]

## Connections

Marshall wrote the opinion, which interprets the Necessary and Proper Clause and reads the Tenth Amendment as reserving only powers not delegated, without the word "expressly."[^s1] The decision secured the constitutionality of the Second Bank of the United States and set a lasting rule for the division of power between the nation and the states.[^s2] Jackson's 1832 veto message challenged the idea that the Court's ruling settled the bank question for the other branches.[^s6] It is often paired with *Marbury v. Madison* as one of the Marshall Court's foundational rulings.[^s8]
