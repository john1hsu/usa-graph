# COVID-19 pandemic in the United States

The COVID-19 pandemic was the spread of a new coronavirus disease through the United States beginning in January 2020. Over the next three years it killed more than a million Americans, erased more than 20 million jobs in a single month, and prompted emergency federal relief laws and a crash program to develop vaccines. Federal officials ended the national public health emergency on May 11, 2023, although the virus itself continued to circulate.[^s1][^s2]

## Background

On January 20, 2020, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) announced the first laboratory-confirmed U.S. case, a patient in Washington state whose samples had been taken two days earlier, and activated its Emergency Operations Center. On January 31, Health and Human Services (HHS) Secretary Alex Azar declared a public health emergency, the same day the World Health Organization (WHO) declared an international emergency.[^s1]

## What happened

The first U.S. death from a laboratory-confirmed case was reported in Washington state on February 29, 2020. WHO declared a global pandemic on March 11, and two days later the Trump administration declared a nationwide emergency. Recorded U.S. deaths passed 100,000 on May 28, 2020.[^s1]

Congress responded with emergency spending. The Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act, or CARES Act, became Public Law 116-136 on March 27, 2020, after the Senate passed its version on March 25 and the House agreed two days later.[^s4]

Shutdowns meant to slow the virus produced an economic shock without modern precedent. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reported that payroll employment fell by 20.5 million in April 2020 and that unemployment reached 14.7 percent, the highest rate and the largest one-month increase since the series began in 1948. Leisure and hospitality lost 7.7 million jobs, almost half its workforce, in that one month.[^s3]

On April 30, 2020, the administration launched Operation Warp Speed to develop a vaccine as fast as possible. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued its first COVID-19 vaccine emergency use authorization, for the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine, on December 11, 2020, and on December 14 Sandra Lindsay, a New York nurse, became the first American outside clinical trials to be vaccinated. New variants brought fresh waves: Delta became dominant by June 1, 2021, and the first U.S. Omicron case was found in California on December 1, 2021. Recorded U.S. deaths reached 1 million on May 12, 2022.[^s1]

## Aftermath and legacy

The pandemic left a measurable mark on American lives. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) reported that life expectancy at birth fell to 77.0 years in 2020 and 76.1 years in 2021, the lowest level since 1996. COVID-19 accounted for about three-quarters of the 2020 drop and half of the 2021 drop.[^s5]

The losses fell unequally. American Indian and Alaska Native people lost 6.6 years of life expectancy between 2019 and 2021, falling to 65.2 years. Hispanic Americans lost 4.0 years from 2019 to 2020. Black Americans fell to 70.8 years in 2021, and for both Black and White Americans the 2021 figure was the lowest since 1995.[^s5]

When HHS announced the end of the public health emergency in May 2023, it reported that COVID-19 deaths had fallen 95 percent and hospitalizations nearly 91 percent since January 2021, and that more than 270 million people had received at least one vaccine dose. The end of the emergency stopped required no-cost coverage of tests by Medicare and private insurers and ended HHS's express authority to require some laboratory reporting. Vaccines stayed free, treatments stayed available, and existing emergency use authorizations were unaffected.[^s2]

## Connections

The pandemic took place across the United States, from the first case in Washington state to the first Omicron case in California.[^s1] It led directly to the CARES Act, a major emergency relief law named for the coronavirus.[^s4] It also led to Operation Warp Speed, the federal effort to speed vaccine development.[^s1]

Among the communities it affected, Native Americans suffered the steepest losses in life expectancy of any group NCHS measured.[^s5] It invites comparison with the 1918 influenza pandemic, which CDC estimates killed about 675,000 people in the United States; COVID-19's recorded toll passed that figure and reached 1 million by May 2022.[^s6][^s1]
