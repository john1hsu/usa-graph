  - question: Why did the Senate censure Jackson in 1834, and what happened to the censure?
    answer: After vetoing the Bank's recharter he fired a treasury secretary who would not remove federal deposits and had Roger Taney move them to state banks; the Senate censured him 26 to 20 for assuming powers not given by law, and a Democratic majority expunged the censure in January 1837.

    answer: States dropped property requirements so more poor white men could vote, but by 1839 almost all states limited Black voting, women remained excluded, and Jackson's removal policy drove nearly 50,000 Native people from their homelands.

# Andrew Jackson

Andrew Jackson (1767–1845) was the seventh president of the United States, serving from March 4, 1829, to March 4, 1837.[^s5] A frontier lawyer, cotton planter and general, he became the most famous American soldier since George Washington and then built a presidency on the claim to speak for ordinary people against entrenched power.[^s1] He was also an enslaver and the chief architect of Native removal, and historians still argue over how to weigh those parts of his record.[^s3][^s11]

## Life and work

Jackson was born on March 15, 1767, in the Waxhaw settlement on the Carolina frontier, to Scotch-Irish immigrant parents; his father died before he was born.[^s1][^s4] As a teenager in the Revolution he and his brother Robert were captured by the British in 1781 and caught smallpox, which killed Robert; his mother and other brother also died during the war.[^s2] He studied law and moved in 1788 to Nashville, where he became a district prosecutor. Tennessee sent him to the U.S. House in 1796 and the Senate in 1797, and he sat on the state's superior court from 1798 to 1804.[^s4] In 1791 he began living with Rachel Donelson Robards, whose first husband had abandoned her; they formally married in 1794 after her divorce, and the episode was used against him for decades.[^s1][^s2]

Elected major general of the Tennessee militia in 1802, Jackson led the war against the Red Stick Creeks in 1813–1814, ending at Horseshoe Bend in March 1814; the Treaty of Fort Jackson that August transferred most Creek land in Alabama and southern Georgia to the United States.[^s4] On January 8, 1815, his army defeated the British at New Orleans, weeks after the Treaty of Ghent had been signed; British casualties exceeded two thousand, against thirteen Americans killed.[^s2] In 1816 forces under his command destroyed the so-called Negro Fort in Spanish Florida, a refuge for people who had escaped slavery, killing about 270 people, and in 1818 he invaded Florida during the First Seminole War.[^s14][^s1] Spain ceded Florida in 1819, and Jackson briefly governed the new territory.[^s1][^s2]

Jackson's wealth rested on slavery. The number of people he enslaved rose from 20 in 1812 to 44 in 1820 and to nearly 100 by his presidency; his estate inventory in 1845 counted 161 people at the Hermitage and a Mississippi plantation.[^s11]

In 1824 Jackson led a four-way race in both popular and electoral votes but lacked a majority, and the House chose John Quincy Adams after Henry Clay backed him; when Adams named Clay secretary of state, Jacksonians denounced a "corrupt bargain."[^s14] Jackson beat Adams in 1828, but Rachel died on December 22, 1828, before the inauguration.[^s4]

As president he replaced many officeholders on partisan grounds, the start of the "spoils system," and vetoed twelve bills, more than his six predecessors combined.[^s1][^s3] Congress passed only one major law at his request, the Indian Removal Act, which he signed on May 28, 1830.[^s3][^s10] His administration signed almost seventy removal treaties, and nearly 50,000 people from eastern Native nations were forced to Indian Territory; under the Treaty of New Echota, about 4,000 of 16,000 Cherokees died on the Trail of Tears in 1838–39.[^s9][^s10] When the Supreme Court upheld Cherokee sovereignty in *Worcester v. Georgia* in 1832, Jackson did nothing to protect the Cherokees and urged them to move.[^s12]

On July 10, 1832, Jackson vetoed the recharter of the Second Bank of the United States, attacking laws that would "make the rich richer and the potent more powerful."[^s6] He then had federal deposits moved to state banks, firing a treasury secretary who refused; the Senate censured him 26 to 20 on March 28, 1834, and expunged the censure in January 1837.[^s8] When South Carolina declared the federal tariff null and void, his proclamation of December 10, 1832, rejected nullification, and a compromise tariff in 1833 ended the crisis.[^s7][^s1] He retired to the Hermitage and died there on June 8, 1845.[^s5][^s1]

## Legacy

Jackson turned a personal following into the Democratic Party, the country's most durable party, and his opponents organized as Whigs, mocking him as a would-be king; the rivalry helped fix the national two-party system.[^s3][^s14] His direct appeal to voters and aggressive use of the veto enlarged the presidency.[^s3] His era widened voting for poor white men while almost every state restricted Black voting by 1839.[^s14]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians have long disagreed about Jackson.[^s3][^s15] Frederick Jackson Turner saw Jacksonianism as a frontier protest against eastern elites, and Arthur Schlesinger Jr.'s *The Age of Jackson* (1945) cast it as an alliance of farmers and workers against capitalists.[^s15] Richard Hofstadter and Bray Hammond instead saw rising entrepreneurs, while Sean Wilentz has portrayed Jacksonian politics as broadly democratizing and Robert Remini defended Jackson as a genuine man of the people in his time.[^s15] Michael Rogin stressed his violent hostility to Native peoples, Alexander Saxton argued that the Democrats chiefly defended slavery and white supremacy, and Daniel Walker Howe judged the Whigs the more democratic party.[^s15] Daniel Feller writes that admirers see a symbol of individualism and democracy, critics a vengeful near-Caesar.[^s3]

## Connections

Jackson **held the office** of the presidency from 1829 to 1837 as a Democrat, and so was a **member of** the Democratic Party; earlier he was a **member of** the U.S. Senate.[^s5][^s4] He **participated in** the American Revolutionary War as a boy, the War of 1812, the Creek War, the Battle of New Orleans and the First Seminole War.[^s2][^s4][^s14] He also **participated in** the election of 1828, the nullification crisis and the Bank War that led to his censure.[^s1][^s14][^s8] He signed the Indian Removal Act, and his response to *Worcester v. Georgia* gave rise to the "let him enforce it" myth.[^s10][^s13]
