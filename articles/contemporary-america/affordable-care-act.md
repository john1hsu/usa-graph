  - question: Why did the Supreme Court uphold the individual mandate in NFIB v. Sebelius even though it rejected the Commerce Clause argument for it?
    answer: The Court held that the penalty for going without insurance could be sustained as an exercise of Congress's power to levy taxes, so the mandate was constitutional on that basis even though it was not valid under the Commerce Clause or the Necessary and Proper Clause.
  - question: How did NFIB v. Sebelius change the Medicaid expansion?

# Affordable Care Act

The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act, usually called the Affordable Care Act (ACA) and nicknamed "Obamacare," is the federal law that reshaped how Americans buy and qualify for health insurance. President Barack Obama signed it on March 23, 2010, and a week later signed the Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act, which amended it.[^s4] Its main goal was to widen access to coverage through subsidized private insurance and a larger Medicaid program, paid for by new taxes and fees and by savings in Medicare and Medicaid.[^s3] It passed without a single Republican vote and has remained one of the most debated laws of the early twenty-first century.[^s2][^s4]

## Background

Broad health coverage had been a goal of Democratic presidents since Harry Truman. Congress created Medicare and Medicaid in 1965, but later efforts under Jimmy Carter and Bill Clinton to cover more of the uninsured failed.[^s4] Obama chose to pursue reform in his first year, while his party held large majorities in both houses. Support sagged through the summer of 2009, when Tea Party protesters confronted lawmakers at town-hall meetings; a prime-time address to a joint session of Congress on September 9, 2009, helped steady it.[^s4]

The Senate passed its version of the bill, H.R. 3590, by 60 votes to 39 on December 24, 2009.[^s1] On March 21, 2010, the House agreed to the Senate's version 219 to 212: all the yes votes came from Democrats, while 34 Democrats and all 178 voting Republicans voted no.[^s2] A separate reconciliation bill, signed a week after the main law, restored some features House Democrats preferred.[^s4]

## What it says

The law has ten titles; most deal with how health care is financed, organized and delivered, and Title IX holds the revenue provisions.[^s3] Its best-known parts are these:[^s3]

- **Individual mandate.** From 2014 most people had to have health insurance or pay a penalty, and some employers with more than 50 workers that did not offer coverage could also face penalties.[^s3]
- **Exchanges and premium tax credits.** Each state could run a marketplace, or "exchange," for individual policies, with the federal government running one where a state did not; people with incomes from 100 to 400 percent of the federal poverty level could receive tax credits toward premiums.[^s3]
- **Medicaid expansion.** Eligibility was to extend to non-elderly adults with incomes up to 133 percent of the poverty level, with Washington paying all of the new cost at first and 90 percent by 2020.[^s3]
- **Insurance rules.** Young adults could stay on a parent's plan until 26, and insurers could no longer exclude preexisting conditions, for children at once and for everyone from 2014.[^s3]
- **Medicare and financing.** Lower payments to Medicare Advantage plans and hospitals, an additional Medicare payroll tax on high earners, and other taxes and fees.[^s3]

In March 2012 the Congressional Budget Office projected that the law would leave about 33 million more non-elderly people insured in 2021, raising the insured share from about 82 to about 93 percent.[^s3]

## Legacy

The Supreme Court heard three major challenges. In *NFIB v. Sebelius* (June 28, 2012) a 5–4 majority upheld the individual mandate as an exercise of Congress's taxing power while rejecting the Commerce Clause argument for it, and seven justices held that states could not be forced to expand Medicaid, making the expansion optional.[^s3][^s4] In *King v. Burwell* (June 25, 2015) the Court ruled 6–3 that tax credits were available to buyers on the federal exchange, not only in states that built their own; this was a question of reading the statute, not of its constitutionality.[^s7] Other cases dealt with parts of the law, such as *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby* (2014), which let closely held companies with religious objections decline to cover contraception.[^s4]

The 2013 launch of the online marketplace was marred by website failures.[^s4] Republicans, who won the House in 2010 and the Senate in 2014, voted many times to repeal the law, and in January 2016 Obama vetoed the first repeal bill to reach him.[^s4] The 2017 tax law set the penalty for going without coverage at $0 starting in 2019. Texas and other states then argued that the mandate, and with it the whole law, was invalid, but in *California v. Texas* (June 17, 2021) the Court ruled 7–2 that the plaintiffs lacked standing; Justices Alito and Gorsuch dissented.[^s5]

Coverage grew as the main provisions took effect. The Census Bureau's survey found 13.3 percent of people uninsured for all of 2013 and 8.8 percent, or 28.1 million, in 2016, with higher uninsured rates in states that had not expanded Medicaid than in states that had.[^s6] The Miller Center notes that the uninsured share of adults fell from 18 to 11 percent during Obama's presidency and that the growth of health costs slowed.[^s4]

## Debates and interpretations

Supporters, led by Obama, argued that the law would give more security to people who had insurance, cover people who did not, and slow rising costs without adding to the deficit.[^s4] Critics, including many Americans who already had insurance, worried that the changes would leave them worse off and add to the deficit; Republican leaders pledged for years to "repeal and replace" it.[^s4] The Census figures describe how coverage changed after 2013; how much of that change the law caused, and whether its costs were worth it, remain debated by Americans, who stayed deeply divided about it.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

- **United States Congress:** enacted the law through the Senate vote of December 2009 and the House vote of March 2010.[^s1][^s2]
- **Barack Obama:** chose to pursue health care reform in his first year in office and signed the law in 2010.[^s4]
- **Medicaid:** the law sought to extend the program to low-income adults, an expansion that *NFIB v. Sebelius* made optional for states.[^s3]
- **NFIB v. Sebelius** and **California v. Texas:** two of the constitutional challenges the law led to; both left it standing.[^s3][^s5]
- **King v. Burwell:** settled whether tax credits flowed through the federal exchange.[^s7]
