  myth: The delegates were sent to Philadelphia to write a new constitution.
  fact: Congress called the meeting only to revise the Articles of Confederation and report proposed alterations to Congress and the states; the delegates decided on their own, by mid-June 1787, to draft an entirely new frame of government, and their Constitution took effect once nine state conventions had ratified it.
  why: Because the convention's product replaced the Articles completely and is commemorated on September 17, it is easy to assume a new constitution was the assignment from the start, which hides how bold, and how contested, the delegates' decision was.

# Constitutional Convention

The Constitutional Convention, also called the Federal Convention, was the meeting in the Pennsylvania State House (now Independence Hall) in Philadelphia where, between May and September 1787, delegates drafted the Constitution of the United States in closed sessions.[^s1] Fifty-five men attended, representing every state except Rhode Island, which sent no one.[^s6][^s9] Called only to revise the Articles of Confederation, the convention instead produced an entirely new frame of government.[^s1][^s2]

## Background

In September 1786 commissioners from five states who met at Annapolis reported that the federal government was ineffective and its powers needed strengthening.[^s2] On February 21, 1787, the Confederation Congress resolved that a convention should meet in Philadelphia in May "for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation," reporting any alterations back to Congress and the state legislatures for approval.[^s2]

## What happened

The convention was due to open on May 14, but only two delegations had arrived, so members adjourned from day to day until seven states were present on May 25.[^s1][^s3] That day the delegates unanimously elected George Washington their president and chose William Jackson as secretary.[^s3] Each state delegation cast one vote, and on May 29 the convention agreed that nothing said inside could be printed or communicated without leave.[^s6][^s4]

On May 29 Edmund Randolph of Virginia opened the main business with fifteen resolutions, the Virginia Plan, drafted by the Virginia delegation that included James Madison. They proposed a national legislature of two branches, a national executive and a national judiciary, with representation based on free population or taxes paid.[^s4][^s6] Smaller states rallied behind William Paterson's New Jersey Plan, which kept one vote per state.[^s6] By mid-June the delegates had accepted that they were writing a wholly new constitution rather than amending the Articles.[^s1]

The quarrel over representation nearly broke up the meeting. A grand committee with one member from each state produced what is known as the Great or Connecticut Compromise, credited to Roger Sherman and Oliver Ellsworth: seats in the House by population, two senators for every state.[^s6][^s5] It passed on July 16 by a margin of one state.[^s5]

Slavery ran through the debates. Madison told the convention on June 30 that the deepest division lay between states that held enslaved people and those that did not.[^s7] Counting enslaved people at three-fifths for representation, proposed on June 11 and adopted on July 12, enlarged slave states' seats in the House and later their weight in the electoral college.[^s7][^s6] In August, New England and Deep South delegates struck a bargain that barred Congress from banning the Atlantic slave trade before 1808 in return for protecting New England's interest in navigation laws; Southern delegates also dropped a demand for a two-thirds majority on commercial laws, and the North accepted a fugitive slave clause.[^s7][^s6] The delegates avoided the word "slave," writing "other persons" instead.[^s7]

After a late-July recess, a five-member Committee of Detail turned the resolutions into a draft with enumerated powers, a committee chaired by David Brearley designed the electoral college and four-year presidency, and Gouverneur Morris's Committee of Style wrote the final text, opening with "We the People of the United States."[^s6] On September 17, 1787, thirty-eight delegates signed thirty-nine names, George Read signing for the absent John Dickinson; Elbridge Gerry, Edmund Randolph and George Mason refused.[^s9] Benjamin Franklin, who urged delegates to accept the document despite its faults, remarked that the sun carved on Washington's chair was "a rising and not a setting sun."[^s9]

## Aftermath and legacy

The convention sent its work to Congress in New York, which on September 28 unanimously resolved to forward the Constitution to the states for ratifying conventions. Delaware ratified first, on December 7, 1787, and New Hampshire became the ninth state on June 21, 1788, enough for the Constitution to take effect.[^s10] By vote of the delegates, the convention's official journals were deposited with Washington.[^s9]

The National Archives describes the convention's work as a model of cooperative statesmanship and the art of compromise.[^s1] Its compromises also built protections for slavery into the national framework that were removed only after the Civil War by the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what the delegates did but not on what their choices about slavery meant. Law professor and historian Paul Finkelman argues that the three-fifths clause, the slave-trade clause and the fugitive slave clause, together with indirect protections, made the Constitution a proslavery compact in which the North gave up little.[^s7] Sean Wilentz, in *No Property in Man* (2018), argues that the framers rewarded slaveholders but refused to write the principle of property in people into the text, leaving an opening that antislavery politicians later used.[^s8] Reviewing Wilentz, Nicholas Guyatt notes that the scholar Mary Sarah Bilder doubts Madison actually said in 1787 that it would be wrong to admit "the idea that there could be property in men," suspecting he added the line to his notes later; the quotation should therefore be treated with caution.[^s8]

## Connections

The convention **occurred in** the Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, and was **led by** George Washington as its elected president.[^s1][^s3] It **led to** the United States Constitution and to the state-by-state **ratification** that put it into effect in 1788.[^s1][^s10] Its decisions **affected** slavery in the United States by protecting the slave trade until 1808 and strengthening slaveholders' power in Congress.[^s7] Understanding it **requires** the Articles of Confederation, the weak first framework it was called to revise.[^s2]
