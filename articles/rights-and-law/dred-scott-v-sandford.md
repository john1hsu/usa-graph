# Dred Scott v. Sandford

*Dred Scott v. Sandford* is the U.S. Supreme Court decision of March 6, 1857, in which an enslaved man, Dred Scott, lost an eleven-year legal fight for his family's freedom. In the opinion delivered by Chief Justice Roger B. Taney, the Court held that people of African descent were not citizens of the United States and that Congress had no power to ban slavery from a federal territory.[^s1][^s2] The ruling inflamed the national quarrel over slavery and moved the country closer to civil war.[^s1][^s5] The defendant's name was actually John F. A. Sanford; the official record misspells it "Sandford," and the case is known by that spelling.[^s6][^s7]

## Background

Dred Scott was born into slavery in Virginia, around 1800 according to the State Historical Society of Missouri (some accounts give 1795), and was brought to St. Louis by the Blow family in 1830.[^s3][^s7] Peter Blow sold him to Dr. John Emerson, an army surgeon, who took Scott to Fort Armstrong in the free state of Illinois in 1833 and then, in 1836, to Fort Snelling in the Wisconsin Territory (now Minnesota), where the Missouri Compromise prohibited slavery. At Fort Snelling Scott married Harriet Robinson, an enslaved woman; their daughter Eliza was born on a steamboat in free waters.[^s3] After Emerson died in 1843, his widow, Irene Emerson, claimed the family.[^s3]

Enslaved people in St. Louis had a long record of suing for freedom: the city's courthouse, now part of Gateway Arch National Park, saw more than 300 such freedom suits, of which the Scotts' became the best known.[^s4] On April 6, 1846, Dred and Harriet Scott each sued Irene Emerson there, arguing that residence on free soil had made them free. Their first trial failed on a technicality in 1847, but on January 12, 1850, a jury found them free. On March 22, 1852, the Missouri Supreme Court reversed, rejecting the long-standing principle of "once free, always free."[^s3][^s4]

Scott then sued in federal court against John Sanford, Irene Emerson's brother and executor of her late husband's estate. Because Sanford lived in New York, Scott invoked the federal courts' jurisdiction over suits between citizens of different states. In May 1854 Judge Robert W. Wells held that Missouri law governed and ruled for Sanford, and the Scotts appealed.[^s6]

## The decision

The Supreme Court heard argument in February 1856 and re-argument in December 1856. The majority first leaned toward a narrow ruling that Scott's status depended on Missouri law, but it broadened the decision after learning that two dissenters planned to argue that Congress could regulate slavery in the territories.[^s5] Two days before the ruling, President James Buchanan said in his inaugural address that he would "cheerfully submit" to the Court's decision.[^s5]

On March 6, 1857, the Court ruled 7 to 2 against Scott, with the justices writing separate opinions; Justices Benjamin R. Curtis and John McLean dissented.[^s6][^s5] Taney's opinion, which largely stood for the majority, held that Scott was not a citizen of Missouri within the meaning of the Constitution and so could not sue in federal court, and that people of African descent, enslaved or free, were not among the "people" the Constitution described.[^s2][^s6] Describing founding-era attitudes, Taney wrote that Black people were regarded as having "no rights which the white man was bound to respect."[^s2] The Supreme Court Historical Society notes that only three justices clearly endorsed the view that no Black person could be a citizen.[^s5]

Taney went further. He held that the 1820 prohibition of slavery north of 36°30′ was not warranted by the Constitution and was void, reasoning that stripping a citizen of property for taking it into a territory would violate the Fifth Amendment's guarantee of due process. He ruled that Scott's status after returning to Missouri depended on Missouri law, which treated him as enslaved, and directed that the case be dismissed for want of jurisdiction.[^s2] In dissent, Curtis pointed out that free Black men had been citizens and voters in New Hampshire, Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey and North Carolina when the Articles of Confederation were ratified, and he treated the 1820 law as validly enacted.[^s2]

## Legacy

Hopes that the Court would calm sectional tensions collapsed at once.[^s5] Republicans saw the ruling as proof that the "Slave Power" reached even the Supreme Court, because it made their central aim, keeping slavery out of the territories, unconstitutional; it also undermined Stephen Douglas's doctrine of popular sovereignty.[^s7] The decision was a key issue in the 1858 Lincoln–Douglas Senate race. Lincoln warned that it and the Kansas-Nebraska Act aimed to nationalize slavery, while Douglas's answer at Freeport, that settlers could still keep slavery out in practice, angered Southern Democrats and helped split his party.[^s7] The 1860 Republican platform condemned the decision as a "dangerous political heresy," and many Northerners argued that Taney's ruling on the territories was unnecessary to the case.[^s6]

Taylor Blow, of the family that had enslaved Scott before selling him to Emerson, emancipated Dred and Harriet Scott on May 26, 1857; Dred Scott died of tuberculosis on September 17, 1858.[^s3] The decision's holdings were undone step by step: Congress banned slavery in the western territories in June 1862, the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery in 1865, the Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared Black Americans citizens, and the Fourteenth Amendment wrote birthright citizenship into the Constitution in 1868.[^s6] A statue of Dred and Harriet Scott now stands outside the Old Courthouse.[^s4]

## Connections

The opinion was **authored by** Chief Justice Roger B. Taney.[^s1] It **interprets** the United States Constitution and it **interprets** the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause as protecting property in enslaved people.[^s2] It **overturns** the Missouri Compromise's ban on slavery north of 36°30′, which is why understanding the case **requires** that 1820 law.[^s2] It **affected** the Lincoln–Douglas debates of 1858, where it was a central issue, and it **affected** the Republican Party, which made opposition to it part of its 1860 platform.[^s7][^s6] Its citizenship holding was in turn overturned by the Fourteenth Amendment, and it is closely related to *Marbury v. Madison*, the only earlier case in which the Court struck down an act of Congress.[^s6][^s5]
