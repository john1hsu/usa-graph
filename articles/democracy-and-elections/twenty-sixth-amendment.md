# Twenty-sixth Amendment

The Twenty-sixth Amendment, ratified on July 1, 1971, forbids the United States and the states from denying or abridging the right to vote of citizens eighteen or older "on account of age," and its second section gives Congress power to enforce that guarantee.[^s8] It lowered the effective voting age from 21 to 18 in every federal, state and local election, and it moved from congressional approval to ratification in about 100 days, faster than any other amendment.[^s1][^s10]

## Background

The original Constitution left voter qualifications to the states. Following British custom, most states limited the vote to men at least 21 years old, and for generations many Americans believed that age was needed to ensure voters' independence and judgment.[^s4] Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment even wrote 21 into the Constitution as the age at which a state's denial of the vote to male citizens would cost it representation.[^s4]

World War II changed the argument. When Congress lowered the minimum draft age to 18 in November 1942, the slogan "old enough to fight, old enough to vote" took hold, and Representative Jennings Randolph of West Virginia introduced the first of the eleven voting-age bills he would sponsor during his career.[^s9] Georgia lowered its voting age to 18 in 1943, but no federal proposal reached the floor of either chamber in the 1940s.[^s4] In his 1954 State of the Union address President Dwight D. Eisenhower recommended an amendment, yet the Senate fell short of the two-thirds majority it needed that year; Kentucky moved to 18 in 1955, and Alaska and Hawaii entered the Union in 1959 with voting ages of 19 and 20.[^s4]

During the Vietnam War, men as young as 18 could be drafted while few states let anyone under 21 vote, and public support grew; President Lyndon B. Johnson asked Congress for an amendment.[^s5] Student activists added pressure, and youth organizers worked through the Youth Franchise Coalition, whose members ranged from the NAACP to the Republican Ripon Society.[^s1][^s12] In 1970 Congress, extending the Voting Rights Act of 1965, lowered the voting age to 18 for all elections by statute. President Richard Nixon signed the bill on June 22, 1970, while saying he favored the 18-year-old vote but doubted Congress could enact it without an amendment.[^s5]

On December 21, 1970, in Oregon v. Mitchell, a divided Supreme Court upheld the statute for federal elections but struck it down for state and local elections; Justice Hugo Black, whose vote decided both questions, wrote that the Constitution reserves to the states the power to set qualifications in their own elections.[^s7] States now faced keeping two sets of registration books and running separate election systems before the 1972 presidential race.[^s3][^s5]

## What it says

Section 1 protects citizens eighteen or older against denial or abridgment of the vote by the United States or any state on account of age; Section 2 authorizes Congress to enforce it by appropriate legislation.[^s8] In the March 1971 debates, House Judiciary chairman Emanuel Celler said the protected vote covered primary, special and general elections alike.[^s6]

Senator Randolph's Senate Joint Resolution 7 was introduced on January 21, 1971.[^s1] The Senate passed it unanimously on March 10, and on March 23 the House, after approving its own version 401 to 19, passed the identical Senate resolution and sent the amendment to the states.[^s9][^s6] Several states acted on June 30; the National Archives counts North Carolina, which completed its action on July 1, as the 38th.[^s10] Forty-three states eventually ratified.[^s10]

## Legacy

On July 5, 1971, General Services Administrator Robert Kunzig certified the amendment in the White House East Room; Nixon and three 18-year-old students from the choral group Young Americans in Concert then signed as witnesses.[^s11][^s10] The President has no constitutional role in the amendment process, so Nixon's signature was ceremonial.[^s2] The Census Bureau estimated in December 1971 that about 11 million citizens aged 18 to 20 would be newly eligible to vote for president in 1972, part of roughly 25 million first-time eligible voters under 25.[^s13] Congress authorized the Justice Department to enforce the amendment in federal court and made denial of its rights a crime.[^s6]

The Supreme Court has never issued a full opinion construing the amendment, so its reach has been worked out in lower courts.[^s8] In the early 1970s state and federal courts struck down rules that made unmarried 18-year-olds living away from home register at their parents' address or made college students take extra steps to register where they lived, and in Symm v. United States (1979) the Court summarily affirmed a ruling against a Texas registrar's residency questionnaire aimed at students.[^s8]
## Debates and interpretations

Accounts differ on why the amendment passed so quickly. Constitution Annotated stresses the practical burden that Oregon v. Mitchell placed on states facing dual registration systems.[^s3] The National WWII Museum traces it to the World War II link between conscription and citizenship.[^s9] Election lawyer Yael Bromberg emphasizes youth organizers and the civil rights movement's "Second Reconstruction."[^s12]

Its present-day scope is also debated. Some federal courts require proof of discriminatory intent, and in 2020 the Fifth Circuit upheld a Texas law giving no-excuse mail voting only to voters 65 and older, finding it left younger voters no worse off than before.[^s8] Bromberg argues that this ties the amendment to the voting methods of 1971 and that it should be read, like the Fifteenth and Nineteenth Amendments it was modeled on, to forbid intentional and disparate burdens on young voters, such as rejecting campus addresses or removing campus polling places.[^s12]

## Connections

The amendment amends the United States Constitution and was proposed by the United States Congress, which passed Senate Joint Resolution 7 in March 1971.[^s1][^s6] It protects the right to vote against age discrimination for adults eighteen and over.[^s8] It was introduced by Jennings Randolph, who had first proposed the idea in 1942.[^s1][^s9] It was shaped by Oregon v. Mitchell and by the Vietnam War draft.[^s5] Its wording follows the Fifteenth and Nineteenth Amendments, and the statute it replaced extended the Voting Rights Act of 1965.[^s12][^s3]
