# Yellowstone National Park

Yellowstone National Park, established in 1872, was the first federally protected national park.[^s13] It covers about 3,472 square miles (2,221,766 acres), roughly 96 percent in Wyoming, 3 percent in Montana and 1 percent in Idaho, ranging from 5,282 feet at Reese Creek to 11,358 feet on Eagle Peak, according to the park's nonprofit partner Yellowstone Forever.[^s8] Its high plateau sits on one of the world's largest active volcanic systems: the latest of three giant eruptive cycles left a caldera about 28 by 47 miles, and the heat beneath feeds more than 10,000 hydrothermal features, more than 500 of them geysers.[^s7][^s8] Its bison are the only wild herd that has roamed freely and continuously since the great Plains herds, alongside grizzly bears, gray wolves and large elk herds.[^s7]

## History

Human history here reaches back more than 11,000 years. Before the park existed, people hunted, fished, gathered plants, quarried obsidian and used thermal waters for religious and medicinal purposes; today 27 tribes have ties to its lands and resources, among them the Crow, Blackfeet, Kiowa, Nez Perce, Shoshone, Bannock and Cayuse.[^s2] The Tukudika, or Sheep Eaters, a Mountain Shoshone band, lived in the area for thousands of years, shaping bighorn-horn bows prized in trade across the Rockies and Northern Plains.[^s2][^s6] Obsidian from Obsidian Cliff traveled so far that tools made from it turn up in Hopewell mounds in Ohio built about 2,000 years ago.[^s6]

John Colter became the first known European American visitor in 1807–1808, and organized expeditions followed in 1869, 1870 and 1871.[^s5] Ferdinand Hayden's 1871 government survey, with Thomas Moran's paintings and William Henry Jackson's photographs, gave Congress vivid images of the region, while Northern Pacific Railroad financier Jay Cooke, anticipating tourist traffic, backed the campaign.[^s13][^s1] Bills were introduced in both houses on December 18, 1871; the Senate passed S. 392 on January 30, 1872, and the House approved it 115 to 65 on February 27.[^s14] Grant signed it on March 1, 1872.[^s14] The act withdrew the land from settlement, occupancy or sale, dedicated it as a "public park or pleasuring-ground for the benefit and enjoyment of the people," and placed it under the Secretary of the Interior with orders to protect its timber, minerals and natural wonders and prevent the wanton destruction of fish and game.[^s13]

The law came with little money. Nathaniel Langford served as an unpaid first superintendent from 1872 to 1877, and in 1886, after Congress refused to fund administration, the U.S. Army took charge.[^s4] In the summer of 1877 about 800 Nez Perce (Nimiipuu) men, women and children with nearly 2,000 horses, fleeing the Army, crossed the park in 13 days; they encountered about 25 tourists, two of whom were killed, before most of the band was stopped near the Bears Paw Mountains in Montana that October.[^s9] After soldiers caught the poacher Ed Howell killing bison in 1894, Congress within two months passed the National Park Protection Act, or Lacey Act, which banned hunting in the park.[^s4][^s11] Market hunting had already cut the park's wild bison to about two dozen by the late 1880s, and in 1902 managers bought 21 more from private owners to rebuild the herd.[^s11] The National Park Service, created in 1916, took over in 1918.[^s4]

The park was not empty land. The military was already moving tribes onto reservations, early superintendents discouraged Native people from hunting and gathering there, and the Tukudika were dispersed under the 1868 Fort Bridger Treaty to the Wind River and Fort Hall reservations.[^s2] The second superintendent, Philetus Norris, promoted the idea that Native people had avoided the area for fear of the geysers—a claim the park itself now calls a myth.[^s4] More than 1,900 archeological sites have been recorded although only about 3 percent of the park has been professionally surveyed, and Kiowa, Crow and Shoshone traditions describe its hot springs and geysers in origin stories and as homes of benevolent spirits.[^s3][^s6]

## Significance

Yellowstone set the template for a national system: about twenty years later Congress created Sequoia, Yosemite and General Grant national parks, and in 1978 Yellowstone became the first U.S. World Heritage Site.[^s1][^s7] Its management has also mirrored changing ideas about nature. Predator control killed at least 136 wolves in the park between 1914 and 1926, when the last pack was destroyed; after years of lawsuits and a 1994 decision that drew more than 160,000 public comments, 31 wolves captured in Canada were released in 1995 and 1996.[^s10] Since 2019 the park has transferred bison to the Fort Peck Assiniboine and Sioux Tribes, who share them with other tribes through the InterTribal Buffalo Council.[^s11]

How the park's founding is remembered has changed too. For generations rangers and conservationists told how, at a campfire near Madison Junction on September 19, 1870, Cornelius Hedges proposed a national park—an account Langford published only in 1905, while Hedges's own diary recorded nothing unusual that night.[^s12] Historian Aubrey Haines challenged the story in the 1960s and historians now treat it as more legend than fact; Paul Schullery and Lee Whittlesey argue the park arose from a mix of social, scientific, economic and aesthetic forces rather than a few heroic founders.[^s12][^s15] Other historians stress different forces: Richard Sellars emphasizes tourism and railroad interests over conservation, and Mark David Spence ties the founding of national parks to the removal of Native peoples.[^s1]

## Connections

The park was created by the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act of 1872 (`establishedBy`) and lies in Wyoming, Montana and Idaho (`locatedIn`).[^s13][^s8] Poaching inside its borders led directly to the National Park Protection Act of 1894 (`ledTo`), and its creation helped push the Tukudika off lands they had long used (`affected`).[^s4][^s2] It is tied to the Nez Perce War of 1877, to the National Park Service and to the story of Native Americans and tribal sovereignty in the West.[^s9][^s4][^s2]
