# Mississippi River

The Mississippi River is the great central river of the United States. It begins as a small stream flowing out of Lake Itasca in northern Minnesota and runs about 2,340 miles south to the Gulf of Mexico, which federal agencies including the National Park Service now call the Gulf of America.[^s1][^s3] For thousands of years it has been a homeland, a road and a source of food for Native nations; it became a prize contested by European empires and the young United States, a decisive front in the Civil War, and today an engineered corridor for barges, drinking water and migrating birds.[^s1][^s3][^s6]

## Geography

The river's watershed covers about 1.2 million square miles, roughly 40 percent of the contiguous United States, taking in all or part of 31 states and two Canadian provinces; the National Park Service ranks it the fourth-largest watershed in the world.[^s1] Geographers divide it at Cairo, Illinois, where the Ohio River joins: the Upper Basin runs from Lake Itasca to Cairo, and the Lower Valley from Cairo to the Gulf.[^s3] The Missouri is its largest tributary, supplying about 15 percent of the total flow, and the Arkansas, Red and White rivers join it in the lower valley, where the Mississippi forms almost all of Arkansas's eastern border.[^s3] Over millennia its floods laid down sediment that built natural levees along its banks and the Delta, part of the nation's largest alluvial plain.[^s3]

The river changes dramatically along its course. It is only 20 to 30 feet wide at Lake Itasca and more than 11 miles wide where it passes through Lake Winnibigoshish in Minnesota; its flow grows from about 6 cubic feet per second at the source to roughly 600,000 at New Orleans, and water takes about three months to make the whole trip, according to the National Park Service.[^s1] Published lengths differ — the Park Service gives about 2,350 miles, while noting figures of about 2,300 from the USGS and 2,320 from the EPA — because a meandering, engineered channel can be measured in several ways.[^s1][^s2]

The corridor is also a major ecosystem. The Park Service reports that about 40 percent of the nation's migratory waterfowl use it in spring and fall, that at least 260 fish species live in the river, and that more than 50 cities draw their daily water from it.[^s1]

## Human history

Native peoples lived along the river for thousands of years, fishing, farming its floodplains and trading along it.[^s1][^s3] Between about A.D. 1000 and 1350 the Mississippian culture, named for the valley, flourished across it and the Southeast. Its preeminent center, Cahokia, a few miles east of the river near present-day St. Louis, held an estimated 10,000 to 20,000 people, and its Monks Mound is the largest Indigenous earthen structure north of Mexico.[^s5] Later nations such as the Quapaw lived along the lower river in what is now eastern Arkansas.[^s3] The river's English name comes from Algonquian words: the Ojibwe of northern Minnesota called it *Messipi*, "Big River," and another rendering was traditionally translated as "Father of Waters."[^s3]

Hernando de Soto's Spanish expedition reached the river in 1541, and Louis Jolliet and Jacques Marquette traveled it for France in 1673; in the early 1700s the French founded New Orleans, St. Louis and Arkansas Post along it.[^s3] For Americans who settled west of the Appalachians, access to the river was an economic lifeline. Pinckney's Treaty with Spain, signed October 27, 1795, won American ships the right to navigate the river and duty-free passage through Spanish-held New Orleans, a provision that made frontier farms in Kentucky and Tennessee more profitable and encouraged westward expansion.[^s7]

Steamboats transformed the river in the early 1800s, making it cheaper to ship goods downriver through New Orleans than overland across the mountains, until railroads overtook river commerce from the 1870s.[^s3] Settlers who arrived by steamboat turned riverbank lands into farms and cities, displacing the Native Americans who lived there.[^s1]

In the Civil War, Confederate forces closed the river to Northern commerce. Union commanders set out to reopen it, split the Confederacy and cut its supply lines; the long campaign for Vicksburg, Mississippi, which cost more than 48,000 casualties, ended with the city's surrender on July 4, 1863.[^s6] Admiral David Porter later recalled Lincoln calling Vicksburg "the key" — a remark known from Porter's memoir rather than Lincoln's own writings.[^s6]

The Great Flood of 1927 was the river's worst modern disaster. After rains that began in August 1926, the river spent a record 153 days at flood stage and covered about 26,000 square miles in seven states, driving an estimated 931,159 people from their homes and killing 250 to 500.[^s4] To protect New Orleans, Louisiana officials had the levee at Caernarvon dynamited beginning April 29, flooding downstream communities whose residents received little of the compensation they claimed; relief shelters were segregated by race, and many Black tenant farmers left the land for Chicago, Detroit and other northern cities.[^s4] The disaster showed that levees alone could not hold the river, and the Flood Control Act of 1928 added spillways, floodways and diversion works; the federal government went on to build more than 1,600 miles of levees below Cape Girardeau, Missouri, and dams on the upper river and its tributaries.[^s3][^s4] Serious floods returned in 1973, 1993 and 2008.[^s3]

Today the upper river has 29 locks and dams, and the Park Service reports that barges move about 175 million tons of freight on it each year; dredging lets barges reach Minneapolis and St. Paul and oceangoing ships reach Baton Rouge.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

The river lies within the United States (`locatedIn`), with only its watershed extending into Canada, and it borders Arkansas along nearly that state's whole eastern edge (`borders`).[^s1][^s3] It contrasts with the Missouri River (`contrastsWith`), its longer tributary, which the USGS lists at 2,540 miles.[^s2] The river's strategic value shaped the Siege of Vicksburg (`affected`), fought to control it.[^s6] It is closely related to Cahokia, the largest Mississippian center; to Pinckney's Treaty and the Louisiana Purchase, which settled who controlled navigation; to New Orleans and Louisiana at its mouth; and to the Great Flood of 1927.[^s4][^s5][^s7]
