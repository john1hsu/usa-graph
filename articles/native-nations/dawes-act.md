# Dawes Act

The Dawes Act, formally the General Allotment Act, was the federal law signed by President Grover Cleveland on February 8, 1887, that authorized the president to break up reservation land that Native nations held in common and assign it to individual tribal members as private "allotments."[^s2][^s1] Its sponsors presented it as a path to assimilation; its main result was the loss of most of the land Native nations still held. Native-held land fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million in 1934, when the policy ended.[^s3]

## Background

Allotment — dividing land held in common into individually owned parcels — was not new in 1887. Earlier treaties had used it to reward cooperative leaders and, in the 1830s, to let some southeastern families avoid removal, though many lost those allotments to speculators' fraud.[^s5] By the late 1860s reformers had come to see private ownership as the way to turn Native people into farmers and absorb them into the national economy, while western development interests wanted the land that allotment would free for settlement, mining, ranching and forestry. Historian Emily Greenwald calls the two camps an "uneasy alliance" that carried the bill through eight years of congressional debate.[^s5]

The bill was written by Senator Henry L. Dawes of Massachusetts, and its aim was "severalty": dealing with Native Americans as individuals rather than as members of tribes.[^s1] Cleveland, who saw Native people as wards of the nation in need of a guardian, was an enthusiastic supporter and helped the bill through Congress.[^s2]

## What it says

The act let the president survey a reservation and allot it: a quarter section (160 acres) to each head of family, an eighth of a section to each single adult and orphan, and a sixteenth to other children, with grazing land allotted in double amounts.[^s1][^s4] People who did not choose within four years had land picked for them.[^s1][^s4] The United States held each allotment in trust for twenty-five years, and any sale or contract for the land before then was void.[^s1]

Section 5 let the Interior secretary negotiate to buy the reservation land left after allotment and sell farmland to settlers in tracts of no more than 160 acres, holding the money in the Treasury for the tribe.[^s1] Section 6 made allottees subject to state or territorial law and declared them, and Native people who had left their nations and adopted what the act called "civilized" habits, citizens of the United States.[^s1] Section 8 exempted the Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole, Osage, Miami and Peoria, and Sac and Fox lands in Indian Territory, the Seneca reservations in New York and a strip of Nebraska.[^s1]

## Legacy

**Land loss.** Unallotted reservation land was declared "surplus" and opened to non-Indian settlement.[^s3] Many allotments were then lost through tax foreclosures, distressed sales and unfavorable leases, and Plains reservations became a checkerboard of Indian trust land and white-owned property.[^s4] The Supreme Court smoothed the way in *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* (1903), rejecting a Kiowa challenge and holding that a treaty could not limit Congress's power over tribal property; the ruling spurred a new wave of allotment.[^s9]

**Extension and amendment.** Congress weakened the trust protections in the 1890s and early 1900s.[^s5] The 1906 Burke Act withheld citizenship until an allottee received a fee patent and gave the Interior secretary authority to judge allottees "competent," and competency rulings forced some allottees to take outright title, leaving their land open to sale.[^s7][^s5] The Five Tribes, who had petitioned to be excluded, were allotted through the Dawes Commission created in 1893 and agreements ratified under the Curtis Act of 1898, at the price of their tribal governments.[^s5][^s6][^s10][^s1] The commission processed more than 250,000 applications and approved over 101,000 before enrollment closed on March 4, 1907.[^s6]

**Ending and aftermath.** The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended allotment and kept existing allotments in trust indefinitely.[^s3] But the damage persisted: because heirs inherited undivided shares, many tracts now have hundreds of owners, one on the Lac Courte Oreilles Reservation more than 1,200, and lease income can be split into a few cents per person.[^s8] Through its Land Buy-Back Program the Interior Department paid $1.69 billion to more than 123,000 individuals and restored nearly 3 million acres in 15 states to tribal trust ownership before its funding ended in November 2022 (Interior Department, 2023).[^s11]

## Debates and interpretations

**Motives.** Many modern commentators accept that sponsors such as Dawes cared sincerely about Native welfare and believed tribal life was an obstacle to progress; other analysts argue that allotment was a thin legal cover for non-Indians who wanted Native land.[^s4] Greenwald's account combines the two, describing a policy whose purpose shifted and whose passage depended on reformers and developers needing each other.[^s5] Clara Sue Kidwell places allotment as the culmination of efforts to destroy tribes and their governments and to open Indian land to settlers and railroads.[^s10]

**Judgment.** The Miller Center calls it "a disastrous policy that robbed Native Americans of much of their land,"[^s2] and Mark Scherer counts the allotment era among the darkest chapters of federal-Indian relations.[^s4]

**Native agency.** Native nations were not only victims of the policy. The Omahas lobbied for their own allotment acts to secure title to their homelands, while the Five Tribes argued for exclusion and the Kiowa leader Lone Wolf took his nation's case to the Supreme Court.[^s5][^s9]

## Connections

The act was enacted by the United States Congress (`enactedBy`), introduced by Senator Henry L. Dawes (`introducedBy`) and signed by Grover Cleveland (`signedBy`).[^s1][^s2] It affected Native Americans across the West by stripping roughly 90 million acres from their landholding (`affected`).[^s3] It led to the Dawes Commission that allotted the Five Tribes (`ledTo`),[^s6] to the Supreme Court's plenary-power ruling in *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* (`ledTo`),[^s9] and to the fractionation of allotted land that still burdens tribes and owners (`ledTo`).[^s8] It belongs to federal Indian law (`broader`) and requires an understanding of tribal sovereignty, since allotment aimed to dissolve tribes and their governments.[^s10] Related nodes include the Burke Act, the Curtis Act and the Indian Reorganization Act that ended allotment.[^s7][^s3]
