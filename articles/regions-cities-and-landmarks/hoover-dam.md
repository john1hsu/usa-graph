# Hoover Dam

Hoover Dam is a concrete arch-gravity dam that plugs Black Canyon of the Colorado River where it forms the border between Arizona and Nevada.[^s10] Just over 726 feet tall and 1,244 feet long at its crest, it is 660 feet thick at the base and 45 feet at the top, and the dam, powerplant and related works hold about 4.4 million cubic yards of concrete.[^s1] Built for the federal Bureau of Reclamation between 1931 and 1936 to control floods and silt and to store water for irrigation, cities and hydroelectric power, it created Lake Mead, which holds more than 32 million acre-feet at full capacity.[^s10][^s1]

## History

The dam grew out of the 1922 Colorado River Compact, which split the river between an Upper and a Lower Basin, and the Boulder Canyon Project Act, which President Calvin Coolidge signed on December 21, 1928, authorizing $165 million for the project.[^s1][^s2] Its first casualties came even earlier: in 1922 two Reclamation surveyors, J. G. Tierney and Harold Connelly, drowned while studying possible sites from barges on the river.[^s4] At a September 1930 ceremony opening the project, Interior Secretary Ray Lyman Wilbur announced that it would be called Hoover Dam, honoring Herbert Hoover's part in negotiating the Compact.[^s3][^s1]

Six construction firms pooled their resources as Six Companies Inc., whose low bid of about $48.9 million was then the largest federal construction contract in U.S. history; it was awarded on March 11, 1931, and work formally began that April.[^s3][^s2] Job-seekers flocked to Las Vegas in the depths of the Depression, and employment peaked at 5,218 in a single month.[^s3][^s11] Crews first bored diversion tunnels through the canyon walls between June 1931 and November 1933, and on November 14, 1932, the river was turned around the dam site.[^s3][^s2] Conditions in the tunnels and the summer heat were brutal. When Six Companies moved tunnel workers to lower-paying jobs on August 7, 1931, the whole workforce walked out, demanding clean water, flush toilets and compliance with state mining laws; superintendent Frank Crowe refused, and the men returned with the pay cut in place, though the company sped construction of housing.[^s7] Fourteen men died of dehydration-related illness that summer, and about fifty tunnel workers later sued over what was probably carbon monoxide poisoning from truck exhaust, settling out of court in 1936.[^s8]

The workforce was overwhelmingly white by design. In May 1931 the Colored Citizens Labor and Protective Association of Las Vegas protested that none of the first thousand men hired were Black; by 1933 only 24 African Americans, under one percent of the workforce, were on the job, assigned to the hottest work in the Arizona gravel pits, barred from living in the government town of Boulder City and bused about 30 miles each way from West Las Vegas.[^s6][^s1] American Indian workers, by contrast, were among the "high scalers" who dangled on ropes from the canyon walls with jackhammers and dynamite to strip loose rock.[^s5]

Concrete placement ran from June 6, 1933, to May 29, 1935.[^s2] Because so much concrete would generate enough heat to crack as it cured, engineers poured it as interlocking columns and threaded about 582 miles of steel pipe through them, circulating river water and then refrigerated water before grouting the joints.[^s8] The dam began impounding Lake Mead on February 1, 1935; President Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicated it on September 30, 1935, and the first generator went into full operation on October 26, 1936.[^s2] In May 1933 Roosevelt's Interior Secretary, Harold Ickes, had ordered the structure renamed Boulder Dam; Hoover's reputation had collapsed during the Depression, and Ickes argued that Wilbur had improperly named it for a sitting president and that it was already known as Boulder Dam. The new name stuck in official use until a congressional resolution of April 30, 1947, made "Hoover Dam" official.[^s1][^s3]

The official death toll is 96 industrial fatalities at the site—drownings, blasts, falls and falling rock—while a commonly cited figure of 112 adds deaths before construction and from heat, pneumonia and other illness.[^s4][^s1] A bas-relief by sculptor Oskar J. W. Hansen at the dam memorializes the men who died.[^s11] Despite a persistent legend, the Bureau of Reclamation states that no one is buried in the dam: the concrete rose in five-foot-high blocks as crews packed each layer, which made an unnoticed burial virtually impossible.[^s4]

## Significance

Hoover Dam delivers water that irrigates about 2 million acres, including California's Imperial Valley, supplies cities such as Los Angeles, Phoenix and Tucson, and generates hydroelectric power for about 1.3 million people, according to the National Park Service.[^s3] Its powerplant's 17 turbines have a total capacity of 2,080 megawatts, nearly half of it bound for southern California.[^s10] Filling Lake Mead also set off more than 6,000 minor tremors in its first decade, the first recognized reservoir-induced earthquakes.[^s1] In 1985 the Interior Department designated it a National Historic Landmark.[^s1] With about seven million visitors a year, it is often described as the world's most-visited dam.[^s3][^s10]

The dam's costs also fell on the land and its earliest peoples. Rising Lake Mead submerged Pueblo Grande de Nevada, the "Lost City" in the Moapa Valley, a village of Basketmaker and Ancestral Puebloan people whom the Hopi claim as ancestors; archaeologists and Civilian Conservation Corps workers raced the water to excavate it, but much was lost.[^s9]

## Connections

The dam stands in Black Canyon of the Colorado River (`locatedIn`) and straddles the Arizona–Nevada line, so it is located in both states.[^s3][^s10] It was authorized and funded by the Boulder Canyon Project Act (`establishedBy`) and was named in honor of Herbert Hoover (`commemorates`), a name officially restored in 1947.[^s1] Its construction created Lake Mead (`ledTo`), named in 1936 for Reclamation Commissioner Elwood Mead, and the rising reservoir flooded the Lost City site of Pueblo Grande de Nevada (`affected`).[^s2][^s1][^s9] It is closely tied to the Colorado River Compact, Boulder City and the public works of the Great Depression.[^s1][^s11]
