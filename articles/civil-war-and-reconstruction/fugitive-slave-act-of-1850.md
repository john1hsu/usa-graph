# Fugitive Slave Act of 1850

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, signed by President Millard Fillmore on September 18, 1850, was the federal law that put the national government directly in the business of returning people who had escaped slavery.[^s3][^s1] Passed as one of the five statutes of the Compromise of 1850, it strengthened the original fugitive slave law of 1793, and its enforcement in Northern cities turned an abstract sectional quarrel into scenes of armed marshals, courtroom crowds and rescues.[^s9][^s2] Congress repealed it on June 28, 1864, in the middle of the Civil War.[^s6]

## Background

The Constitution required that a person bound to service who fled to another state be delivered up, and Congress put that clause into effect with the act signed on February 12, 1793.[^s4] Under the 1793 law an enslaver could prove ownership by affidavit or oral testimony before a judge or local magistrate, and anyone who hid or helped a freedom seeker owed a $500 penalty to the enslaver.[^s4][^s3] Many free states answered with personal liberty laws; George Latimer's case in 1842, for example, prompted one in Massachusetts.[^s3] In *Prigg v. Pennsylvania* the Supreme Court upheld Congress's power over fugitive cases, yet Northern resistance to enforcement continued.[^s2] Southern leaders wanted a law with federal teeth.

On January 29, 1850, Senator Henry Clay of Kentucky offered resolutions to settle the disputes between the states over slavery, including one calling for more effective return of fugitives.[^s9] After the Senate rejected Clay's package, Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois steered it through as separate bills: California's admission as a free state, territorial governments for Utah and New Mexico, a Texas boundary settlement, an end to the slave trade in Washington, D.C., and a new fugitive slave law.[^s9]

## What it says

The act created a network of federal commissioners appointed by the circuit courts, with the same power as judges to hear claims and issue certificates of removal.[^s1] Hearings were summary: a claimant could prove ownership by deposition or affidavit, the alleged fugitive's own testimony was barred, and the certificate was conclusive, shielding the claimant from interference by other courts.[^s1][^s2] There was no jury.[^s6] Commissioners earned $10 when they issued a certificate and only $5 when they refused one.[^s1][^s2]

The law also reached ordinary Northerners. Marshals who failed to execute warrants could be fined $1,000, commissioners could summon bystanders as a posse, and the statute declared that "all good citizens are hereby commanded to aid and assist."[^s1] Anyone who hid, rescued or obstructed the capture of a freedom seeker faced up to six months in prison, a fine of up to $1,000 and civil damages of $1,000 for each person lost, and marshals could hire extra force at federal expense.[^s1][^s3]

## Legacy

Northern cities became battlegrounds. In Boston a crowd rescued Shadrach Minkins in February 1851, and he reached Montreal.[^s6][^s3] Two months later Thomas Sims was seized and marched to a ship for Savannah under an escort of more than 300 local officers; he remained enslaved for more than a decade before escaping in 1863.[^s5] In 1854 Anthony Burns was arrested in Boston, a deputy marshal died in a failed rescue, and federal troops escorted Burns to a ship on June 2; his freedom was purchased in February 1855.[^s5] In 1851 alone, rescues in Boston, at Christiana in Pennsylvania and at Syracuse freed six people by force; 38 men were indicted for treason over Christiana, but only one person in the three cases was ever convicted.[^s7] Massachusetts passed another personal liberty law after the Burns case.[^s3]

For free Black communities the danger was personal. In Philadelphia a commissioner ordered Adam Gibson, seized in December 1850, sent to Maryland, where the claimant disowned him, and the former constable George Alberti was convicted in a state court in 1851 for kidnapping a free-born child.[^s7] The Canadian Encyclopedia describes the act as a concession to Southern slaveholders threatening secession that extended slavery's reach into the free states.[^s6] Thousands of freedom seekers and free Black people moved to Canada, where Black communities in Canada West grew.[^s6] The fight over enforcement also became a constitutional clash: after Joshua Glover was rescued in Milwaukee, Wisconsin claimed a right to nullify the law, and in *Ableman v. Booth* (1859) Chief Justice Roger Taney held that states could not obstruct federal enforcement.[^s2][^s7] Georgia's secession declaration later called the federal fugitive code a dead letter in the North.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

**Was the law enforced or defeated?** Stanley Campbell's *The Slave Catchers* (1970) documented 332 people claimed between 1850 and 1860 and found that 82.2 percent of alleged fugitives brought before commissioners were returned, concluding that the law was firmly enforced despite Northern hostility.[^s8] Matthew Pinsker counters that Campbell's tally mixes in kidnappings and recaptures outside the law, that his appendix shows only about 125 formal rendition hearings, and that records from New York, Boston and Philadelphia alone document about 1,250 successful escapes; he judges the act a "spectacular failure in its actual operations."[^s7]

**How many left for Canada?** The Canadian Encyclopedia reports historian Fred Landon's estimate that 15,000 to 20,000 African Americans settled in Canada between 1850 and 1860, while noting that estimates vary.[^s6] Pinsker, working from Michael Wayne's analysis of the 1861 Canadian census, suggests that perhaps 6,500 to 8,500 arrived in the 1850s and argues that economic conditions, such as the Panic of 1857, shaped Black migration as much as the law did.[^s7]
