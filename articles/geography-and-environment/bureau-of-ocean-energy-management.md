# Bureau of Ocean Energy Management

The Bureau of Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) is the Department of the Interior bureau that decides where and when the seabed of the U.S. Outer Continental Shelf is opened to energy and mineral development.[^s3] It manages almost 3.2 billion acres of submerged land, more than the nation's entire land area, stretching from 3 to 200 nautical miles offshore.[^s3][^s4] BOEM was carved out of the Minerals Management Service in the reorganization that followed the 2010 Deepwater Horizon oil spill and became a separate bureau on October 1, 2011.[^s1][^s2] In July 2026 a new secretarial order began folding it, together with its sister safety bureau, into a single Marine Minerals Administration.[^s5]

## Origins

Federal control of the offshore seabed rests on the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act of 1953, which affirmed federal jurisdiction over its minerals and directed Interior to oversee leasing, environmental review and safety; amendments in 1978 strengthened environmental safeguards, gave coastal states a larger role and reinforced the five-year leasing program.[^s4] In 1981 Secretary of the Interior James Watt set up a commission to investigate royalty underpayments, and on January 19, 1982, Secretarial Order 3071 created the Minerals Management Service (MMS) to consolidate leasing work.[^s2] Within months offshore regulation was also moved into the new agency from the Bureau of Land Management.[^s6]

That design put three missions in one agency: offering offshore resources, enforcing safety and environmental rules, and collecting royalties that averaged more than $13 billion a year in the late 2000s.[^s2] The legal scholar Peter Jan Honigsberg argued that the arrangement gave the regulator a financial stake in the industry it oversaw, noting that before the spill about 60 inspectors covered 3,795 Gulf platforms.[^s6] Inspector-general reports in 2008 documented ethical lapses and conflicts of interest, including employees accepting gifts from companies they regulated.[^s2][^s7]

The Deepwater Horizon blowout of April 20, 2010, which killed 11 workers, turned those problems into a national crisis.[^s6] On May 19, 2010, Secretary Ken Salazar signed Secretarial Order 3299, dividing the MMS into three bodies so that conflicting missions would sit in separate hands.[^s2] Order 3302 renamed the agency the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, Regulation and Enforcement on June 18, 2010, and revenue collection moved to a new Office of Natural Resources Revenue on October 1, 2010.[^s2][^s1] On October 1, 2011, Interior formally established BOEM and the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE) as independent bureaus, with Tommy Beaudreau as BOEM's director.[^s1]

## Powers and structure

BOEM handles the planning and resource side of offshore development: leasing, resource evaluation, review of exploration and development plans, renewable energy and environmental analysis under the National Environmental Policy Act.[^s1] BSEE, by contrast, issues permits for operations, inspects them and enforces safety and environmental rules.[^s1] Both bureaus were placed under Interior's Assistant Secretary for Land and Minerals Management, while revenue collection went to a different assistant secretary.[^s2] BOEM has offices in New Orleans; Camarillo, California; Anchorage; and the Washington, D.C., area.[^s3]

Its work falls into three programs. For oil and gas it runs the national leasing program, a five-year schedule of lease sales developed through drafts and public comment; in fiscal year 2024 the shelf produced about 667.5 million barrels of oil, roughly 14 percent of U.S. crude output, and companies paid about $7 billion in bonuses, rents and royalties (BOEM, FY 2024).[^s3] The Energy Policy Act of 2005 extended offshore authority to renewable energy, and BOEM issues leases and rights-of-way for offshore wind, working with tribal, state and local governments to identify wind energy areas; renewable lease sales and rents had brought in more than $5.7 billion by March 2025 (BOEM).[^s4][^s3] It also manages offshore sand and gravel: by December 2025 it had authorized more than 200 million cubic yards for about 75 beach and coastal-restoration projects in eight states (BOEM).[^s3] Environmental studies are a legal requirement underpinning all three programs.[^s3]

## Debates and interpretations

From the start, scholars questioned whether separation would be enough. The Congressional Research Service noted that a conflict of missions and a conflict of interest are different problems, and that Interior's own safety board said structural change must be accompanied by much more.[^s2] Honigsberg observed that the new bureaus still answered to assistant secretaries reporting to the same Secretary, and judged that "the jury is still out."[^s6]

The question returned in 2026. Interior announced on April 3 that BOEM and BSEE would be merged, and Order 3451 of July 10 vested both bureaus' functions in a Marine Minerals Administration, keeping existing regulations in force and giving the new director nine months to finalize the structure.[^s7][^s5] The order cited reduced duplication, better coordination and the administration's energy and critical-minerals priorities, and stated that the spill's root causes lay mainly in operators' failures; the National Ocean Industries Association, an offshore industry group, said two agencies administering one law caused inconsistencies and delays.[^s5][^s7] Environmental groups argued the split had been made because regulators were too close to industry, and Democratic ranking members of the Senate and House energy committees asked the Government Accountability Office to review the merger, warning of "collapsing the firewall" between development and safety oversight.[^s7][^s8]

## Connections

BOEM is part of the Department of the Interior, and its land base and core duties come from the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act as amended in 1978 and 2005.[^s3][^s4] It was established through Secretarial Order 3299, issued in response to the Deepwater Horizon oil spill, as one of three successors to the Minerals Management Service alongside BSEE.[^s2][^s1] Tommy Beaudreau led it as its first director.[^s1] Much of its oil and gas work centres on the Gulf of Mexico, and its functions are now being transferred to the Marine Minerals Administration.[^s1][^s5]
