# Stock market crash of 1929

The stock market crash of 1929 was a run of collapsing prices on the New York Stock Exchange in the last week of October 1929 and the weeks that followed. Its worst days, Black Thursday (October 24), Black Monday (October 28) and Black Tuesday (October 29), ended the great bull market of the 1920s, and by mid-November the Dow Jones Industrial Average had lost almost half its value.[^s1][^s2] It is the best-known symbol of the start of the Great Depression, though scholars still debate how much it caused.[^s1][^s4]

## Background

Through the 1920s stock prices climbed steeply. The Dow rose from 63 in August 1921 to a closing peak of 381.17 on September 3, 1929.[^s1] Much of the buying was on margin: an investor paid part of a share's price, about 10 percent according to a Federal Reserve history and 25 percent by a Hoover Library account, and borrowed the rest against the shares.[^s1][^s4] The arrangement worked only while prices kept rising.[^s2]

Officials worried about the boom without agreeing on what to do. In a letter of February 2, 1929, the Federal Reserve Board asked the regional reserve banks to deny credit to member banks that lent to speculators. In August it let the New York Fed raise its discount rate to 6 percent, which under the gold standard pushed central banks abroad to raise theirs too.[^s1] Not everyone saw danger. As late as mid-October 1929 the economist Irving Fisher said stock prices had reached "a permanently high plateau."[^s1]

## What happened

After the September peak, heavy selling on Wednesday, October 23, cost the market about $4 billion in value.[^s6] On Black Thursday, October 24, nearly 13 million shares changed hands.[^s5][^s6] Leading banks, among them Chase National, National City and J.P. Morgan, bought large blocks of blue-chip stocks such as U.S. Steel, and prices recovered part of the day's losses.[^s2][^s6] The next day President Herbert Hoover told reporters that the country's basic business was "on a sound and prosperous basis," pointing to steady commodity prices and rising wages while acknowledging that speculation had driven up interest rates.[^s5]

The bankers' support did not hold. On Monday, October 28, the Dow fell nearly 13 percent, and on Tuesday, October 29, it fell nearly 12 percent more.[^s1] Black Tuesday's volume of over 16 million shares compares with about 3 million on a busy day, and between September 1 and November 30 the market's total value fell from about $64 billion to about $30 billion.[^s2]

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York bought government securities, lent freely through its discount window, cut its discount rate and promised banks the reserves they needed; before trading opened on October 30 it announced $100 million in purchases.[^s1][^s6] A Federal Reserve history credits these steps with keeping banks open, though the Board in Washington complained that New York had exceeded its authority.[^s1]

## Aftermath and legacy

The crash was the start of a long fall, not a single bad week. The Dow closed at 41.22 on July 8, 1932, about 89 percent below its peak, and did not return to its 1929 high until November 23, 1954.[^s1] The crash cut consumer and business spending on big-ticket items and led to layoffs, deepening a slowdown that had begun in the summer of 1929. OpenStax notes that over nine in ten banks had invested in the market, and the thousands of bank closings that followed spread the damage to depositors who had never bought a share.[^s1][^s2]

The crash also changed how Washington regulates Wall Street. On March 2, 1932, the Senate authorized its Committee on Banking and Currency to investigate stock exchange practices. Under chief counsel Ferdinand Pecora, hired in early 1933, it questioned National City Bank chairman Charles Mitchell, who admitted selling stock to a relative at a loss to avoid income taxes and soon resigned.[^s3] The hearings built support for the Banking Act of 1933 (Glass-Steagall), passed on June 16, 1933, which separated commercial from investment banking and created federal deposit insurance, and for the Securities Exchange Act, which President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed on June 6, 1934.[^s3]

Popular memory pictures ruined investors leaping from Wall Street windows, but the business historian Robert Sobel noted that suicide rates fell in this period, and reports name only a handful of such deaths in New York.[^s4][^s6] Nor did most Americans own stock: estimates put stockholders at about 3 million in a nation of roughly 120 million, and OpenStax says only about 10 percent of households held stock.[^s4][^s6][^s2]

## Debates and interpretations

Scholars agree on the sequence of events but not on its causes or weight. A Federal Reserve history treats the late-1920s market as a speculative bubble fed by borrowed money, while cautioning that bubbles are hard to detect and deflate.[^s1] Thomas F. Schwartz of the Hoover Presidential Library counters that most stocks traded at 14 to 19 times earnings, with profits growing faster than prices, and that economists still disagree about why the market fell.[^s4]

On the link to the Depression, the Federal Reserve history holds that the crash deepened an existing contraction but that its effects faded within months and recovery looked likely by fall 1930; problems elsewhere in the financial system then turned recession into depression. It also reports that Allan Meltzer and others see the crash as a symptom of the 1929 downturn rather than a contributing cause.[^s1] OpenStax, by contrast, sets the crash within a wider pattern of weaknesses, including easy credit, Federal Reserve policy, international war debts and a highly unequal distribution of income, and stresses how panic, once started, drove prices down further.[^s2]

## Connections

- The crash took place on the New York Stock Exchange in **New York City**.[^s2]
- It occurred early in the **Great Depression**, deepening a contraction already under way.[^s1]
- The **Federal Reserve System** tried to curb speculative lending before the crash and lent heavily to contain it.[^s1]
- **Herbert Hoover** called the nation's business sound the day after Black Thursday.[^s5]
- It led to the Senate's **Pecora investigation**, which helped bring about the **Banking Act of 1933** and the **Securities Exchange Act of 1934**.[^s3]
