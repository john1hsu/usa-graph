# Immigration to the United States

Immigration to the United States is the arrival and settlement of people from other countries, and the body of law that decided who could enter, who could stay and who could become a citizen. The Library of Congress frames this history broadly: some newcomers sought a better life, some fled oppression, and some, like enslaved Africans, were moved against their will.[^s13] The numbers have swung widely. Foreign-born residents were 9.7 percent of the population in 1850, peaked at 14.8 percent in 1890, fell to a record low of 4.7 percent in 1970, and reached a record 50.2 million people, again 14.8 percent, in 2024 (Census Bureau).[^s1][^s2]

## Origins

The federal government first set rules for citizenship, not entry. The Naturalization Act signed on March 26, 1790, let "any Alien being a free white person" become a citizen after two years in the country, if a court found them of good character and they swore to support the Constitution.[^s3] That racial limit shaped American citizenship for generations. The census began asking where people were born only in 1850, and its marshals were told to record the birthplace of each *free* inhabitant; that year, 92.2 percent of the 2.2 million foreign-born residents had come from Europe.[^s1][^s2]

Large-scale migration grew with industry. *The American Yawp* counts over 25 million immigrants arriving between 1870 and 1920. The earlier newcomers came mostly from Germany, the British Isles and Scandinavia; by 1890 most new arrivals came from southern and eastern Europe — Poles, Italians, Czechs, Hungarians, Lithuanians and others.[^s12] They built neighborhoods with mutual aid societies, churches and newspapers in dozens of languages, and their letters home drew relatives after them in chain migration. Many, especially from Italy and the Balkans, meant to return home.[^s12]

## Exclusion, gates and quotas

The first major federal restriction on entry was aimed at one people. The Chinese Exclusion Act, signed by President Chester A. Arthur on May 6, 1882, suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers for ten years and barred courts from admitting Chinese immigrants to citizenship; the National Archives calls it the first time federal law proscribed entry of an ethnic working group. Exclusion was extended in 1892, made permanent in 1902 and repealed only in 1943, when China was a wartime ally.[^s4]

Federal inspection stations followed. Ellis Island in New York Harbor opened in 1892, with Annie Moore of Ireland the first immigrant processed, and over 12 million immigrants passed through it by 1924; inspection took about three to seven hours, and some people were denied entry.[^s8][^s9] Its peak year was 1907, when about 1.2 million arrivals were examined, and the last detainees left on November 12, 1954.[^s9]

After World War I, Congress turned to numerical limits. A 1921 law capped admissions at 3 percent of each nationality counted in the 1910 census. The Immigration Act of 1924 cut that to 2 percent of each nationality in the 1890 census — before most southern and eastern Europeans had arrived — and completely excluded immigrants from Asia. The State Department's historians conclude that its most basic purpose was to preserve "the ideal of U.S. homogeneity."[^s5] Quotas applied to Europe; immigration from most of Asia was already barred. The 1952 Immigration and Nationality Act, passed over President Truman's veto, kept the national-origins system but allowed small quotas for Asian nations.[^s7]

## A new system after 1965

President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Immigration and Nationality Act on October 3, 1965, at the base of the Statue of Liberty. It abolished the national-origins quotas and gave preference to family reunification and to skilled workers, with an overall ceiling of about 320,000 admissions a year.[^s6] It also placed the first numerical limits on immigration from the Western Hemisphere, including Mexico.[^s7] Later laws built on it: the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 gave legal status to roughly 2.7 million people, and the Immigration Act of 1990 raised visa numbers, created a diversity visa category and created temporary protected status.[^s7]

The effect on origins was large. The Asian share of the foreign-born rose from 8.9 percent in 1970 to 19.3 percent in 1980; Latin America passed Europe after 1980; and by 1990 more foreign-born residents came from Asia than from Europe. In 2024, 51.4 percent were born in Latin America, 30.8 percent in Asia, 9.7 percent in Europe and 5.9 percent in Africa (Census Bureau).[^s1]

## Significance

Immigration has repeatedly remade American cities and the national population. By 1900 nearly 80 percent of Chicago's residents were foreign-born or the children of immigrants.[^s12] The law that governed it also recorded the nation's changing ideas about race and belonging, from the 1790 whites-only rule and Chinese exclusion to the 1965 law, which the National Archives says eliminated the formally racial character of immigration policy.[^s3][^s4][^s6]

A popular family story holds that Ellis Island clerks changed newcomers' names. The records say otherwise: passenger lists were written by shipping-line clerks in Europe before departure, inspectors checked arrivals against them with the help of interpreters, and federal rules barred inspectors from changing a name unless the immigrant asked or the record was wrong.[^s10][^s11] Immigrants did often change their own names, frequently within a few years of arrival, which is why naturalization papers have recorded name changes since 1906.[^s10][^s11]

## Connections

- Immigration affected **Chicago**, where immigrants and their children made up nearly 80 percent of residents by 1900.[^s12]
- It affected **New York City**, whose harbor station at Ellis Island processed over 12 million immigrants between 1892 and 1924.[^s8]
- It contrasts with **slavery in the United States**: enslaved Africans were moved against their will rather than choosing to migrate.[^s13]
- It affected **census and apportionment**: the census added a birthplace question in 1850 and has measured the foreign-born population ever since.[^s2]
- Related laws and places include the **Chinese Exclusion Act**, the **Immigration Act of 1924**, the **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965** and, on the West Coast, the **Angel Island Immigration Station**.[^s4][^s5][^s6]
