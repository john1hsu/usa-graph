# Declaration of Independence

The Declaration of Independence is the statement in which the Second Continental Congress, meeting in Philadelphia, announced in July 1776 that the thirteen colonies had broken their political ties with Great Britain and explained why.[^s3] Drafted mainly by Thomas Jefferson and approved on July 4, 1776, it combined a short statement of political philosophy with a long list of charges against King George III, and its single signed parchment is displayed today in the Rotunda of the National Archives Museum in Washington, D.C.[^s1]

## Background

By 1776 the colonies had been at war with British forces for more than a year, and the case for a complete break was spreading. Thomas Paine, a recent immigrant from England, published *Common Sense* in Philadelphia early that year; its plain, fiery attack on monarchy and on the idea of an island ruling a continent circulated widely.[^s4][^s3] In May, Congress urged colonies without revolutionary governments to form new ones, which historians of *The American Yawp* call a de facto first declaration of independence.[^s4] There were practical reasons as well: France would consider a formal alliance only after the colonies declared themselves independent.[^s3]

On June 7, 1776, Richard Henry Lee of Virginia moved that the colonies were free and independent states. Moderates needed time, so the vote was put off to July 1, and Congress named a committee of John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, Roger Sherman and Robert R. Livingston to prepare a declaration in case it passed.[^s2] Jefferson wrote the draft between June 11 and June 28, and Adams and Franklin suggested changes.[^s1] On July 1 nine colonies voted yes, Pennsylvania and South Carolina voted no, Delaware was split and New York abstained; on July 2 twelve colonies voted for independence, with New York still abstaining.[^s2]

## Drafting, debate and signing

Congress then spent July 2 to 4 revising Jefferson's text.[^s2] It cut a passage that blamed the king for the transatlantic slave trade. In his notes of the debate, Jefferson wrote that the clause was struck "in complaisance to" South Carolina and Georgia, which wanted the trade to continue, and that Northern delegates were uneasy because Northern merchants had carried enslaved people to others.[^s5][^s4] Jefferson himself, who called slavery a moral depravity, held people in slavery throughout his adult life and profited from their labour.[^s9]

Congress approved the declaration on July 4 and ordered it printed; John Dunlap ran off about 200 broadsides, fewer than 30 of which survive.[^s2] The printed copies, sent to state assemblies and army commanders, carried only the names of John Hancock as president and Charles Thomson as secretary.[^s1] Colonel John Nixon read the text aloud to a crowd in the State House Yard on July 8.[^s2] On July 19 Congress ordered a parchment copy under a new title for members to sign; Hancock signed on August 2, as did most likely 50 of the eventual 56 signers.[^s1][^s2] Others signed later, Livingston never did, and delegates including John Dickinson and John Jay declined on principle.[^s1][^s6]

## What it says

The text opens by explaining why a people that separates from another owes the world its reasons. It then states as self-evident truths that all men are created equal, that they hold rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness, and that governments draw their just power from the consent of the governed and may be altered or abolished when they destroy those rights.[^s1] Its preamble rests on natural law rather than on the rights of Englishmen, though neither its ideas nor its grievances were new; they summed up a decade of colonial resistance.[^s4]

Most of the document is a list of about 27 charges against the king, followed by an appeal to the British people and a conclusion declaring the colonies free and independent states.[^s1] One charge accused the king of inciting what it called "the merciless Indian Savages" against frontier settlers.[^s1] That language reflected the war's realities: the Shawnee, Creek, Cherokee and Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) nations largely sided with Britain, hoping to check settlement west of the Appalachians, and *The American Yawp* concludes that American independence marked the beginning of the end of much remaining Native independence.[^s4] Black Americans also weighed the promise of freedom: after Virginia's royal governor, Lord Dunmore, offered freedom to enslaved people who joined British forces, some 500 to 1,000 did so, and tens of thousands more fled their enslavers during the war.[^s4]

## Legacy

Abroad, the Declaration let friendly governments recognise the new nation. France did so formally in the 1778 Treaty of Alliance, the Netherlands followed in 1782, and Britain acknowledged American sovereignty in the 1783 Treaty of Paris.[^s3] At home, Congress first printed the signers' names in January 1777.[^s2]

Its words became a standard that later Americans held the nation to. In July 1848, more than 300 people met at Seneca Falls, New York, where a Declaration of Sentiments modelled on Jefferson's text proclaimed that "all men and women are created equal."[^s7] On July 5, 1852, Frederick Douglass told some 600 people at Corinthian Hall in Rochester, New York, that celebrating independence while millions remained enslaved was the height of hypocrisy, and that the holiday belonged to his listeners, not to him.[^s8] The revolutionary language of equality also helped shape a generation of free and enslaved Black Americans who pushed the antislavery cause forward.[^s4]

## Connections

The Declaration was **authored by** Thomas Jefferson, working for a committee that also included Adams and Franklin, and **signed by** John Hancock as president of Congress, first among the 56 signers.[^s1] It was **influenced by** Paine's *Common Sense*, which helped turn public opinion toward a complete break with Britain.[^s4] It **led to** the 1778 Treaty of Alliance with France, which French officials would consider only after independence was declared.[^s3] It grew out of Lee's resolution of June 7, 1776, and its approval date became the national holiday of Independence Day; its omissions and charges tie it to the histories of slavery, of Native nations in the Revolutionary War, and of later movements for women's rights and abolition.[^s2][^s4][^s7][^s8]
