# Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854

The Kansas–Nebraska Act, signed by President Franklin Pierce on May 30, 1854, organized the land west of Missouri and Iowa into the territories of Kansas and Nebraska, divided at the 40th parallel.[^s5][^s6] It let the settlers of each territory decide whether to allow slavery, a principle its sponsor, Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois, called popular sovereignty, and it declared void the Missouri Compromise's 1820 ban on slavery north of latitude 36°30′.[^s1] Few laws did more to break apart the party system and push the nation toward civil war.[^s1][^s2]

## Background

The region was not empty. It was the homeland of nations such as the Kanza (Kaw), whose lands earlier treaties had already cut from about 20 million acres to 256,000, and federal removal policy had resettled eastern Native nations there in what was then called Indian country.[^s8] By midcentury, farmers wanting land titles and promoters of a transcontinental railroad pressed to open it to white settlement.[^s5][^s6] Douglas, chair of the Senate Committee on Territories, wanted a northern rail route through the Platte Valley, and History Nebraska notes that no one was interested in building a railroad through unorganized Indian country.[^s4][^s2] Earlier Nebraska bills stalled because southern senators would not create another free territory under the Missouri Compromise.[^s4][^s5]

## What it says

Douglas introduced his bill on January 4, 1854.[^s2] Senator David Atchison of Missouri and other southerners demanded an explicit repeal of the 36°30′ line, and Douglas agreed; he also split the land into two territories.[^s2][^s5] The final act declared the 1820 restriction "inoperative and void" and said its intent was to leave the people of each territory free to shape their own domestic institutions.[^s1] It extended the federal fugitive slave laws of 1793 and 1850 to the territories and limited voting in the first elections to free white men over 21.[^s1] A proviso promised that nothing in it would impair Indian rights to person or property until extinguished by treaty.[^s1]

Opposition was fierce. Ohio senator Salmon Chase called the bill "a gross violation of a sacred pledge."[^s2] The Senate passed it 37 to 14 at 5 a.m. on March 4; the House followed on May 22 by 113 to 100, with northern Democrats split evenly, 44 to 44.[^s2][^s6]

## Legacy

Because Kansas bordered slaveholding Missouri, proslavery and antislavery settlers raced to control its vote, and contested elections and armed clashes earned the territory the name "Bleeding Kansas."[^s3][^s1] The Native promise proved hollow: between March and June 1854, Indian Commissioner George Manypenny pressed 11 nations in Kansas to cede nearly 13 million acres, and the Potawatomi refused to give up their communal lands.[^s7] Settlers squatted on reserved lands, including the Kanza reservation, and the Kaw were forced out of Kansas to present-day Oklahoma in 1873.[^s8] Manypenny's push for individual allotments foreshadowed the Dawes Act of 1887.[^s7]

Politically, the act shattered what remained of the Whig coalition.[^s2] Anti-Nebraska meetings across the North gave rise to the Republican Party, and the American Battlefield Trust counts 66 of the 91 House seats Democrats had held lost in the 1854–55 elections.[^s4][^s6] On October 16, 1854, at Peoria, Abraham Lincoln attacked the repeal, saying he hated it because of "the monstrous injustice of slavery itself"; the speech revived his political career.[^s9] Kansas entered the Union as a state in 1861 and Nebraska in 1867.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

**Why did Douglas do it?** Accounts centred on western development stress the railroad. History Nebraska presents the need for a transcontinental line as the main incentive, and Harl Dalstrom summarizes historian James Malin's view that Douglas saw the Mississippi Valley as the pivot of continental growth and popular sovereignty as a rational development policy.[^s4][^s5] Others emphasize party politics: the American Battlefield Trust argues that Douglas offered a western program to unite a fractious Democratic Party under Pierce and put party preservation first.[^s6]

**Was popular sovereignty neutral?** Supporters framed it as letting the people decide.[^s3] The American Battlefield Trust notes that the principle took no side on slavery in itself but that repealing the 1820 line made the act a gain for the South, while Lincoln objected both to its direct effect of letting slavery into Kansas and Nebraska and to the principle it set for the future.[^s6][^s9]
