# Great Recession

The Great Recession was the U.S. economic downturn that the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) dates from December 2007 to June 2009.[^s4] At 18 months it was the longest recession since World War II, and real gross domestic product fell 4.3 percent from peak to trough, the steepest drop of the postwar era (Federal Reserve data as of 2013).[^s4][^s1] It grew out of a collapse in house prices and a financial crisis that the Government Publishing Office, introducing the official inquiry report, calls the most significant since the Great Depression.[^s2][^s10]

## Background

Average U.S. home prices more than doubled between 1998 and 2006, and household mortgage debt climbed from 61 to 97 percent of GDP, according to a Federal Reserve History essay.[^s2] Much of the new lending went to "subprime" borrowers with weaker credit; lenders pooled these riskier mortgages and sold them to investors as securities, which widened access to credit and pushed prices higher.[^s2] *The American Yawp* also points to deregulation, including the partial repeal of the 1933 Glass-Steagall Act's separation of commercial and investment banking.[^s5]

## What happened

After housing peaked in 2006–07, mortgage losses rose, and in August 2007 strains appeared in short-term funding markets.[^s2] The recession began that December.[^s4] In March 2008 the Federal Reserve Bank of New York financed JPMorgan Chase's takeover of the investment bank Bear Stearns.[^s15] The acute panic came in September 2008: the Federal Housing Finance Agency placed the mortgage giants Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac in conservatorship on September 7, Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on September 15, and the next day the New York Fed agreed to lend up to $85 billion to the insurer AIG.[^s15]

Two administrations and Congress responded. After the House rejected a first rescue bill on September 29, a revised version passed, and President George W. Bush signed the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act on October 3, 2008.[^s6] It created the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), letting the Treasury use up to $700 billion, including about $250 billion committed to buying stakes in banks.[^s6] The Federal Reserve cut its federal funds rate target from 5.25 percent in September 2007 to a range of 0 to 0.25 percent in December 2008 and bought about $1.75 trillion in mortgage-backed securities, agency debt and Treasury securities in its first round of asset purchases, commonly called QE1.[^s1] President Barack Obama signed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act on February 17, 2009; the Congressional Budget Office estimated it would add $787.2 billion to deficits over 2009–2019, mostly through new spending and partly through tax cuts.[^s7]

## Who was hurt

Total employment fell 5 percent during the recession, and construction and manufacturing lost jobs at their fastest annual rates of the postwar era, 13.7 and 10.0 percent (BLS, 2012).[^s3] Unemployment rose from 5.0 percent in December 2007 to 9.5 percent in June 2009 and peaked at 10.0 percent in October 2009.[^s3] Home prices fell about 30 percent on average from mid-2006 to mid-2009, and household and nonprofit net worth dropped from about $69 trillion in 2007 to $55 trillion in 2009.[^s1] The median family's net worth fell 38.8 percent between 2007 and 2010, driven above all by falling house prices (Federal Reserve, 2012).[^s14]

Losses were unequal. Using the Census Bureau's Survey of Income and Program Participation, the Pew Research Center found that from 2005 to 2009 inflation-adjusted median wealth fell 66 percent for Hispanic households, 53 percent for Black households and 16 percent for white households, leaving white households in 2009 with about 20 times the median wealth of Black households and 18 times that of Hispanic households.[^s13] Pew traced much of the gap to housing, which made up a larger share of Hispanic and Black wealth.[^s13]

## Aftermath and legacy

Growth averaged only about 2 percent in the first four years of recovery, long-term unemployment stayed high, and in mid-2011 average home prices were still more than a fifth below their early-2007 level.[^s2] When the NBER announced on September 20, 2010, that the recession had ended, it stressed that it was not judging conditions since then to be favorable.[^s4] *The American Yawp* notes that bank profits and stock prices recovered faster than jobs and wages.[^s5]

The crisis reshaped financial law. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, signed July 21, 2010, created a Financial Stability Oversight Council to watch for systemic risk, a Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the Volcker Rule limits on banks' proprietary trading, and an FDIC-run process for winding down failing giant firms.[^s8][^s2] The rescues stirred protest from both right and left: Tea Party activists criticized ties between government and Wall Street, and Occupy Wall Street protesters denounced an economy tilted toward "the one percent."[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

Congress created the ten-member, bipartisan Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission in May 2009; its final report includes 126 pages of dissenting views.[^s10] The majority concluded that the crisis was avoidable, citing "widespread failures in financial regulation," breakdowns in corporate governance, excessive borrowing and risk-taking by households and Wall Street, and policymakers who were ill prepared.[^s9]

Commissioners Keith Hennessey, Douglas Holtz-Eakin and Bill Thomas dissented jointly.[^s9] Holtz-Eakin told House lawmakers in 2011 that several causes, among them credit and housing bubbles and nontraditional mortgages, worked together in a global bust, so blame could not rest on U.S. housing policy, monetary policy or executives alone.[^s11] Commissioner Peter Wallison argued separately that federal housing policy was the essential cause, especially affordable-housing goals for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which he said left about 27 million high-risk mortgages, roughly half of all U.S. mortgages, in the system.[^s12] Federal Reserve historians note a further debate over whether the Fed's low interest rates after 2001 fed the housing boom; some analysts think they explain only a small part of it.[^s2]

## Connections

The Great Recession occurred in the United States in the post–Cold War era.[^s1][^s5] It led to the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act and TARP,[^s6] the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act,[^s1][^s7] the Dodd-Frank Act[^s2][^s8] and the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission.[^s10] The Federal Reserve System was central to the response.[^s1] It hit the wealth of African Americans and Hispanic and Latino Americans hardest,[^s13] and official accounts measure it against the Great Depression.[^s10]
