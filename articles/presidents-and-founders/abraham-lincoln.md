# Abraham Lincoln

Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865) was the 16th president of the United States. Born in Kentucky and raised on the frontier in Indiana and Illinois, he became a lawyer and politician in Springfield, won the presidency in 1860, and led the Union through the Civil War until he was shot at Ford's Theatre in April 1865, days after the war's main Confederate army surrendered.[^s1][^s9] His presidency ended slavery's legal protection in the rebelling states and set the country on the path to the Thirteenth Amendment, yet how much credit he deserves for emancipation, and how to weigh his views on race, remain live questions among historians.[^s10][^s11]

## Life and work

Lincoln was born near Hodgenville, Kentucky, on February 12, 1809. His family moved to Indiana in 1816, where his mother, Nancy Hanks Lincoln, died in 1818, and to Illinois in 1830. He settled in New Salem in 1831, served as a militia captain in the Black Hawk War of 1832, and became the village postmaster.[^s1] He won a seat in the Illinois legislature in 1834 and was reelected three times, moved to Springfield in 1837, and married Mary Todd there on November 4, 1842.[^s1]

Elected to the U.S. House of Representatives as a Whig in 1846, Lincoln served a single term. On December 22, 1847, he introduced the Spot Resolutions questioning whether the "spot" where the Mexican War began was really American soil; Congress never acted on them, and an Illinois paper mocked him as "spotty Lincoln."[^s3] In 1858 he ran for the U.S. Senate, gave his "House Divided" speech on June 16, and lost to Stephen A. Douglas.[^s1]

The Republican Party nominated Lincoln for president on May 18, 1860, on a platform opposing slavery's spread into the western territories.[^s1][^s12] In a four-way race against Douglas, John C. Breckinridge and John Bell, he won 39.8 percent of the popular vote but 180 of 303 electoral votes by carrying every free state, and he did not appear on many Southern ballots.[^s2][^s12] Seven Southern states had seceded by the time he left Springfield, and within little more than a month of his March 4, 1861, inauguration, Confederate guns opened the war at Fort Sumter.[^s4] His first inaugural address had appealed to the South that "We are not enemies, but friends."[^s4]

Lincoln first fought a limited war, wary of driving slaveholding border states such as Kentucky out of the Union.[^s5] Enslaved people forced the issue: from May 1861 escapees reached Union lines, where General Benjamin Butler declared them "contraband of war," and Congress's confiscation acts of 1861 and 1862 began to strip their enslavers' claims. After the Battle of Antietam in September 1862 Lincoln announced emancipation, and the Emancipation Proclamation took effect on January 1, 1863, in areas in rebellion while exempting the border states. It also opened the army to Black men, more than 180,000 of whom served.[^s5] Lincoln had earlier favored gradual, compensated emancipation and the colonization of freed people abroad, and moved away from those plans as the war went on.[^s11]

The war also reached the Dakota in Minnesota. After years of broken treaty promises and late annuity payments left some Dakota facing starvation, factions attacked white settlements in August 1862. Military tribunals condemned more than 300 Dakota men to death; Lincoln commuted all but 39 sentences, one more man was reprieved, and 38 were hanged at Mankato on December 26, 1862, the largest mass execution in U.S. history.[^s6]

Lincoln delivered the Gettysburg Address on November 19, 1863; the Library of Congress holds two copies in his hand.[^s7] He was reelected on November 8, 1864, and gave his second inaugural address on March 4, 1865.[^s1] On April 14, 1865, John Wilkes Booth, an actor who believed he was aiding the South, shot him at Ford's Theatre; Lincoln died at the Petersen House at 7:22 the next morning, the first U.S. president to be assassinated.[^s1][^s9]

## Legacy

His body was carried about 1,700 miles to Springfield and buried on May 4, 1865.[^s9] He is remembered above all for preserving the Union and for emancipation, but his memory has changed as Americans' views of race and slavery have changed: since the civil rights era, scholars have looked harder at his racial views and at the role enslaved people played in their own freedom.[^s10][^s11] A popular story holds that he scribbled the Gettysburg Address on an envelope aboard the train, but he worked on it for weeks, began one copy now at the Library of Congress on Executive Mansion stationery in Washington, and wrote his drafts in a steady hand unlike a jolting train's.[^s7][^s8] Dakota people and others also remember him for the Mankato executions.[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what Lincoln did but differ on how to weigh it. One view, associated with James McPherson, holds that Lincoln's leadership was the decisive force in ending slavery and that without him it might have survived the war.[^s10][^s11] A second view, developed by Ira Berlin and the Freedmen and Southern Society Project, argues that enslaved people were the main, though not the only, movers of emancipation: by fleeing and serving the Union they pushed officers, Congress and the president to act, and Lincoln, in Berlin's words, was "part of history, not above it," though once committed he enlisted Black soldiers and backed the Thirteenth Amendment.[^s11] A smaller group of critics condemns his handling of emancipation and the war as needlessly costly.[^s10] A related debate asks how his support for emancipation fit with his views on race.[^s10]

## Connections

Lincoln **held the office** of president of the United States as the 16th president.[^s1] He was earlier a **member of** the U.S. House of Representatives, serving one term as a Whig, and later a **member of** the Republican Party, which nominated him in 1860.[^s3][^s2] He **took part in** the Black Hawk War as a militia captain and led the Union through the **American Civil War**.[^s1][^s5] His presidency is also tied to the Emancipation Proclamation, the Thirteenth Amendment, the Gettysburg Address, the U.S.-Dakota War of 1862 and the Mexican War he opposed in Congress.[^s5][^s7][^s6][^s3]
