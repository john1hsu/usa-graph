# Hip hop

Hip hop is a youth culture and art form born in New York City in the early 1970s, starting with parties in the Bronx. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture describes it as a do-it-yourself culture of Black and Latino New York youth that drew on African American, Afro-Caribbean and Afro-Latino traditions, and names four core practices: MCing, DJing, graffiti and breaking (breakdancing).[^s2] Rap, the rhymed vocal style most people associate with the word, is one part of that larger culture. In 2023 the U.S. Senate called hip hop an original American art form that began in New York and spread across the country and the world.[^s1]

## Origins

The Bronx of the late 1960s and 1970s was a hard place to grow up. A National Archives history notes that New York City's economic decline brought cuts to schools, job training and the arts, alongside rising unemployment, drug addiction and homelessness, while absentee landlords let Bronx buildings decay.[^s3] Poverty, police brutality and gang violence pressed on African American, Afro-Caribbean and Latino neighborhoods; some early DJs and MCs were former gang members who turned to music and dance as an alternative to gang warfare.[^s6][^s2] The Smithsonian sums up the spirit of the culture with a phrase from Black tradition: "making a way out of no way."[^s2]

The usual starting point is August 11, 1973. That night Clive Campbell — a Jamaican-born teenager known as DJ Kool Herc — played the "Back to School Jam" that his sister Cindy Campbell organized in the recreation room of their apartment building at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue.[^s1][^s3] Herc put the same record on two turntables and switched between them to stretch the "break," the drum-heavy passage that brought dancers to the floor; an MC rhymed over the music while b-boys and b-girls danced.[^s1][^s3] This extended-break method is known as the "merry-go-round" technique, and he brought friends such as Coke La Rock to rhyme at his parties.[^s5]

The 1973 date is a convenient anniversary rather than a single act of invention. The Library of Congress says hip hop is "said to have begun" at Herc's party, and points to earlier spoken-word and funk records — Pigmeat Markham's "Here Comes the Judge" (1968) and Gil Scott-Heron's "The Revolution Will Not Be Televised" (1971) — as forerunners.[^s4] Scholars also trace rapping to older African American oral traditions such as boasting, toasting, signifyin' and playing the dozens, and to Jamaican toasting.[^s6][^s2] Many people built the culture: Afrika Bambaataa founded the Zulu Nation as an alternative to gang life, Grandmaster Flash refined the DJ's turntable techniques, and DJs such as Grand Wizzard Theodore and DJ Hollywood, crews like the Cold Crush Brothers and early mixed-gender groups like Master Don and the Committee pushed it forward.[^s3][^s5][^s6]

## Meaning and practice

Early hip hop lived in parks, community rooms, clubs and streets, advertised by homemade flyers; DJs came first, and MCs at first played a supporting role.[^s5][^s3] Battles between crews — such as the 1981 contest between the Cold Crush Brothers and the Fantastic Five at Harlem World — were showcases of skill.[^s5] Records followed. The Fatback Band's "King Tim III (Personality Jock)" came out in 1979, about two months before the Sugarhill Gang's "Rapper's Delight," which became the first hip hop song on the Billboard Hot 100; that same year Kurtis Blow became the first rapper signed to a major label.[^s7][^s3] "The Message" (1982) by Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five set a socially conscious direction, and films such as *Wild Style* and *Beat Street* spread the culture nationally.[^s6][^s3] Run-DMC's *Raising Hell* (1986) became the first platinum hip hop album.[^s3]

Hip hop then developed regional voices. The Smithsonian traces West Coast hip hop to Los Angeles DJs of the late 1970s and the gangsta rap and G-funk styles of the 1980s and 1990s, notes that Miami and Houston were the first Southern cities to make a national impact before Atlanta groups such as OutKast arrived, and describes a Midwest scene flourishing from the early 1980s.[^s2] The Senate resolution adds that Southern traditions — New Orleans jazz and bounce, Mississippi blues and country music — shaped the music as it grew.[^s1]

Today hip hop is studied and preserved as American heritage. The Smithsonian issued a 129-track *Smithsonian Anthology of Hip-Hop and Rap* in 2021, and its African American history museum holds more than 300 hip hop objects.[^s2] For the 50th anniversary, the Senate designated August 11, 2023, as Hip Hop Celebration Day.[^s1]

## Connections

Hip hop originated in the Bronx, at Herc's Sedgwick Avenue party, and spread from New York City outward.[^s1] It arose among African Americans together with Afro-Caribbean and Latino youth, and it belongs to a long line of Black American music.[^s2] The Senate resolution names the blues and New Orleans jazz among the Southern traditions that later shaped it, linking hip hop to the blues and jazz articles in this graph.[^s1] Its socially conscious strand drew on 1960s and 1970s spoken word, including Gil Scott-Heron, who took his famous title from the Black Power movement.[^s4]
