# World War II and the United States

The United States fought in the Second World War as a formal belligerent from December 1941 to September 1945, but its involvement began earlier, with a policy of legal neutrality that steadily gave way to aid for Britain and its allies.[^s1] By 1945 more than 12.2 million Americans were in uniform, and the National WWII Museum counts 407,316 American service members killed and 671,278 wounded over the course of the war.[^s5] The conflict ended the Great Depression's mass unemployment, drew women and minority workers into new jobs, and also produced some of the century's starkest injustices at home, from segregated armed forces to the mass incarceration of Japanese Americans.[^s4][^s3]

## Background

When war broke out in Europe in September 1939, President Franklin D. Roosevelt declared the country neutral in law while admitting he could "not ask that every American remain neutral in thought as well."[^s1] The 1934 Johnson Act barred loans to nations that had defaulted on World War I debts, including Britain, and the 1939 Neutrality Act allowed warring nations to buy American war materiel only on a cash-and-carry basis.[^s1] On September 2, 1940, Roosevelt traded more than 50 aging destroyers to Britain for 99-year leases on bases in Newfoundland and the Caribbean.[^s1]

Roosevelt then proposed Lend-Lease, and the act Congress approved on March 11, 1941, let the President sell, lend, lease or otherwise transfer defense articles to any country whose defense he judged vital to American security.[^s9] He framed the policy as making the nation an "arsenal of democracy."[^s9] Over the course of the war the United States signed Lend-Lease agreements with more than 30 countries and provided roughly $50 billion in aid.[^s1] Across the Pacific, Washington ended its trade treaties with Japan in 1939 and embargoed oil, steel, rubber and other goods in 1940 as Japan expanded its war in Asia.[^s4]

## What happened

Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, killed about 2,400 Americans.[^s4] The next day the House voted 388 to 1 to declare war on Japan, and the Senate voted 82 to 0.[^s2][^s10] On the morning of December 11 Germany declared war on the United States, with Italy following; Congress then passed resolutions recognizing a state of war with both, by unanimous Senate votes.[^s2][^s10] In June 1942 Congress added declarations against Bulgaria, Hungary and Romania — the last formal declarations of war in American history.[^s10]

In the Pacific, the Battle of Midway on June 4–6, 1942, was a turning point: codebreakers had read enough of the Japanese naval code to show Admiral Chester Nimitz where the enemy was heading, and U.S. dive-bombers wrecked four Japanese carriers, which were then scuttled, for the loss of USS Yorktown and 307 Americans.[^s8] American forces then advanced by island hopping, bypassing strong points to seize airfields, through costly fights such as Iwo Jima and the 80-day struggle for Okinawa, while firebombing raids on Japanese cities killed some 100,000 civilians in Tokyo alone in March 1945.[^s4]

In Europe and North Africa, American combat troops landed in French Morocco in November 1942, and at Casablanca in January 1943 Roosevelt announced that the Allies would demand unconditional surrender.[^s4] On D-Day, June 6, 1944, almost 133,000 Allied troops came ashore on five Normandy beaches from some 7,000 ships and landing craft, at a cost of about 10,300 Allied casualties that day; by June 30 more than 850,000 men had landed.[^s7] Germany was conquered in May 1945, days after Hitler's suicide.[^s4] The secret Manhattan Project tested its first atomic weapon in New Mexico in July 1945; the bomb dropped on Hiroshima on August 6 killed more than 100,000 civilians, and the one dropped on Nagasaki on August 9 killed perhaps 80,000 more.[^s4] Historians still debate the motives behind the bombings; officials later justified them by citing projected casualties of an invasion of Japan.[^s4] Japan signed the Instrument of Surrender aboard USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay on September 2, 1945, with General Douglas MacArthur accepting for the Allied Powers.[^s6]

### The home front

War spending in four years doubled all previous federal spending, unemployment virtually disappeared, the top income-tax rate rose to 94 percent, and rationing covered gasoline, meat, sugar, butter and coffee.[^s4] About 20 million workers left for military service, and more than a million administrative jobs passed from men to women.[^s4] About 350,000 women joined the military, and by 1943 women made up a majority of aircraft-industry workers.[^s3] Draftees accounted for about 61 percent of the men who served from 1941 to 1945, volunteers for the rest.[^s5]

About one million African Americans served, mostly in segregated units under white officers; the Tuskegee Airmen flew more than 1,500 missions, and the armed forces were not fully integrated until 1948.[^s4] After A. Philip Randolph threatened a march on Washington in 1941, Executive Order 8802 created the Fair Employment Practices Committee to bar discrimination in defense industries, and in 1942 the *Pittsburgh Courier* launched its Double V campaign for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home.[^s3] Some 44,000 Native Americans served, including Navajo Marine code talkers in the Pacific; about 350,000 Mexican Americans served, the bracero program brought Mexican laborers north from August 1942, and in 1943 white sailors and civilians attacked young Mexican Americans in Los Angeles in the so-called Zoot Suit Riots.[^s3] Under Executive Order 9066, roughly 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry — two-thirds of them American-born — were forcibly removed and incarcerated, though none was found guilty of espionage or sabotage, while nearly 33,000 Japanese Americans served in the military.[^s3]

## Aftermath and legacy

The United States came out of the war as one of the Big Three Allied powers. Roosevelt and Churchill had set out shared war aims in the Atlantic Charter in August 1941, before the country was even at war, and the Big Three's meetings at Yalta in February 1945 and Potsdam in July 1945 — the latter attended by Roosevelt's successor, Harry S. Truman — shaped the postwar settlement.[^s11] Several wartime arrangements outlived the fighting: the bracero program ran until 1964 and issued some five million labor contracts across 24 states, and the segregated armed forces were finally integrated in 1948.[^s4] The record of the incarceration — no incarcerated Japanese American was found guilty of sabotage or espionage — later became a lasting lesson about wartime civil liberties.[^s3]
