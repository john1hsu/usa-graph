# Gullah Geechee

The Gullah Geechee are the African American people of the lower Atlantic coast, descended from West and Central Africans who were enslaved and forced to labor on the region's rice, Sea Island cotton and indigo plantations.[^s2] By convention, people in the Carolinas are called Gullah and those in Georgia and Florida Geechee.[^s1][^s3] Because their ancestors lived in large, relatively isolated communities on coastal plantations and barrier islands, they kept many African traditions in their language, foodways, crafts and worship to an unusual degree.[^s1][^s2] Their language, Gullah, is described by the National Park Service as the only African American creole language that developed in the United States.[^s1]

## History

**Origins on the rice coast.** Planters in the colonial Lowcountry sought captives from Africa's Windward Coast — Senegambia, Sierra Leone and Liberia — where rice, indigo and cotton were already grown; the region came to be called the Rice Coast. Cultural links have been traced to peoples such as the Mende and Temne, though scholars stress that no single African group produced the culture.[^s3] Georgia, founded in 1732, banned slavery in 1735 but lifted the ban in 1750, after which enslaved West Africans worked its island plantations as well.[^s3] By the Civil War, enslaved people made up about 80 percent of the Sea Islands' population, and South Carolina had outlawed teaching them to read since the early 1700s.[^s5] The origins of the names themselves are uncertain: "Gullah" may come from Angola or from peoples of the Windward Coast, and "Geechee", long used as an insult, may be linked to the Kissi people.[^s3]

Free Black communities were also part of this coast. In Spanish Florida, Fort Mose near St. Augustine, chartered in 1738, was home to people who had escaped slavery; the Park Service calls it the oldest sanctioned free Black community in what became the United States.[^s1]

**War and the promise of land.** When Union forces occupied the South Carolina Sea Islands, Northern abolitionists known as "Gideonites" arrived in March 1862 to run schools and plantations in what became the Port Royal Experiment.[^s5] In June 1862 Laura Towne and Ellen Murray opened the Penn School on St. Helena Island, soon joined by Charlotte Forten; it became the best known of the Sea Island schools and graduated its last class in 1952.[^s5] Some confiscated land was sold to freedpeople.[^s3] On January 16, 1865, after meeting with twenty Black leaders in Savannah, General William T. Sherman issued Special Field Order No. 15, reserving the Sea Islands and a strip thirty miles inland from Charleston to Florida's St. Johns River — about 400,000 acres — for Black families in forty-acre plots. Roughly 40,000 people settled there before President Andrew Johnson returned most of the land to its former owners in the fall of 1865.[^s6]

**A language recognized.** After emancipation most coastal families stayed in the region, and many islands could be reached only by boat until bridges began to be built in the early 1950s.[^s1] Outsiders often dismissed their speech; the University of North Carolina scholar Guy B. Johnson called it little more than the peasant English of two centuries earlier.[^s4] The linguist Lorenzo Dow Turner, who first heard Gullah in 1929 while teaching at South Carolina State College, spent nearly two decades recording speakers, published *Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect* in 1949, and identified some 3,600 African-derived "basket names" given to children.[^s4][^s3] A song he recorded in the 1930s from Amelia Dawley was traced in 1989 to the village of Senehun Ngola in Sierra Leone, and in 1997 Dawley's daughter Mary Moran met Bendu Jabati, who knew the same song.[^s3]

## Today

Gullah Geechee traditions remain living practice. The ring shout, a counterclockwise circle of rapid rhythmic clapping and movement related to West African communal dance, has been held after Sunday services and on weeknights since the Civil War and is kept up by groups such as Georgia's McIntosh County Shouters; during slavery, plantation praise houses were the center of worship.[^s3] Sweetgrass baskets descend from the fanner baskets used to winnow rice, and dishes such as hoppin' John and sweet potato pone carry on coastal foodways.[^s3][^s1] Community members such as Cornelia Bailey of Sapelo Island worked to share the culture with the public.[^s3]

The same coast has also been a site of loss. In the twentieth century, resorts and nature reserves took over land on islands such as Cumberland, Jekyll, Ossabaw, Sapelo and St. Simons.[^s3] Bridges and air-conditioning made the islands attractive oceanfront property, and many communities were changed by mainland influence or lost entirely to real-estate development.[^s1]

On October 12, 2006, the National Heritage Areas Act of 2006 created the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor, running from Pender County, North Carolina, to St. Johns County, Florida, and a federal commission to manage it.[^s2] The corridor reaches about 30 miles inland and includes some 80 barrier islands; the commission works with the Park Service, local groups and the four states' historic preservation offices to research, preserve and interpret the culture.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **African Americans and slavery:** The Gullah Geechee are a distinct African American community whose history begins with the enslavement of West and Central Africans on coastal plantations.[^s2]
- **Sea Islands, South Carolina and Georgia:** The culture took shape on the coastal plantations and barrier islands of the Carolinas, Georgia and northeast Florida, where most descendants still live.[^s2][^s1]
- **Port Royal Experiment and Reconstruction:** Sea Islanders were the first freedpeople served by the wartime schools and land programs of the Port Royal Experiment, including Penn School.[^s5]
- **Special Field Order No. 15 and the Freedmen's Bureau:** Sherman's order briefly settled tens of thousands of freedpeople on coastal land before Johnson's reversal returned most of it, over the objections of Freedmen's Bureau chief Oliver O. Howard.[^s6]
- **Lorenzo Dow Turner:** His fieldwork showed the African roots of the Gullah language.[^s4]
- **Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor:** The 2006 corridor is the federal effort to recognize and preserve the culture.[^s2]
