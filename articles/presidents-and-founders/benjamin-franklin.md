# Benjamin Franklin

Benjamin Franklin was a printer, writer, scientist and civic organizer who left Boston as a young man and became one of the best-known Americans of the eighteenth century: a colonial agent in London, a member of the Continental Congress who helped draft the Declaration of Independence, the new nation's chief diplomat in France, and the oldest delegate to the Constitutional Convention.[^s1][^s2] For about fifty years his household also included enslaved people, and only late in life did he become a public opponent of slavery.[^s4]

## Life and work

Franklin was born in Boston on January 17, 1706 (January 6 under the Old Style calendar).[^s2] He struck out on his own in 1723 and found work as a journeyman printer in Philadelphia, where he went on to publish *The Pennsylvania Gazette* and *Poor Richard's Almanack*.[^s1] He set up a printing partnership in 1728 and became sole proprietor in 1730.[^s2]

He was a builder of institutions. He helped organize the Library Company of Philadelphia in 1731, organized a volunteer fire company through the Junto, his discussion club, and helped establish a hospital; a 1743 effort to create an intercolonial Junto led to the American Philosophical Society, and the Academy of Philadelphia, founded in 1751, grew into the University of Pennsylvania.[^s2] He became Philadelphia's postmaster in 1737 and deputy postmaster general for the northern colonies in 1753.[^s2]

His science won him an international reputation. Letters describing electrical experiments he made between 1747 and 1750 were published in London in 1751; they argued that lightning is electrical and proposed a tall, grounded rod to protect buildings.[^s9] In June 1752 he flew a kite with a metal key in a Philadelphia thunderstorm, with his son William assisting, and described the experiment in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* on October 19, 1752.[^s7] The Royal Society gave him its Copley Medal in 1753.[^s9][^s7] He also described a more efficient stove in 1744, improved the glass armonica and sketched bifocal glasses in 1785.[^s9]

## Politics and diplomacy

On May 9, 1754, Franklin printed the "Join, or Die" woodcut of a severed snake to urge the colonies to unite against France and its Native allies; his plan of union, later called the Albany Plan, was rejected.[^s10] In 1757 he went to London as agent of the Pennsylvania Assembly.[^s2] He at first accepted the 1765 Stamp Act but turned against it once he learned of colonial opposition, and during the 1766 debate over repeal distributed a cartoon of his own design to members of Parliament.[^s10]

Returning to Philadelphia in 1775, he served in the Continental Congress and helped draft both the Declaration of Independence and the Articles of Confederation.[^s1] Congress sent him to France as a commissioner in 1776; in 1778 France recognized American independence and allied with the United States, and in 1779 he became the first American minister received by a foreign government.[^s3] He then helped negotiate the end of the war and the 1783 Treaty of Paris.[^s1] When Thomas Jefferson succeeded him in 1785, Jefferson reportedly told the French foreign minister that no one could replace Franklin.[^s3]

Home again, he was chosen president of Pennsylvania, a largely ceremonial post.[^s8] At the 1787 Constitutional Convention he was the oldest delegate and, during a heated debate over representation, reminded colleagues they were "sent here to consult, not to contend."[^s8][^s2]

## Slavery and abolition

Franklin's household held enslaved people of African descent over about fifty years. He and his wife Deborah bought a married couple, Peter and Jemima, before 1750; a manservant named King and a boy named Othello were also enslaved there, and in 1757 Franklin took Peter and King to London.[^s4][^s8] His *Pennsylvania Gazette* also ran advertisements for slave sales and for people who had escaped slavery.[^s11]

In later life his views changed. He corresponded with the Philadelphia abolitionist Anthony Benezet and in 1787 became president of the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery, backing a plan to emancipate enslaved people and educate free Black people and their children.[^s8][^s4] On February 3, 1790, he signed the society's petition asking the First Congress to act against slavery. The Senate took no action, and a House committee reported on March 5 that the Constitution barred Congress from ending the slave trade before 1808, after which the House tabled the petitions.[^s5] He died in Philadelphia on April 17, 1790, owning no enslaved people.[^s2][^s4]

## Legacy

Franklin's autobiography appeared in France shortly after his death, with British and American editions two years later, and helped fix his popular image.[^s1] Popular memory also simplifies him. He did not discover electricity, and his kite was never struck by lightning.[^s7] Nor did he formally propose the turkey as the national bird: in an unsent 1784 letter to his daughter Sarah Bache, mocking the eagle on the Society of the Cincinnati badge, he called the bald eagle "a bird of bad moral character" and praised the turkey as more respectable.[^s6]

## Connections

Franklin was a **member of** the Second Continental Congress and **participated in** the Constitutional Convention.[^s1][^s2] He **held the office** of American minister to France.[^s3] He was a **member** and president of the Pennsylvania Abolition Society and so **participated in** the early abolition movement.[^s5][^s4] He helped negotiate the Treaty of Paris and helped draft the Declaration of Independence, and his career links Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, the Albany Plan and the national symbols debated in his day.[^s1][^s6]
