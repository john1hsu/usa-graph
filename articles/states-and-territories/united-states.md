# United States

The United States of America is a federal republic in North America made up of fifty states and the District of Columbia, together with territories in the Caribbean and the Pacific. Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence on July 4, 1776, two days after voting that the "United Colonies" were free and independent states.[^s3] Britain recognized that independence in the Treaty of Paris, signed on September 3, 1783,[^s5] and the country has been governed under its present Constitution since 1789.[^s4] The 2020 census counted 331,449,281 residents.[^s1]

**The name.** "America" comes from the German mapmaker Martin Waldseemüller, who in 1507 applied it to the hemisphere's landmasses in honor of Amerigo Vespucci.[^s19] The Declaration's heading spoke of "the thirteen united States of America," with a lowercase "united"; on September 9, 1776, Congress ordered that "United States" replace "United Colonies" in official documents.[^s18] The Articles of Confederation, adopted in 1777, made the name formal: its first article reads, "The Stile of this confederacy shall be, 'The United States of America.'"[^s4] Later proposals such as "Columbia" or "Alleghania" never replaced it.[^s19]

## Geography

The fifty states and the District of Columbia cover 3,796,742 square miles (9,833,517 square kilometers), of which 3,531,905 square miles are land, according to the Census Bureau's 2010 area measurements.[^s2] Alaska is by far the largest state at 665,384 square miles, and Rhode Island the smallest at 1,545.[^s2] To the north, the boundary with Canada runs 5,525 miles (8,891 kilometers) across North America, and is maintained by the binational International Boundary Commission.[^s15] To the south, the boundary with Mexico runs 1,954 miles, not counting its maritime sections; 1,255 miles of it follow the Rio Grande from the Gulf of Mexico to El Paso, and the rest crosses land and the Colorado River to the Pacific.[^s16]

The country's political map has layers. Beyond the states and the federal district lie insular areas that belong to no state; most are unincorporated territories where Congress has applied only selected parts of the Constitution, and Puerto Rico and the Northern Mariana Islands are commonwealths with a more developed relationship set out in written agreements.[^s13] American Samoa, Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands are the other inhabited territories.[^s13] Within the states, 575 federally recognized tribes keep a government-to-government relationship with the United States and inherent rights of self-government.[^s14] The national capital sits on the Potomac River, where the Residence Act of 1790 fixed the permanent seat of government; Congress moved there in 1800.[^s17]

## History

**Indigenous America.** People had lived in the Americas for many thousands of years before Europeans arrived; fossil footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, suggest people walked there about 23,000 years ago.[^s6] Communities burned underbrush to create open hunting grounds and to clear fields for corn, beans and squash.[^s6] Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, grew rapidly around 1050 and was the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico, with some 120 mounds including Monks Mound, the largest prehistoric earthwork in the Americas.[^s7][^s6]

**Independence and a new government.** Thirteen British colonies declared independence in 1776, and Britain recognized the new nation as independent in 1783.[^s3][^s5] Their first constitution, the Articles of Confederation, came into force on March 1, 1781, and was replaced in 1789 by the present Constitution.[^s4] The Treaty of Paris set the new nation's western boundary at the Mississippi River.[^s5]

**Expansion and its costs.** The United States bought the Louisiana territory from France in 1803 for $15 million, a tract of about 530 million acres,[^s8] and purchased Alaska from Russia in 1867 for $7.2 million.[^s9] Expansion took Native homelands. Under the Indian Removal Act, signed in 1830, the Jackson administration negotiated almost 70 removal treaties and relocated nearly 50,000 eastern Indians; in 1838–39 the Cherokees were forced west to Indian Territory, and about 4,000 of 16,000 died on the way.[^s10] In 1898 Queen Liliuokalani protested to Congress against the U.S. claim of ownership over Hawaii.[^s12]

**Slavery and the Civil War.** The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery in the United States; Congress passed it on January 31, 1865, as the Civil War neared its end, and it was ratified on December 6, 1865.[^s11] Because the Emancipation Proclamation of 1863 applied only to areas in rebellion, a constitutional amendment was needed to end slavery nationwide; the amendment still allows involuntary servitude as punishment for a crime.[^s11]

**Fifty states.** Alaska became a state on January 3, 1959,[^s9] and Hawaii became the fiftieth on August 21, 1959.[^s12]

## Today

The 2020 census counted 331,449,281 residents on April 1, 2020, an increase of 22,703,743, or 7.4 percent, from 308,745,538 in 2010; Utah grew fastest and Texas added the most people.[^s1] Census counts also decide representation: the apportionment count divides seats in the House among the fifty states, and excludes the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico.[^s1] Government operates on several levels at once: federal, state, territorial and tribal.[^s13][^s14]
