# Oklahoma City bombing

At 9:02 a.m. on April 19, 1995, a truck bomb exploded outside the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building in downtown Oklahoma City.[^s2][^s3] It killed 168 people, among them 19 children, and injured about 850.[^s1][^s2] It was the nation's worst single act of domestic terrorism, surpassed in deaths only by the September 11, 2001, attack on the World Trade Center.[^s2] It forced Americans and federal law enforcement to reckon with terrorism carried out by their own citizens.[^s1]

## Background

The bomber, Timothy McVeigh, was a Gulf War veteran drawn, like his accomplice Terry Nichols, to the anti-government militia culture of the early 1990s.[^s2] According to testimony summarised by the federal appeals court, McVeigh wrote of answering the government's 1993 siege of the Branch Davidians at Waco, Texas. He chose the anniversary of the siege's end for his attack and picked the Murrah Building because he believed the Waco orders had come from there; he considered anyone inside guilty by association.[^s3]

The plot took months. In the fall of 1994 McVeigh bought about 4,000 pounds of ammonium nitrate in Kansas under an alias, and with Nichols stole explosives and blasting caps from a Kansas quarry; the two robbed an Arkansas gun dealer to pay for it.[^s3] McVeigh described the plan to his friend Michael Fortier and showed him the building, and Fortier's wife, Lori, helped make the fake driver's licence used to rent the truck.[^s3]

## What happened

McVeigh parked a rented Ryder truck holding an ammonium nitrate and fuel oil bomb, estimated at 4,800 pounds, at the building's north entrance.[^s2][^s3] The blast destroyed most of the nine-storey building and damaged more than 300 others across a 48-block area.[^s1][^s2] Of the dead, 163 were inside the building and 5 outside; 15 were children in the building's day care centre and 4 more were children visiting, and 8 were federal law enforcement officers.[^s3] The governor's office later counted 30 children orphaned, 219 who lost at least one parent, 462 people left homeless and about 7,000 who lost their workplace.[^s2] Rescue work soon turned into the recovery of the dead.[^s2]

About 77 minutes after the blast, Oklahoma state trooper Charles Hanger stopped McVeigh's yellow Mercury on Interstate 35 because it had no licence plate, found a concealed loaded gun, and jailed him in Perry.[^s3] Meanwhile FBI agent Barry Black found the truck's rear axle; its vehicle identification number led to a rental shop in Kansas, where workers helped draw a composite sketch that a hotel employee recognised.[^s1] Federal charges followed on April 21.[^s3] The investigation involved more than 1,400 investigators, over 28,000 interviews and more than three tons of evidence.[^s1]

In the first hours, news reports blamed Islamic terrorists, setting off about two days of anti-Muslim hysteria across the country before the arrests showed the attackers were Americans.[^s2] A second FBI sketch, "John Doe #2," drew thousands of tips; the memorial museum notes that the sketch was identified as Todd Bunting, a man who had rented a truck unrelated to the bombing the previous day.[^s7]

## Aftermath and legacy

A grand jury indicted McVeigh and Nichols on August 10, 1995.[^s2] Finding a fair trial impossible in Oklahoma, Judge Richard Matsch moved the case to Denver.[^s3] McVeigh was convicted on all counts on June 2, 1997, sentenced to death, and executed on June 11, 2001; Nichols was convicted of conspiracy and manslaughter and sentenced to life without parole; Fortier received 12 years.[^s2][^s3][^s1] The federal appeals court affirmed the judgment against McVeigh in 1998.[^s3]

The bombing reshaped federal counterterrorism. The FBI came to treat homegrown terrorism as a priority, expanding its Joint Terrorism Task Forces and bomb-technician training.[^s1] President Bill Clinton asked federal law enforcement what new powers it needed against domestic terrorism and sent Congress proposals in May 1995; the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act that emerged was signed on April 24, 1996.[^s6] The act required chemical markers in plastic explosives and banned fundraising for terrorist groups, but it also narrowed federal habeas corpus review; Clinton said it should have been stronger and voiced concern about its habeas and immigration provisions.[^s6]

Oklahoma Citians celebrated the "Oklahoma standard," the selfless response of thousands of residents.[^s2] Families and survivors helped choose the memorial design, dedicated in 2000.[^s2][^s1] On the building's footprint, 168 empty chairs stand in nine rows for its nine floors.[^s5] Gates mark 9:01, which the Park Service calls "the last minute of innocence for our nation in regards to domestic terrorism," and 9:03, the first moment of recovery.[^s4] A surviving American elm, the Survivor Tree, still stands.[^s4]

## Connections

The bombing occurred in Oklahoma City and belongs to the history of terrorism in the United States; McVeigh's motive grew out of the 1993 Waco siege.[^s3][^s2] It led to the Oklahoma City National Memorial and contributed to the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996.[^s2][^s6] It changed the priorities of the Federal Bureau of Investigation.[^s1] Of terrorist attacks in the United States, only the September 11 attacks have killed more people.[^s2]
