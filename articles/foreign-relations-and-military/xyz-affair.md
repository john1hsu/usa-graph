# XYZ Affair

The XYZ Affair was a diplomatic crisis of 1797–1798 in which agents of the French foreign minister, Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand, told three American envoys in Paris that France would not negotiate until the United States paid money to Talleyrand and the ruling Directory and lent money to France.[^s1][^s6] When President John Adams released the envoys' dispatches in 1798, he replaced the agents' names with letters, and the episode took its name from the three who did most of the talking.[^s1] The revelation turned American opinion sharply against France and led to an undeclared naval war, the Quasi-War, which lasted until the Convention of 1800.[^s1][^s2]

## Background

In the 1790s Britain and Revolutionary France were at war, and the young United States, still formally allied to France by the treaties of 1778, tried to stay neutral.[^s6][^s3] The 1794 Jay Treaty with Britain angered the French Directory, which was also short of money for its European wars, and France ordered the seizure of American merchant ships.[^s1] Adams responded in 1797 by sending a three-man commission to Paris: Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, John Marshall and Elbridge Gerry.[^s1][^s6]

## What happened

Talleyrand received the envoys' credentials in a short audience on October 8, 1797, but would not open talks.[^s6] Instead the Americans dealt with go-betweens: Nicholas Hubbard (W), Jean Hottinguer (X), Pierre Bellamy (Y) and Lucien Hauteval (Z), with the playwright Pierre Beaumarchais also involved.[^s1] Hottinguer laid out the terms on October 18, and they arrived in writing the next day.[^s6] France wanted the United States to assume American merchants' claims against France, to make a large loan and to pay a personal sum to Talleyrand and the Directory.[^s1][^s6] The Miller Center puts the demands at a $250,000 bribe and a $10 million loan; the Bill of Rights Institute gives them as £50,000 and 32 million Dutch florins.[^s2][^s6] When the agents pressed again on October 21, Pinckney is reported to have answered, "No! No! Not a sixpence!"[^s6]

The envoys refused, and the French side changed the loan terms and threatened invasion.[^s1] Talleyrand later met the envoys formally and dropped most of the demands, but France did not stop seizing American ships.[^s1] Pinckney and Marshall prepared to leave, and Marshall sailed for home on April 23, 1798; Gerry stayed in Paris in the hope of preventing war.[^s1][^s6]

At home, Adams asked Congress for money for a larger navy, coastal defenses and a provisional army.[^s2] Democratic-Republicans in Congress demanded to see the dispatches.[^s1] On April 3 Adams sent them, leaving out "some names, and a few expressions descriptive of the persons," and asking that the papers be kept confidential until Congress had weighed publishing them; the agents' names were replaced with letters.[^s4][^s1] The papers fed a wave of anti-French feeling and strengthened Federalists who wanted war.[^s1][^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

Congress went on to expand the navy, create the Navy Department, organize the Marine Corps and fund coastal defenses.[^s2] On July 7, 1798, it declared the 1778 treaties and the 1788 consular convention with France no longer binding, citing France's "predatory violence" against American commerce.[^s3] The same Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts.[^s2] War was never formally declared, and the bloodshed came in clashes at sea; in the Caribbean the U.S. Navy also supported Toussaint Louverture in Haiti.[^s1][^s2]

Adams wanted peace on honorable terms, even as some Federalists hoped for full war.[^s2] When Talleyrand signaled in 1798–1799 that a new envoy would be received, Adams named a fresh commission, and after several weeks of talks in Paris it signed the Convention of 1800, also called the Treaty of Mortefontaine, with Napoleon Bonaparte's government.[^s2][^s1] The convention ended the 1778 alliance and the Quasi-War but gave no compensation for seized ships; the United States did not make another formal alliance for nearly 150 years.[^s1] Adams later called the peace "the great jewel in his crown."[^s2]

The affair is also remembered for a slogan. Founders Online's editors note that "Millions for defence but not a sixpence for tribute" was first given as a toast, attributed to Congressman Robert Goodloe Harper, at a June 23, 1798, dinner honoring Marshall's return.[^s5] It is often credited to Pinckney instead, but his reported words in Paris were the shorter refusal.[^s6][^s5]
