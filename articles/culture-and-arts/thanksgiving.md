# Thanksgiving

Thanksgiving is a national holiday held on the fourth Thursday of November, a date fixed by Congress in 1941 after more than 150 years in which presidents set the day by proclamation.[^s1] Many Americans associate it with a 1621 harvest gathering of English colonists and Wampanoag people at Plymouth, but the holiday itself descends from official days of thanksgiving and from a long nineteenth-century campaign, and the 1621 story has been retold in ways that leave out Wampanoag history.[^s4][^s5]

## Origins

The Wampanoag had lived at the coastal place they called Patuxet for about 10,000 years before English colonists settled there in 1620. Between 1616 and 1619, diseases brought by Europeans killed up to 90 percent of New England's Native population. Weakened, and facing pressure from the rival Narragansett, the Wampanoag under Ousamequin (Massasoit), who led about 70 communities from Pokanoket, agreed in 1621 to a treaty in which each side pledged to aid the other if attacked; Tisquantum (Squanto) served as an interpreter.[^s4] The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian stresses that the Wampanoag chose to assist the English for reasons of politics and diplomacy more than friendship, and that the peace was short-lived.[^s5]

That autumn, probably in late September or early October, Massasoit arrived at the colony with about 90 men, who brought five deer, and the two groups shared a three-day outdoor gathering. Edward Winslow's one-paragraph account dates it to when "our harvest being gotten in," and Governor William Bradford mentions plentiful wild turkeys, but neither writer calls it a thanksgiving. Historians disagree on whether the Wampanoag were invited or arrived unexpectedly.[^s4]

The national holiday grew from a different line of observances. In September 1789 Representative Elias Boudinot moved that Congress ask President Washington to recommend a day of public thanksgiving and prayer; Aedanus Burke and Thomas Tudor Tucker objected, the latter arguing that religious matters were not Congress's business, but the resolution passed.[^s2] On October 3, 1789, Washington proclaimed Thursday, November 26, as such a day, after the first Congress had asked him to recommend one.[^s1]

Sarah Josepha Hale, editor of Boston's *Ladies' Magazine* and later *Godey's Lady's Book*, urged a national Thanksgiving from 1827 and organized a letter-writing campaign from 1846. On September 28, 1863, she wrote to Abraham Lincoln, and on October 3 he issued a proclamation, written by Secretary of State William Seward, setting apart "the last Thursday of November next" during the Civil War.[^s3]

## Meaning and practice

After Lincoln, presidents continued to proclaim the day; Andrew Johnson chose the first Thursday of December in 1865 but afterward returned to the last Thursday of November. In 1870 Congress made it a legal holiday in the District of Columbia while leaving the date to the President. In 1939, when the last Thursday fell on November 30, Franklin D. Roosevelt moved the holiday a week earlier; business owners had lobbied for an earlier date since 1933, when the holiday had last fallen so late. Some states followed, others refused and some observed both days. Congress settled the matter with H.J. Res. 41: the House passed it on October 6, 1941; the Senate amended it on December 9 to name the fourth Thursday, so the date would not shift in years with five Thursdays; and Roosevelt signed it on December 26, effective in 1942.[^s1]

The popular image of Pilgrims sharing a friendly meal with nameless Indians took hold in the mid-1800s, after English accounts of 1621 resurfaced, and was reinforced by romantic paintings and stereotyped images. The NMAI calls that story incomplete and inaccurate, and notes that harvest ceremonies had been part of Wampanoag life for thousands of years.[^s5] Wampanoag educators such as Darius Coombs and Linda Coombs emphasize that the gathering was followed by colonization, cultural destruction and servitude.[^s4]

For many Native people the day is one of mourning. In 1970, Frank James (Wamsutta), an Aquinnah Wampanoag invited to speak at the 350th anniversary of the Pilgrims' landing, was told his prepared remarks were too inflammatory. He delivered them instead on Cole's Hill in Plymouth, above Plymouth Rock, on Thanksgiving Day, and Native people and supporters have gathered there every Thanksgiving since for the National Day of Mourning.[^s6] Museums such as Pilgrim Hall and Plimoth Patuxet now present the 1621 events with Wampanoag perspectives alongside English ones.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Established by** Lincoln's Thanksgiving proclamation of 1863 (`us:thanksgiving-proclamation-of-1863`), which began a regular annual national observance on the last Thursday of November.[^s3]
- **Established by** the 1941 joint resolution H.J. Res. 41 (`us:thanksgiving-day-resolution-of-1941`), which fixed the fourth Thursday of November in law.[^s1]
- **Influenced by** Sarah Josepha Hale (`us:sarah-josepha-hale`), whose decades-long campaign and 1863 letter preceded Lincoln's proclamation.[^s3]
- **Influenced by** Washington's Thanksgiving proclamation of 1789 (`us:thanksgiving-proclamation-of-1789`), issued at the request of the first Congress.[^s2]
- **Related** to the Wampanoag, whose alliance with Plymouth Colony framed the 1621 gathering,[^s4] and to the National Day of Mourning held in Plymouth since 1970.[^s6] The storybook "first Thanksgiving" is treated as its own myth node (`us:first-thanksgiving-myth`).[^s5]
