# Voting Rights Act of 1965

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 is the federal law that made the Fifteenth Amendment's ban on racial discrimination in voting enforceable in practice. Its official title called it an act "to enforce the fifteenth amendment," and President Lyndon B. Johnson signed it on August 6, 1965, ninety-five years after that amendment was ratified.[^s1] It outlawed literacy tests, sent federal examiners to register voters, and required places with a history of disenfranchisement to get federal approval before changing their election rules.[^s1][^s5]

## Background

After Reconstruction, Southern states used literacy tests, poll taxes, intimidation and violence to keep Black citizens from voting, despite the Fifteenth Amendment; in 1964 voting-rights workers were murdered in Mississippi.[^s1] Enforcing the amendment one lawsuit at a time left the advantage of delay with the officials who discriminated.[^s5] The gap was stark in Alabama: in 1964 only about 23 percent of eligible Black residents were registered, and in Dallas County, whose seat is Selma, only a few hundred of more than 15,000 eligible Black residents were on the rolls.[^s4][^s3] Lowndes and Wilcox counties had no registered Black voters at all.[^s4]

Black Alabamians, working with the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and local organizers, made Selma the center of a registration campaign. After Jimmie Lee Jackson was fatally shot by a white man in February 1965, activists planned a march to Montgomery. On March 7, "Bloody Sunday," officers attacked about 600 marchers at the edge of Selma; on March 25 some 25,000 people led by Martin Luther King Jr. completed the march under federal protection.[^s3][^s4] Johnson laid out his proposals to a joint session of Congress on March 15.[^s4]

## Passage

The bill drew 66 Senate sponsors. To keep Judiciary Committee chairman James Eastland of Mississippi, an opponent of civil rights bills, from bottling it up, the Senate ordered the committee to report by April 9. A bipartisan group of Democrats and Republicans strengthened it, the Senate broke a filibuster with a 70-to-30 cloture vote, and on May 26 it passed the bill 77 to 19.[^s3] The House passed its version, H.R. 6400, 333 to 85 on July 9 after rejecting a weaker substitute, and both chambers approved a conference version in early August.[^s2][^s3] At the signing, Johnson credited the outrage over Selma and called the denial of Black voting rights a wrong that was "clear and simple."[^s3]

## What it says

- **Section 2** set a permanent, nationwide ban on denying or abridging the vote because of race or color, closely following the Fifteenth Amendment.[^s1][^s5][^s6]
- **Section 4** defined "covered" jurisdictions: those that used a test or device on November 1, 1964, and where under half of voting-age residents were registered or voted in that year's presidential election. The Act also outlawed literacy tests.[^s1][^s5]
- **Section 5** required covered jurisdictions to obtain "preclearance" from the Attorney General or the federal district court in Washington, D.C., showing that any new voting practice had no discriminatory purpose or effect.[^s1][^s5]
- Other provisions let the Attorney General send **federal examiners** to register voters and **observers** to watch polling places, and directed the Attorney General to challenge **poll taxes** in state elections; the Twenty-fourth Amendment had already banned them in federal elections, and the Supreme Court struck down Virginia's in 1966.[^s1][^s5][^s6]

The Supreme Court upheld the Act in *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* (1966), reasoning that Congress could shift the advantage of time and inertia from those who discriminated to their victims.[^s5]

## Legacy

The effect was rapid. About 250,000 new Black voters registered by the end of 1965, a third of them through federal examiners.[^s1] In Alabama, Black registration rose to about 51 percent within two years, and in Dallas County nearly 9,000 Black residents were signed up.[^s4] Black officeholding grew more slowly: Alabama had no Black state legislators in 1966 and 24 Black state or local officials by 1968.[^s4]

Congress renewed and expanded the special provisions in 1970, 1975 (adding protections for language-minority citizens) and 1982, when it also amended Section 2 so plaintiffs could prove a violation without showing discriminatory intent, responding to *Mobile v. Bolden* (1980). In 2006 it renewed them for 25 more years.[^s5][^s7]

Two Supreme Court decisions then narrowed the Act. In *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), a suit brought by an Alabama county, the Court held 5 to 4 that the Section 4(b) coverage formula could no longer be used, which halted preclearance unless Congress writes a new formula. The majority, invoking the principle that states have equal sovereignty, found the formula rested on "decades-old data and eradicated practices."[^s7][^s4] Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg, dissenting for four justices, argued that Congress's 2006 record of more than 15,000 pages showed continuing discrimination and that the choice belonged to Congress.[^s7] Critics of the ruling, including civil rights groups, point to later polling-place closures in majority-Black Alabama counties as evidence of its cost.[^s4] In *Louisiana v. Callais* (2026), the Court held, in an opinion by Justice Samuel Alito, that Section 2 makes a state liable in redistricting only when the evidence supports a strong inference of intentional discrimination, and struck down a second majority-Black congressional district as a racial gerrymander; Justice Elena Kagan, dissenting for three justices, argued that the new test returned Section 2 to the intent standard Congress had rejected in 1982 and would make claims nearly impossible to win.[^s8]

## Connections

The Act was **enacted by** the U.S. Congress through separate Senate and House bills reconciled in conference,[^s2][^s3] and **signed by** Lyndon B. Johnson.[^s1] It **protects** the right to vote by enforcing the Fifteenth Amendment, which is the background a reader needs to understand it.[^s1] It was **influenced by** the Selma-to-Montgomery marches, which Johnson credited with prompting federal action,[^s3] and by the broader civil rights movement's campaigns against violent resistance to Black voting.[^s1] It sits within the long history of voting rights in the United States alongside the Twenty-fourth Amendment, and its meaning has been shaped by court cases from *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* to *Shelby County v. Holder* and *Louisiana v. Callais*.[^s5][^s7][^s8]
