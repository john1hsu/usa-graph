# Greensboro sit-ins

The Greensboro sit-ins were a nonviolent campaign against segregated lunch counters that began on February 1, 1960, when four Black freshmen from North Carolina Agricultural and Technical College — Ezell Blair Jr. (later Jibreel Khazan), Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil and David Richmond — sat at the whites-only counter of the F. W. Woolworth store in downtown Greensboro and asked to be served.[^s3] Within weeks students across the South had copied them, and the campaign ended on July 25, 1960, when the Greensboro counter was desegregated.[^s1][^s6]

## Background

In 1960 racial segregation of private businesses was still legal.[^s3] Woolworth's sold to Black shoppers throughout the store but served them food only at a stand-up counter.[^s5] Greensboro, a city of 119,574 people at the time according to the Census Bureau, cultivated a moderate "New South" image and was the first Southern city to announce it would comply with *Brown v. Board of Education*, yet its schools were not integrated until the 1970s.[^s2][^s4] A&T students already had a record of protest, including a 1937 movie-theater boycott.[^s4]

The four friends had talked often about injustice, encouraged by Ralph Johns, a white clothing-store owner downtown, and in January 1960 McNeil proposed a sit-in.[^s4] The Census Bureau's account stresses that the four planned their own protest rather than acting for an organization.[^s2]

## What happened

On the afternoon of February 1 the four walked from A&T's library to Woolworth's, bought small items, kept their receipts and sat down at the counter.[^s4] Their reasoning was simple: a store that took their money should also serve them.[^s7] They were refused service; manager Clarence "Curly" Harris, told by his supervisor to refuse them, expected them to give up, and he closed the counter at the end of the day rather than have them arrested.[^s2][^s5]

The protest grew daily. About two dozen students came the next morning, and on February 3 students held 63 of the counter's 65 seats and spread the protest to the nearby S. H. Kress store, while the local NAACP endorsed the sit-ins.[^s1][^s4] Three white students from Woman's College (now UNC Greensboro) joined on February 4, and by February 5 more than 300 students were taking part.[^s4] That day police made the campaign's first arrests — three white men charged with intimidating the protesters, one for setting a Black man's coat on fire.[^s4] Accounts of crowd sizes differ, but the Census Bureau estimates that about 1,400 students sought service on February 6.[^s2] North Carolina's attorney general said no law required stores to serve both races, and President Dwight D. Eisenhower declined to judge the protests.[^s4]

After the first week, stores closed and the students paused to let a city committee led by councilman Ed Zane negotiate.[^s6][^s4] When talks failed, demonstrations resumed on April 1, with as many as 1,200 A&T and Bennett students picketing; Martin Luther King Jr. and Thurgood Marshall urged them not to accept token integration.[^s4] By the end of June the boycott had hurt sales so badly that managers gave up.[^s6] Woolworth's lost an estimated $200,000, and on July 25, 1960, Harris had three Black employees served at the counter, with little publicity because many students had left for the summer.[^s2][^s4]

## Aftermath and legacy

The tactic spread fast. By the end of February sit-ins had taken place at more than 30 locations in seven states, and by the end of April more than 50,000 students had joined; in Nashville, workshops in Gandhian nonviolence led by James Lawson trained future leaders such as Diane Nash and John Lewis.[^s1] Many protesters elsewhere were beaten, heckled and arrested.[^s1] On April 15–17, 1960, Ella Baker of the SCLC called sit-in leaders to Shaw University in Raleigh, where about 120 students from 12 Southern states founded SNCC as an independent youth organization; King praised a movement "initiated, fed and sustained by students."[^s1] NCpedia counts SNCC's creation as the sit-ins' main national result.[^s5]

The Woolworth store closed in 1993, and its building now houses the International Civil Rights Center and Museum.[^s5] A section of the counter, donated after long negotiations with company and community representatives, is on permanent display at the Smithsonian, which links such protests to the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and its ban on segregation in public accommodations.[^s3]

## Connections

The sit-ins **occurred in** Greensboro, North Carolina.[^s2] They **led to** the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, founded by sit-in leaders at Shaw University,[^s1][^s5] and, as the Smithsonian notes, protests like them **led to** the Civil Rights Act of 1964.[^s3] They were part of the wider civil rights movement and challenged the segregation that Jim Crow laws imposed, drawing on the same nonviolent approach used in the Montgomery bus boycott.[^s2] Ella Baker, Martin Luther King Jr. and the NAACP supported the students.[^s1][^s4]
