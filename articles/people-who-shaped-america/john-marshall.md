# John Marshall

John Marshall (1755–1835) was Chief Justice of the United States from 1801 to 1835 and, at 34 years, the longest-serving chief justice in the nation's history.[^s1] Born near Germantown in Fauquier County, Virginia, on September 24, 1755, he was a soldier, Richmond lawyer, state legislator, diplomat, congressman and secretary of state before President John Adams named him to lead the Supreme Court in 1801.[^s1][^s9] His opinions established that federal courts may refuse to enforce laws that conflict with the Constitution and gave a broad reading to the powers of the national government.[^s3][^s4] He was also a large-scale enslaver and the author of the decisions on which federal Indian law still rests, and historians weigh those records alongside his reputation as the Court's great builder.[^s5][^s7][^s8]

## Life and work

Marshall was one of fourteen children of Thomas Marshall, a landowner active in local government, and Mary Randolph Marshall.[^s1] When the Revolutionary War began he joined Virginia's forces, enlisting as a lieutenant in 1776 and rising to captain in the Continental Army by July 1778.[^s10] He endured the winter at Valley Forge with Washington's army, and later wrote that the war taught him to regard "America as my country, and Congress as my government."[^s3]

He studied law at the College of William & Mary in 1780, was admitted to the bar, and built a practice in Richmond.[^s1][^s2] He served in the Virginia House of Delegates for most of the years from 1782 to 1797 and on the state's Council of State from 1782 to 1784.[^s9] In June 1788 he voted to ratify the Constitution as a delegate to the Virginia convention.[^s9]

National office followed. In 1797 Adams sent him with two other envoys to negotiate with the French foreign minister Talleyrand; the failed mission became the XYZ Affair.[^s2][^s9] Marshall entered the U.S. House of Representatives in December 1799, and in May 1800 Adams nominated him as secretary of state; he took up the post on June 6.[^s1][^s2] After Chief Justice Oliver Ellsworth resigned, Adams nominated Marshall, the Senate confirmed him on January 27, 1801, and he served as chief justice while acting as secretary of state until March 4, 1801.[^s1][^s2]

As chief justice he wrote most of the Court's important opinions.[^s3] In *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) he held that the Court could not grant William Marbury his commission because the part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 authorizing the writ went beyond the Constitution; it is "emphatically the province and duty" of courts, he wrote, to say what the law is.[^s4] *McCulloch v. Maryland* and the *Dartmouth College* case (both 1819) and *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) upheld broad national powers, protected contracts from state interference and gave Congress wide authority over interstate commerce.[^s1][^s3] By the Supreme Court Historical Society's count, the Court issued 1,129 decisions during his tenure, 1,042 of them unanimous.[^s1]

Marshall also wrote the three opinions that shape federal law on Native nations. In *Johnson v. M'Intosh* (1823) he grounded American land titles in the European "doctrine of discovery," leaving Native peoples a right of occupancy rather than full ownership.[^s8] In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) he called the Cherokees a domestic dependent nation and dismissed their suit, but in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) he held that the Cherokee Nation remained a separate sovereign whose territory Georgia's laws could not reach.[^s7] Georgia ignored the ruling, President Andrew Jackson declined to enforce it, and in 1838 the U.S. Army forced most Cherokees from their homeland on the Trail of Tears.[^s7]

Marshall's household and fortune rested on slavery. His family's farm, Oak Hill, held 22 enslaved people, and he bought enslaved people to work his home and lands; by the 1830s he and his sons held about 250 people on their plantations.[^s1] Robin Spurlock was given to Marshall and his wife Polly as a wedding present in 1783, and Robin's daughter Agnes was later given away as a dowry gift when Mary Willis Marshall married Fielding Lewis Douthat; a sketch of her is the only known likeness of anyone Marshall enslaved.[^s6] Marshall was a life member of the American Colonization Society and president of its Richmond branch, and after Nat Turner's rebellion he petitioned Virginia's legislature for funds to remove free Black people from the state.[^s5] His wife died in 1831, and he died on July 6, 1835.[^s1]

## Legacy

Chief Justice William Rehnquist credited Marshall with making the Supreme Court a full partner in the federal government by the time of his death.[^s3] His opinions on judicial review, national power and commerce remain foundational, and his Native nations trilogy still governs relations among tribes, states and the federal government.[^s4][^s7] The Library of Congress notes that the Vatican formally repudiated the doctrine of discovery he relied on in 2023.[^s8] Preservation Virginia has published the stories of people he enslaved, such as Agnes Spurlock, who mostly did not live to see emancipation.[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

**Nation-builder.** In this view, which Rehnquist set out, Marshall's clear reasoning and personal skill turned a weak tribunal into a coequal branch, holding the Constitution supreme and the federal courts its final interpreters despite about 24 years of opposition from the more state-centered Presidents Jefferson, Madison and Monroe.[^s3]

**Slavery and the law.** Paul Finkelman argues that biographers have badly understated Marshall's slaveholding and that it shaped his jurisprudence: enslaved plaintiffs lost all seven freedom cases in which he wrote the majority opinion, and in *The Antelope* he called the slave trade contrary to natural law yet declined to free the captives on that basis.[^s5] He also reports the readings he disputes, such as R. Kent Newmyer's view that the rulings reflected deference to the states.[^s5]

**Native sovereignty.** Tim Alan Garrison's account of the Cherokee cases stresses that *Worcester* affirmed tribal nations as distinct political communities with title to their lands, even though it could not stop removal.[^s7] The Library of Congress's legal staff emphasize instead that *Johnson v. M'Intosh* embedded the doctrine of discovery in American law and served as a legal basis for taking Indigenous land.[^s8]
