# Census and apportionment

Apportionment is the rule that divides seats in the U.S. House of Representatives among the states according to population. Article I of the Constitution ties House seats (and, originally, direct taxes) to an "actual Enumeration" of the people, to be taken within three years of Congress's first meeting and every ten years after, in a manner Congress directs by law.[^s1] The Constitution caps representation at one member for every 30,000 people and guarantees each state at least one.[^s1] Seats have been reapportioned after every census since 1790 with one exception, the census of 1920.[^s2] The Supreme Court has said the clause means that each state's comparative power in the House reflects comparative population rather than comparative wealth.[^s1]

## Origins

The formula was hammered out at the Constitutional Convention in the summer of 1787. On June 11, James Wilson of Pennsylvania proposed counting free people plus three-fifths of "other persons," a ratio the Confederation Congress had recommended in 1783 for apportioning revenue among the states.[^s11] South Carolina delegates, among them Pierce Butler, moved to count enslaved people equally with free people, which would have given slaveholding states still more seats; the motions failed. Gouverneur Morris of Pennsylvania opposed counting enslaved people at all. On July 12 the convention voted to tie representation to direct taxation on the three-fifths basis.[^s11] The final text counted free persons and those bound to service for a term of years, excluded "Indians not taxed," and added "three fifths of all other Persons," meaning enslaved people, who could not vote but enlarged the House delegations of the states that held them.[^s1] Until a count could be made, the Constitution itself assigned 65 seats, from Virginia's 10 to one each for Rhode Island and Delaware.[^s1][^s2]

The first census began on August 2, 1790, carried out by U.S. marshals and their deputies, and counted 3,929,214 people.[^s5] Turning that count into seats produced the first test of the presidential veto. Congress sent George Washington a bill for a 120-seat House; Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson and Attorney General Edmund Randolph argued it was unconstitutional, while Alexander Hamilton and Henry Knox supported it.[^s5] On April 5, 1792, Washington vetoed it, objecting that no single ratio produced its allotment and that it gave eight states more than one representative per 30,000. In his words, "the bill has allotted to eight of the States, more than one for thirty thousand." Congress then passed a 105-seat plan using Jefferson's method, which Washington signed on April 14.[^s5]

## How it works

Three things decide each state's share: its apportionment population, the size of the House and the mathematical method.[^s2] The population base has changed over time. Until the Fourteenth Amendment, it was free people plus three-fifths of the enslaved, minus "Indians not taxed." The amendment, ratified in 1868, counted "the whole number of persons in each State."[^s2][^s6] Because freed people would now be counted in full, the amendment's framers feared a gain in Southern power while Southern states resisted Black suffrage; Section 2 therefore ordered a state's representation reduced if it denied adult male citizens the vote. No serious effort was ever made in Congress to enforce that penalty.[^s6] Most Native people were not counted in 1790 because they lived under separate, treaty-recognized sovereignty. American Indians were counted fully on regular schedules from 1940, the year the Attorney General concluded that none remained "not taxed."[^s7][^s2]

The House's size was set by Congress, not the Constitution. From 1800 to 1840 Congress fixed a ratio of people per member, so the House grew with the population. From 1850 it fixed the size first. In 1911 it set 433 seats, plus one each for Arizona and New Mexico on statehood, and the House has stayed at 435 since, apart from a temporary 437 after Alaska and Hawaii joined.[^s2][^s3] Methods changed too: Jefferson's method discarded fractions (1790–1830); Webster's rounded them (1840); the Vinton or Hamilton method gave leftover seats to the largest remainders (1850–1900) but produced the "Alabama paradox," in which a state could lose a seat when the House grew.[^s2] Since 1941 the law has used equal proportions, known as the Huntington-Hill method, which reapportions the 435 seats automatically after each census.[^s3]

The one break came after 1920. That census showed a major shift of Americans from farms to cities, and representatives of rural districts, fearing a loss of power, stalled reapportionment for the rest of the decade.[^s4] The Reapportionment and Census Act of June 18, 1929, ended the stalemate by making apportionment automatic after the 1930 count, with the House kept at 435.[^s3][^s4]

The most recent apportionment followed the 2020 census, which counted a resident population of 331,449,281 on April 1, 2020, up 7.4 percent from 2010.[^s8] The apportionment population of the 50 states was 331,108,434, which includes 348,698 overseas military and federal civilian employees and their dependents assigned to home states.[^s9] The District of Columbia and Puerto Rico are excluded because they have no voting seats. Texas gained two seats; Colorado, Florida, Montana, North Carolina and Oregon gained one each; California, Illinois, Michigan, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania and West Virginia lost one each. The average district held 761,169 people (Census Bureau, 2020 census).[^s8] The Supreme Court upheld the equal-proportions method in 1992, observing that exactly equal districts are achievable within a state but not across the whole nation.[^s1]

## Connections

- **United States Constitution:** Article I, Section 2 creates the census and the apportionment of House seats.[^s1]
- **Fourteenth Amendment:** Section 2 replaced the three-fifths formula with a count of all persons and added the unenforced voting penalty.[^s6]
- **House of Representatives:** every census since 1790, except 1920, has redistributed its seats among the states.[^s2]
- **Native Americans:** the "Indians not taxed" exclusion kept most Native people out of the count until 1940.[^s7]
- **Redistricting:** once seats are apportioned, states redraw their district boundaries using the small-area counts the Census Bureau must deliver within a year of Census Day.[^s10]
