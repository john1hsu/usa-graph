# War in Afghanistan (2001–2021)

The war in Afghanistan was a twenty-year campaign that began less than a month after the September 11 attacks and ended with the Taliban back in power.[^s3][^s1] It cost the lives of 2,443 U.S. troops and 1,144 allied troops, at least 66,000 Afghan soldiers and police, and more than 48,000 Afghan civilians — the last two figures likely significant undercounts (SIGAR, 2021).[^s2]

## Background

The Taliban, who had ruled Afghanistan in the 1990s and barred women from school and most work, offered al Qaeda's leader Osama bin Laden sanctuary, and al Qaeda trained thousands of fighters in camps there.[^s1][^s4] The country was, in the Miller Center's words, a "safe haven for al Qaeda."[^s3] On September 18, 2001, Congress authorized the president to use all necessary and appropriate force against the nations, organizations and people who planned, aided or harbored those behind the attacks.[^s6]

## What happened

U.S. air strikes on Taliban installations and al Qaeda camps began on October 7, 2001. Working with CIA-funded Northern Alliance fighters, U.S. Special Forces helped take Mazar-i-Sharif in early November; the Taliban abandoned Kabul, and on December 7 Hamid Karzai's southern forces and U.S. Marines took Kandahar.[^s4] Bin Laden escaped to Pakistan, and al Qaeda's fighters scattered there after the battle of Tora Bora.[^s4][^s3] A UN-mandated International Security Assistance Force (ISAF), created after the Bonn Conference of December 2001, initially secured Kabul; NATO took command of it on August 11, 2003, and extended it across the country by October 2006.[^s8]

From 2003 Washington's attention turned to Iraq, where about 150,000–170,000 U.S. troops served while roughly 20,000 were in Afghanistan, and the Taliban regrouped in the south and east, reasserting themselves around 2005–2006.[^s3] President George W. Bush raised U.S. forces from about 20,000 to more than 30,000 after attacks rose sharply in 2006.[^s4] President Barack Obama approved 21,000 more troops in early 2009 and, at West Point on December 1, 2009, a surge of 33,000, with withdrawals to begin in July 2011; U.S. strength peaked at 97,000 in 2011.[^s5] NATO-led forces exceeded 130,000 personnel from 51 countries.[^s8] On May 2, 2011, Navy SEALs killed bin Laden in Abbottabad, Pakistan.[^s5] Afghan forces took the lead nationwide in June 2013, ISAF's combat mission ended in late 2014, and on January 1, 2015, NATO began a smaller train-and-advise mission; about 12,000 U.S. troops remained in 2015.[^s8][^s5]

On February 29, 2020, the Trump administration and the Taliban signed an agreement in Doha, Qatar: U.S. forces would drop to 8,600 within 135 days and all foreign forces would leave within 14 months, while the Taliban pledged not to let al Qaeda or other groups use Afghan soil to threaten the United States and its allies.[^s7] On April 14, 2021, President Joe Biden announced a final withdrawal, later setting August 31 as the mission's end.[^s1] On August 15 the Taliban entered Kabul and President Ashraf Ghani fled.[^s1] The United States evacuated or helped about 124,000 people leave, including about 6,000 U.S. citizens, through Kabul's airport.[^s1] On August 26 a suicide bombing claimed by the local Islamic State affiliate killed 13 U.S. service members and dozens of Afghans at an airport gate.[^s1][^s10] The last U.S. forces left on August 30, 2021.[^s1]

## Aftermath and legacy

The Defense Department obligated $837.3 billion for the war through March 2021, and about $145 billion went to reconstruction, roughly $83 billion of it to Afghan security forces; Brown University's Costs of War Project put broader costs at $2.26 trillion (CRS, 2021).[^s1] Under Operation Allies Welcome, begun on August 29, 2021, the United States received more than 79,000 Afghan evacuees, including more than 33,000 applicants for and about 3,400 holders of Special Immigrant Visas or their families (DHS, 2022).[^s9] The Taliban's 2021 caretaker cabinet was entirely male, and women who protested in Kabul faced bans on demonstrations and violence.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

Why the war ended as it did remains debated. The government's own watchdog, SIGAR, argued that the U.S. never sustained a coherent strategy, set unrealistic timelines that fed corruption, misread Afghan society, and empowered abusive powerbrokers.[^s2] Officials interviewed in Miller Center oral histories stressed different causes: Lt. Gen. Douglas Lute blamed the diversion of attention and resources to Iraq, Secretary Robert Gates thought the goals were too ambitious, and Gen. Peter Pace, a former chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, argued that Afghanistan had never had a strong central government and the U.S. did not need to build one.[^s3] On the withdrawal, Biden argued that more years of U.S. presence would not have mattered if the Afghan military could not hold the country, and rejected "an endless American presence" in another nation's civil conflict.[^s1] Critics, including some lawmakers, argued that a few thousand troops could have backed a peace deal at modest cost, that the Doha agreement failed to tie withdrawal to reduced Taliban violence, and that the exit was poorly planned.[^s1]

## Connections

The war followed directly from the September 11 attacks, which are needed to understand it, and Congress's 2001 force authorization supplied its legal basis.[^s6] It was fought in Afghanistan, against the Taliban and al Qaeda, whose fighters it dispersed into Pakistan.[^s2][^s3] It ran alongside the Iraq War, which drew U.S. resources away from it.[^s3] Its end led to Operation Allies Welcome and the resettlement of tens of thousands of Afghans in the United States.[^s9]
