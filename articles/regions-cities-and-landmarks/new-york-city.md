  myth: The Lenape sold Manhattan to the Dutch for $24 worth of beads and trinkets, a naive bargain that shows they did not understand what they were giving up.
  fact: The only contemporary record, Pieter Schaghen's letter of November 5, 1626, says the island was acquired for goods worth 60 guilders and names no Native participants; the $24 figure is a nineteenth-century currency conversion. Scholars caution that the Lenape probably saw the agreement as shared or temporary use, while others stress that Native leaders did transfer and lease land deliberately and were hardly dupes.
  why: The story turns a poorly documented diplomatic exchange into a joke about the "greatest real estate deal in history," erasing the Lenape's own perspectives and the later wars, disease and unequal bargaining that actually drove them from their homeland.

# New York City

New York City is the largest city in the United States, ranked first in the 2020 census ahead of Los Angeles, a metropolis of five boroughs at the mouth of the Hudson River in New York State. It grew from a Dutch trading post on Lenape land into the nation's leading commercial city and the arrival point for millions of immigrants; the 2020 census counted 8,804,190 residents, the most in its history.[^s17][^s13]

## Geography

The city occupies the islands and shores around New York Harbor, part of Lenapehoking, the homeland of the Lenape people that stretched from the New York area through New Jersey into eastern Pennsylvania and Delaware.[^s6] Its central island kept a Munsee name, rendered Manahatta or Manhattes, which the New Netherland Institute glosses as "the place where we get bows."[^s2] Since January 1, 1898, the city has comprised five boroughs: Manhattan, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island.[^s13] The Hudson River ties the harbor to the interior: once the Erie Canal opened, goods from the Great Lakes and the Midwest could float down the Hudson to the city's docks and out to the Atlantic, a route rival ports blocked by the Appalachians could not match.[^s10]

## History

**Lenape homeland and New Amsterdam.** The first known person of African descent on Manhattan was Jan (Juan) Rodrigues, a free Black sailor from Santo Domingo who set up a trading post with the Lenape in 1613.[^s7] In a letter dated November 5, 1626, the Dutch official Pieter Schaghen reported: "They have purchased the Island Manhattes from the Indians for the value of 60 guilders."[^s1] Schaghen was in Amsterdam, not Manhattan, and no record describes the Native people who took part or how they understood the exchange.[^s3] Over the following decades land disputes between the Dutch and Native nations led to war, and the Lenape found it ever harder to keep their land.[^s2] They were pushed west through Pennsylvania, Ohio and Kansas to Oklahoma, and their descendants today include the Delaware Tribe of Indians in Bartlesville, Oklahoma, and Munsee and Delaware communities in Ontario.[^s6]

**Slavery in a northern city.** Enslaved Africans arrived in New Amsterdam in 1625 to labor for the Dutch West India Company; in the 1650s they helped build the town's defensive wall and the road to New Haarlem. In 1644 eleven enslaved men were freed and granted farmland north of the town, the first free Black community in the colonial United States. After the English conquest of 1664 renamed the town New York, the city made the Meal Market its official slave market in 1711. Its burial ground for Africans, used until 1794, eventually covered 6.6 acres and may hold some 20,000 people.[^s7]

**Revolution and the first capital.** British forces occupied New York from 1776 until November 25, 1783, using it as their stronghold. A 1779 British offer of freedom to enslaved people who reached their lines made the city the largest refuge for freedom seekers in North America, and about 3,000 Black Loyalists left with the British in 1783.[^s8] On April 30, 1789, George Washington took the oath as the first president on the balcony of Federal Hall, which also housed the first Congress.[^s9]

**Port, canal and conflict.** The completion of the 363-mile Erie Canal in 1825 cemented New York as the country's leading commercial city, drawing people and capital well into the twentieth century.[^s10] During the Civil War, conscription, with its $300 exemption fee, provoked fierce resistance among working-class New Yorkers, especially recent Irish immigrants; in 1863 rioters burned draft offices and killed as many as 119 people, many of them Black New Yorkers, before the Union Army restored order.[^s11]

**Gateway and Greater New York.** From January 1, 1892, Ellis Island in the harbor processed more than 12 million immigrant steamship passengers until it closed as an immigration station in 1954; the Park Service estimates that about 40 percent of Americans can trace ancestry through it.[^s12] Six years after it opened, the five boroughs merged into Greater New York. The consolidation referendum had passed 64,744 to 64,467, and opposition was strongest in Brooklyn, whose residents feared losing their identity and their Republican-led government to Manhattan's Democratic voters.[^s13]

**Twentieth century.** Black southerners of the Great Migration made Harlem, about three square miles in Manhattan, home to nearly 175,000 African Americans, the largest concentration of Black people in the world. From the end of World War I to the mid-1930s it was the center of the Harlem Renaissance, with Black-owned publishers, newspapers and music companies.[^s14] In the early hours of June 28, 1969, a police raid on the Stonewall Inn set off days of protest; the proclamation creating Stonewall National Monument notes that many consider the uprising the catalyst of the modern LGBT civil rights movement.[^s15] On September 11, 2001, terrorists destroyed the World Trade Center; 2,753 of the attacks' 2,977 victims died in New York.[^s16]

## Today

The 2020 census counted 8,804,190 New Yorkers, up 7.7 percent from 8,175,133 in 2010, a population peak for the city. Brooklyn (2,736,074) and Queens (2,405,464) were the largest boroughs, followed by Manhattan (1,694,251) and the Bronx (1,472,654), while Staten Island, the least populous, grew 5.8 percent. City planners put New York's diversity index at 76 percent against 61 percent for the nation as a whole (2020 census).[^s17] Its past is increasingly marked in public: the remains of 419 people excavated at the African Burial Ground in 1991–1992 were reinterred in 2003, and in 2006 the site became a national monument, the first dedicated to Africans of early New York and Americans of African descent.[^s7] Stonewall became a national monument in 2016,[^s15] and the National September 11 Memorial opened to the public on September 12, 2011.[^s16]

## Connections

New York City is located in New York State (`locatedIn`), and city planners rank it the nation's largest city in the 2020 census.[^s17] It borders the Hudson River (`borders`), whose link to the Erie Canal made its harbor the outlet for the interior's trade.[^s10] It stands on the homeland of the [[us:lenape]], whose 1626 dealings with the Dutch gave rise to the $24 legend.[^s4][^s5] Its colonial history is bound up with [[us:slavery-in-the-united-states]], remembered at the [[us:african-burial-ground-national-monument]].[^s7] It was a stronghold of the British in the [[us:american-revolutionary-war]] and a refuge for [[us:black-loyalists]],[^s8] and [[us:george-washington]] was inaugurated at [[us:federal-hall]].[^s9] Its harbor held [[us:ellis-island]], a symbol of [[us:immigration-to-the-united-states]],[^s12] and its neighborhoods were centers of the [[us:harlem-renaissance]] and the [[us:great-migration]][^s14] and the site of the [[us:stonewall-uprising]][^s15] and the [[us:september-11-attacks]].[^s16]

## Debates and interpretations

What the Lenape thought they were agreeing to in 1626 is uncertain, because only Dutch sources survive.[^s3] The city's archaeologists, drawing on recent research, suggest the Lenape probably understood the arrangement as temporary, more like a lease, with the goods serving as a ceremonial gift to seal it, and Smithsonian writers describe it as an agreement to share land that the Dutch treated as a sale.[^s4][^s6] Historian Andrew Lipman rejects the idea that Native people could not grasp land transfer: many leaders bounded, leased and sold land deliberately, and later deals were shaped less by trickery than by disease and unequal economic power, so the sellers "were hardly defeated dupes."[^s5] Both views reject the $24 legend: the 60 guilders recorded in 1626 were converted to $24 by a nineteenth-century historian, and the figure entered schoolbooks.[^s5][^s4]
