  myth: Americans in 1867 overwhelmingly ridiculed the Alaska purchase as "Seward's Folly."

review:
  - question: Why did Russia sell Alaska, and why is the story of universal American ridicule misleading?
    answer: The Crimean War had shown how vulnerable Russian America was, and the tsar sold it rather than risk losing it in another war. In the United States some critics mocked the deal, but the Senate approved the treaty 37–2, the House funded it 113–43, and much of the press favored it.
  - question: How did statehood lead to the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act?
    answer: The 1958 Statehood Act let the new state select about 105 million acres of federal land, but Alaska Native claims had never been settled. Native protests led the Interior Department to freeze selections in 1969, and the state and oil companies then pressed Congress, which in 1971 extinguished claims to more than 360 million acres in exchange for about 45 million acres and about $962.5 million, managed largely through Native corporations.
  - question: How did World War II affect the Unangax̂ (Aleut) people?
    answer: After Japan attacked the Aleutians in 1942, U.S. authorities evacuated 881 Unangax̂ to abandoned canneries and camps in Southeast Alaska without plumbing or electricity and often without medical care; at least 74 died in four camps, while some Unangax̂ men served in the armed forces.
  - question: Why was Alaska's statehood delayed, and how did Cold War concerns shape it?
    answer: Statehood bills were introduced year after year; Eisenhower supported statehood but wanted concerns settled first, including national security on the border with the Soviet Union and the fact that about 99 percent of the land was federally owned. A compromise on land transfers allowed the 1958 act to pass.

# Alaska

Alaska is the forty-ninth state of the United States, admitted by presidential proclamation on January 3, 1959, "on an equal footing with the other States of the Union."[^s6] It is the largest state by far, with about 571,000 square miles of land, and one of the most thinly settled: the 2020 census counted 733,391 people, about 1.3 per square mile.[^s7]

## Geography

Alaska stretches from the North Slope, site of the Prudhoe Bay oil field, south to Valdez and the islands of Southeast Alaska, and west along the Aleutian Islands.[^s16][^s11][^s12] Its highest summit, North America's tallest peak, measured 20,310 feet to the top of its ice cap in a 2015 GPS survey.[^s13] Its best-known name, Denali, is Koyukon Athabascan, one of many Native names for the mountain.[^s13] Federal naming has shifted: the name Denali was officially restored in 2015, and a January 2025 executive order and an Interior order reinstated "Mount McKinley" for federal use, while the surrounding park, renamed by Congress in 1980, remains Denali National Park and Preserve.[^s13][^s14] On March 27, 1964, a magnitude 9.2 earthquake on the Aleutian subduction zone, the largest ever recorded in the United States, and its tsunamis killed 129 people and badly damaged Anchorage.[^s12]

## History

**Alaska Native peoples.** People crossed into the Americas by way of Beringia, the land that once joined Asia and Alaska, and some who stayed became the ancestors of today's Inupiat, Yupik, Unangax̂ and Athabascan peoples.[^s9] Alaska Native people are often grouped into eleven cultures speaking about twenty languages, among them Athabascans; Yup'ik and Cup'ik; Inupiaq and St. Lawrence Island Yupik; Unangax̂ (Aleut) and Alutiiq (Sugpiaq); and the Eyak, Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian of the southeast.[^s10] The state's language council counts some 23 distinct Native languages, several now with few or no fluent speakers.[^s20]

**Russian America.** A Russian expedition under Vitus Bering reached Alaska in 1741, and Siberian fur traders followed, taking hostages and forcing Unangax̂ and Alutiiq people to hunt sea otters for them.[^s8] In 1799 the tsar chartered the Russian-American Company to run the colony. The Sitka Tlingit destroyed a Russian post in 1802; in 1804 the Russians returned, and after the Tlingit withdrew, Alexander Baranov founded New Archangel at Sitka as the colonial capital.[^s8]

**Purchase.** The Crimean War exposed how hard the colony would be to defend, and Russia chose to sell.[^s8] Secretary of State William H. Seward signed the treaty on March 30, 1867, for $7.2 million, less than two cents an acre for nearly 600,000 square miles.[^s1] The treaty offered citizenship to inhabitants who stayed, except "uncivilized native tribes," who were made subject to federal Indian law—an early sign that Native land rights would go unresolved for a century.[^s1][^s15] The Senate approved it 37–2 on April 9, and the House, after delaying more than a year, voted the money 113–43 in July 1868.[^s2] Critics attacked the secret negotiations, and some newspapers mocked the deal as "Seward's folly" or "Seward's icebox."[^s2] Yet most of the press clippings and letters preserved in Seward's papers favored the acquisition, and one Philadelphia paper called it a significant asset.[^s3] As early as 1958, historian Richard Welch argued that textbook accounts casting Seward as the only American who saw Alaska's value were mistaken.[^s4]

**District and territory.** Gold drew newcomers: ore samples brought in by Auk Tlingit chief Kowee led prospectors to the 1880 strike that founded Juneau, and the 1896 Klondike strike in Canada's Yukon sent tens of thousands through Skagway and Dyea, followed by a rush to Nome in 1899.[^s18][^s19] The seat of government moved from Sitka to Juneau in 1906, and Alaska became a territory in 1912.[^s18][^s2] In 1942 Japan occupied Attu and Kiska.[^s2] U.S. authorities evacuated 881 Unangax̂ from nine villages to abandoned canneries and camps in Southeast Alaska, where at least 74 died of disease in four camps; servicemen burned the village of Atka to keep it from the enemy.[^s11] Alaska Native activists also fought racial discrimination: led by Elizabeth and Roy Peratrovich of the Alaska Native Sisterhood and Brotherhood, they won the territory's Anti-Discrimination Act on February 16, 1945, the first such law in Alaska's history.[^s17]

**Statehood.** Alaskans voted for statehood in 1946 and ratified a constitution on April 24, 1956.[^s2][^s6] Eisenhower supported statehood but first wanted Cold War security concerns on the Soviet frontier resolved, along with the problem that about 99 percent of the land was federally owned.[^s5] He signed the Statehood Act on July 7, 1958; voters accepted its terms on August 26, and admission followed on January 3, 1959.[^s5][^s6]

## Today

After the 1968 Prudhoe Bay discovery, Congress authorized the 800-mile trans-Alaska pipeline in 1973, oil began flowing on June 20, 1977, and by the 1990s North Slope royalties provided more than two-thirds of the state budget (University of Alaska Fairbanks, 1997).[^s16] The oil boom also forced a reckoning with Native land claims. The Statehood Act let the state select about 105 million acres, Native protests led the Interior Department to freeze selections in 1969, and the 1971 Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act extinguished claims to more than 360 million acres in return for about 45 million acres and about $962.5 million, distributed through twelve regional and over 200 village corporations.[^s15] More than 200 federally recognized tribes are located in Alaska (CRS, 2021).[^s15] The Census Bureau's QuickFacts profile puts the American Indian and Alaska Native share of residents at 15.6 percent and estimates the population at 737,270 on July 1, 2025.[^s7]
