# Wright brothers' first flight

On the morning of December 17, 1903, on a stretch of sand dunes on North Carolina's Outer Banks, Orville and Wilbur Wright made four short flights in an engine-driven biplane they had designed and built themselves. The flights are recognized as the first demonstration of sustained flight by a heavier-than-air machine under the pilot's complete control.[^s2][^s1] The first lasted twelve seconds; within a few years the brothers had turned that hop into a practical airplane.

## Background

The Wrights ran a bicycle business in Dayton, Ohio, and had been experimenting with flight since 1899.[^s1] They first became interested in flying through the work of the German aviator Otto Lilienthal, whose cambered wings and roughly 2,000 glides they built upon, and they corresponded often with the engineer Octave Chanute.[^s3][^s2] Seeking steady winds, soft sand and few trees, they wrote to Weather Bureau stations; only Kitty Hawk answered, and its assistant weatherman, Bill Tate, offered to host them. Their glider tests there ran from 1900 to 1903.[^s3][^s6]

Each season they returned to the Outer Banks each season with improved gliders, making over a thousand glides from Big Kill Devil Hill. In October 1902 Wilbur glided 622 feet in 21 seconds, an American record.[^s1][^s3] Local Outer Bankers helped throughout, and Dan Tate was their only paid employee.[^s3] Unable to buy a light enough engine, the brothers built their own and assembled the 1903 Flyer — about 40 feet long and 605 pounds, with two chain-driven pusher propellers — in sections in the back of their Dayton shop.[^s1][^s2] They filed a patent application for their "Flying-Machine" on March 23, 1903.[^s7]

The Wrights were not the only ones trying. Samuel Pierpont Langley, Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution, had a better-funded effort, but his Great Aerodrome crashed on launch for the second time on December 8, 1903.[^s4]

## What happened

A first attempt on December 14 failed: Wilbur, who had won a coin toss, oversteered with the elevator, and the Flyer stalled into the sand.[^s1] After repairs, on December 17 the brothers signalled the nearby lifesaving station, and five men came to watch — John T. Daniels, Adam D. Ethridge, William S. Dough, W. C. Brinkley and a teenager, John T. Moore.[^s3]

At 10:35 a.m., flying into a stiff headwind, Orville released the restraining wire while Wilbur ran alongside steadying a wing. The Flyer covered about 120 feet in 12 seconds, and Daniels, who had never taken a photograph before, caught the moment on a tripod camera.[^s1][^s3] The brothers took turns for three more flights; on the fourth, Wilbur flew 852 feet in 59 seconds. Soon afterwards a gust flipped the machine and damaged it so badly that it never flew again.[^s1] Daniels later recalled that at the time he "didn't think it amounted to much."[^s3]

The brothers walked about four miles to Kitty Hawk and telegraphed their father, Bishop Milton Wright, in Dayton: "Success four flights Thursday morning all against twenty one mile wind." The telegram gave the longest flight as 57 seconds, slightly less than the 59 seconds recorded in later accounts.[^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

The 1903 flights were a beginning, not a finished invention. In 1904 the Wrights moved to Huffman Prairie, a cow pasture eight miles east of Dayton, where a sturdier machine with a new engine was making half-mile flights and full turns by September 1904; the press largely ignored them.[^s8] Their patent was granted on May 22, 1906, and the Army signed a contract with them in 1908.[^s7][^s2]

Recognition of the 1903 flight was contested for decades. After Langley's death, the Smithsonian hired Glenn Curtiss — then in a patent dispute with the Wrights — to rebuild the Aerodrome; after extensive changes and a new engine it made brief straight-line hops in 1914, and the Smithsonian then labelled it the first man-carrying airplane capable of sustained flight.[^s4][^s5] Orville protested by shipping the Flyer to London's Science Museum in 1928. The Smithsonian published a correction in its 1942 annual report, and after Orville's death in January 1948 the Flyer came home, installed in Washington on December 17, 1948.[^s4]

At the dunes, Congress created the Kill Devil Hill National Memorial in 1927; the monument was completed in 1932, the National Park Service took over in 1933, and more than 30,000 people attended the centennial in 2003.[^s6]

## Connections

The flights took place in **North Carolina**, on the Outer Banks near Kitty Hawk.[^s1] They **led to** the memorial now called **Wright Brothers National Memorial**, authorized in 1927.[^s6] The brothers' wing design was **influenced by** Otto Lilienthal's gliding experiments.[^s3] Their success **led to** the 1904–1905 campaign at **Huffman Prairie**, where the 1903 design was rebuilt into a sturdier, more controllable machine.[^s8] The story also connects to the **Smithsonian Institution** and **Samuel Pierpont Langley**, whose rival Aerodrome lay at the heart of a long dispute over credit.[^s4][^s5]
