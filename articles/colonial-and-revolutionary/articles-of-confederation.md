# Articles of Confederation

The Articles of Confederation were the first constitution of the United States. Adopted by the Second Continental Congress on November 15, 1777, and in force from March 1, 1781, until 1789, they bound the thirteen states into what the text called a "league of friendship," in which each state kept its sovereignty and every power not expressly delegated to Congress.[^s1] Their weaknesses, above all Congress's lack of any power to tax, persuaded many leaders to replace them with the Constitution.[^s1][^s3]

## Background

Benjamin Franklin had proposed a confederation in 1775, but Congress tabled it.[^s3] On June 11, 1776, after the Lee Resolution proposed independence, Congress appointed a committee with one member from each colony to plan a confederation; John Dickinson of Delaware was its principal writer, and his draft gave the union the name the United States of America.[^s1] Delegates split over representation by population or by state, and final debate waited until October 1777, after the British captured Philadelphia.[^s3]

Ratification required all thirteen states. Virginia ratified first, on December 16, 1777, but Maryland, Delaware and New Jersey held out, wanting states with western land claims to give them up. New Jersey and Delaware relented by early 1779; Maryland agreed only on March 1, 1781, after Virginia consented to relinquish its western claims, and the Confederation Congress came into being that day.[^s3][^s1]

## What it says

The Articles created a single national body, Congress, in which each state had one vote whatever its size. State legislatures appointed between two and seven delegates a year and could recall them.[^s1][^s2] Congress controlled war and peace, foreign alliances, coinage and the post office, could borrow money, and served as the final court of appeal in boundary disputes between states, but the most important decisions required the assent of nine states.[^s1][^s2] There was no independent national executive or judiciary; Congress chose one of its own members to preside, and a committee of one delegate per state could sit during recesses.[^s2] States could not make treaties or wage war without Congress's consent unless invaded.[^s2][^s1]

Expenses were to come from a common treasury, with each state's share set by the value of its surveyed land, but only the state legislatures could levy the taxes.[^s2][^s1] Free inhabitants of each state received the privileges and immunities of citizens in the others, a guarantee whose wording left out enslaved people as well as paupers, vagabonds and fugitives from justice.[^s2] Amendments required approval in Congress and confirmation by every state legislature.[^s1] The weakness was by design: as the failed Albany Plan had shown, many Americans feared losing power to a central body, and Article II reserved to each state its sovereignty and every power not expressly delegated.[^s3][^s2]

For Native nations, the Articles gave Congress power over "managing all affairs with the Indians, not members of any of the States," a qualification that invited conflict.[^s2] In 1784 New York claimed the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy as within its jurisdiction and held its own council at Fort Stanwix; in October, Confederation commissioners told the roughly 600 Haudenosaunee present that they were a subdued people, held six high-ranking leaders as hostages until American prisoners were released, and demanded a strip of land running south from Fort Niagara. Joseph Brant objected that two separate bodies were claiming to manage Indian affairs, and nations left out of the talks, including the Delaware, Shawnee and Mingo, later tried to disavow the treaty as coerced, complaints Congress ignored. New York then sold land it had failed to obtain by treaty, exposing how little Congress could enforce.[^s6]

## Legacy

Without taxing power, Congress depended on requisitions: according to Mount Vernon's Digital Encyclopedia, between 1781 and 1787 it received about $1.5 million of the $10 million it requested from the states, and a 1783 proposal for a national tariff on imports died when New York rejected it in 1787.[^s4] Congress could not make states honor the 1783 Treaty of Paris clause on British creditors, and Britain kept its Great Lakes forts in response.[^s3] When indebted farmers in western Massachusetts, many of them Revolutionary War veterans, rose in Shays' Rebellion, Congress had no army, and a privately financed Massachusetts force put the uprising down.[^s4][^s3] George Washington and James Madison feared the union was near collapse.[^s1]

Yet the Confederation also had achievements: Congress created departments of Foreign Affairs, War, Marine and Treasury, established post offices, and its Land Ordinances of 1784 and 1785 and Northwest Ordinance of 1787 set a process for adding new states equal to the original thirteen and provided for public education.[^s4][^s3] In May 1787 a convention met in Philadelphia to revise the Articles and instead wrote a new constitution.[^s1] Article II's reservation of powers to the states became the basis for the Tenth Amendment in 1791.[^s4] The signed parchment original is held by the National Archives.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians have long argued about how badly the Confederation failed. John Fiske's *The Critical Period of American History* (1888) portrayed the 1780s as a crisis of weak government, depression and unrest from which the Constitution rescued the republic, much as Washington and Madison saw it at the time.[^s5][^s1] Charles Beard (1913) argued that the crisis mattered mainly to creditors and commercial interests who wanted a stronger government to protect property, and later suggested the Articles might have served as a workable permanent government; Merrill Jensen's *The New Nation* (1950) described the decade as a hopeful time and the Constitution as a limit on democracy.[^s5] Robert E. Brown and Forrest McDonald challenged Beard in the 1950s, and Gordon Wood (1969) stressed elites' alarm at perceived democratic excess.[^s5] Recent accounts, such as Mount Vernon's, weigh real weaknesses against real achievements.[^s4]

## Connections

The Articles were **authored by** John Dickinson for a committee of the Second Continental Congress soon after the Declaration of Independence.[^s1] Their failures **led to** the Constitutional Convention, and they **contrast with** the stronger Constitution that replaced them.[^s3][^s1] Their Indian-affairs clause **affected** the Haudenosaunee at Fort Stanwix,[^s6] and Article II **led to** the Tenth Amendment and its reservation of powers to the states.[^s4]
