# Bill of Rights

The Bill of Rights is the name Americans give to the first ten amendments to the U.S. Constitution. Congress proposed them on September 25, 1789, as part of a package of twelve articles, and they took effect when three-fourths of the state legislatures had ratified them on December 15, 1791.[^s1] They protect religious liberty, speech, the press, assembly and petition; address the militia and the right to keep and bear arms, the quartering of soldiers and searches and seizures; set out the rights of people accused of crimes, including due process, jury trial and counsel; bar excessive bail and cruel and unusual punishment; and close with two rules of interpretation: that listing some rights does not deny others retained by the people, and that powers not delegated to the federal government are reserved to the states or the people.[^s1]

## Background

The Constitution drafted in Philadelphia in 1787 contained no general declaration of rights. George Mason was one of three delegates present on the convention's last day who refused to sign for that reason, and his published objections helped rally opposition to the new government.[^s2] Opponents warned that a strong central government could become tyrannical, and memories of British violations of colonists' rights fed demands for written guarantees.[^s1] Supporters, Madison among them, answered that a bill of rights was unnecessary because the federal government could exercise only the powers the Constitution listed.[^s2] When ratification was in danger in Massachusetts, Federalists agreed to a compromise: states would ratify while recommending amendments for the First Congress to consider.[^s2] Michael Klarman, reviewing Pauline Maier's history of ratification, calls the convention's unanimous rejection of Mason's proposal a serious strategic error that this concession had to repair.[^s8]

## What it says

On June 8, 1789, Madison, once one of the most vocal opponents of a bill of rights, introduced amendments in the House, which met at Federal Hall in New York.[^s2] He told colleagues that many citizens remained dissatisfied, that moderate amendments could reassure them and draw in the two states still outside the Union, and that written guarantees, though only "paper barriers against the power of the community," could still restrain majorities and give independent courts something to enforce.[^s4] He also proposed that no state could violate equal rights of conscience, freedom of the press or jury trial in criminal cases.[^s4] Madison wanted his changes woven into the original text; Roger Sherman of Connecticut insisted on a separate list at the end, and that format prevailed.[^s5] The House passed seventeen amendments, the Senate reduced them to twelve and dropped the clause binding the states, and a conference committee settled the final wording.[^s2][^s5]

Washington sent the twelve articles to the states on October 2, 1789.[^s2] Rhode Island's entry into the Union in May 1790 and Vermont's admission raised the number of approvals required, and Virginia supplied the deciding vote as the eleventh state on December 15, 1791.[^s5][^s3] Only the third through twelfth articles had been approved, and Jefferson notified the states on March 1, 1792, that ten amendments had been adopted.[^s5] The first article, on how many constituents each representative would serve, was approved by nine of fourteen states and never took effect.[^s11] The second, delaying changes in congressional pay until after an election, was finally ratified on May 7, 1992, as the Twenty-seventh Amendment.[^s1] Georgia rejected the amendments in 1789, and Massachusetts and Connecticut never completed action; all three ratified symbolically in March and April 1939, on the 150th anniversary of Congress's proposal.[^s6][^s5]

## Legacy

For its first century and more, the Bill of Rights restrained only the federal government. In Barron v. Baltimore (1833) the Supreme Court held that it did not bind the states, which meant, among other things, that Southern states could ban abolitionist speech without violating the U.S. Constitution while slavery remained lawful.[^s7] After the Civil War, Representative John Bingham and other Reconstruction Republicans framed the Fourteenth Amendment to protect such rights against the states, though early rulings narrowed its reach.[^s7] Beginning in earnest with Gitlow v. New York (1925), the Court applied guarantees to the states one at a time through the Due Process Clause; the 1960s added protections such as the exclusionary rule and the right to counsel, and McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010) added the Second Amendment.[^s7] The Third Amendment, the Fifth Amendment's grand jury right and the Seventh Amendment's civil jury right still apply only to the federal government.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

**Why Madison acted.** Madison's own speech presents the amendments as a way to reassure the public and limit abuses of power without weakening government's useful functions.[^s4] The National Archives likewise stresses that he came to see how much voters valued written protections and how they could teach citizens their rights.[^s2] Klarman emphasizes strategy: Madison had promised amendments to win his House seat, wanted to keep Federalist promises, and confined his proposals almost entirely to individual rights while rejecting the structural changes Antifederalists wanted, leading critics such as Aedanus Burke to call them "a tub thrown out to a whale."[^s8]

**When it became "the Bill of Rights."** Gerard Magliocca argues that contemporaries generally spoke simply of amendments to the Constitution, that the label "was not widely used for those provisions until after 1900," and that the amendments became an icon only with the New Deal and World War II.[^s9] Kurt Lash disputes this, finding that Americans commonly called the 1791 amendments the Bill of Rights from the founding through Reconstruction, while agreeing that the public came to see them less as limits on federal power and more as fundamental rights of citizenship.[^s10]

## Connections

The Bill of Rights **amends** the [[united-states-constitution]] and was **enacted by** the [[united-states-congress]].[^s1] It was **introduced by** [[james-madison]] in June 1789,[^s4] and its creation was **influenced by** [[george-mason]], whose objections helped make a bill of rights the price of ratification.[^s2] It **protects** [[due-process]] in the Fifth Amendment, a guarantee later extended against the states by the [[fourteenth-amendment]].[^s1][^s7] Its reach to the states is the story of the [[incorporation-of-the-bill-of-rights]], from [[barron-v-baltimore]] to [[mcdonald-v-city-of-chicago]].[^s7]
