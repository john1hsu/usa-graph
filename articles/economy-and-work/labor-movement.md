# Labor movement

The American labor movement is the effort by wage earners to win better pay, shorter hours, safer workplaces and a say over their jobs by acting together: forming unions, bargaining, striking, boycotting and pressing for protective laws. It has never been a single organization: craft unions, industrial unions and radical groups often disagreed about whom to admit and how to act.[^s1][^s4] At its mid-twentieth-century height about a third of nonfarm workers belonged to unions; in 2025 the Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 14.7 million members, 10.0 percent of wage and salary workers.[^s4][^s15]

## Origins

Among the best-documented early actions were the "turnouts" of young women in the textile mills of Lowell, Massachusetts. In February 1834 about 800 women walked out against a 12.5 percent wage cut and lost; in October 1836 some 1,500 to 2,000 struck for months against higher board charges and forced several mills to back down.[^s2] In *Commonwealth v. Hunt* (1842) Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw of Massachusetts held that combining in a union was not in itself a criminal conspiracy and that workers could use peaceful pressure to raise wages.[^s3]

The Knights of Labor, founded by Philadelphia garment cutters in 1869, grew under Uriah Stephens and Terence Powderly into a "One Big Union" open to nearly all wage workers, including women, African Americans, Native Americans and immigrants, and calling for the eight-hour day and equal pay for women.[^s1][^s4] Its solidarity had limits. At Rock Springs, Wyoming Territory, on September 2, 1885, white miners who had met at the Knights of Labor hall killed 28 Chinese miners whom the Union Pacific employed at lower wages; no one was charged.[^s7]

## Goals and methods

Unions called a national strike for the eight-hour day on May 1, 1886, and more than 30,000 Chicago workers struck.[^s6] At a protest in Haymarket Square on May 4 someone threw a bomb at police, killing an officer; seven anarchist speakers were sentenced to death, and although the Knights had no part in it, the press blamed them. The Knights fell from more than 700,000 members to about 100,000 by the end of 1886.[^s1] That year Samuel Gompers and twenty craft unions founded the American Federation of Labor (AFL), which focused on skilled workers' wages and conditions and left most factory hands unorganized; it grew to four million members by 1920.[^s1][^s12]

Employers fought back with lockouts, strikebreakers, private guards and "yellow-dog" contracts. At Homestead in 1892 a gunfight with Pinkerton guards left nine dead before militia broke the strike, and in 1894 federal troops ended the Pullman strike led by Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union.[^s1] Craft unions mostly excluded women and Black workers, so many organized separately.[^s4] The Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, founded in 1925 and led by A. Philip Randolph, won a contract from the Pullman Company in 1937, the first major agreement between an African American union and a corporation.[^s8]

The National Labor Relations (Wagner) Act, signed on July 5, 1935, guaranteed workers' right to form and join unions, required employers to bargain with a union chosen by a majority, and created the National Labor Relations Board to enforce it.[^s9] That year John L. Lewis of the United Mine Workers led the Committee for Industrial Organization to organize whole industries such as steel, autos and rubber; it became the independent Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) in 1938.[^s12] Its breakthrough came in Flint, Michigan, where auto workers occupied General Motors plants from December 30, 1936, until GM reached an agreement with the United Automobile Workers on February 11, 1937; strikers' relatives formed a Women's Emergency Brigade to support them.[^s11]

## Outcomes

Union density rose from about 21 percent of nonfarm workers in 1939 to a peak near 35 percent in 1945.[^s4] In June 1947 Congress passed the Taft-Hartley Act over the veto of President Harry S. Truman, who called it "unworkable"; its backers kept the policy of encouraging collective bargaining but made unions subject to unfair labor practice charges and called for ending union practices that impair commerce.[^s10] After the CIO expelled 11 unions it called Communist-dominated in 1949–50, the AFL and CIO merged in 1955 as the AFL-CIO under George Meany.[^s12]

Addressing the AFL-CIO in December 1961, Martin Luther King Jr. called labor and civil rights "the two most dynamic and cohesive liberal forces in the country" and urged it to confront discrimination in its ranks.[^s13] King was killed in 1968 while supporting about 1,300 striking Black sanitation workers in Memphis.[^s13] In the fields, more than 800 Filipino farmworkers led by Larry Itliong began the Delano grape strike on September 8, 1965; César Chávez's National Farm Workers Association voted to join eight days later, and a consumer boycott won the union's first contract.[^s14]

In 2025, 32.9 percent of public-sector workers but only 5.9 percent of private-sector workers belonged to unions, and total membership was below the 17.7 million of 1983.[^s15] Labor's legacy also survives in the calendar: New York's Central Labor Union held the first Labor Day parade on September 5, 1882, and Congress made it a national holiday on June 28, 1894.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

Scholars disagree about why American labor was weaker than its counterparts abroad and why it declined. Gerald Friedman attributes the weakness to the AFL's political strategy, employer and government repression, racial divisions and court injunctions, arguing that unions advanced only when government sheltered them.[^s4] OpenStax's textbook instead names a cultural obstacle, the public's faith in individual hard work over collective action.[^s1] On the postwar decline, Friedman weighs structural change, such as foreign competition and industry moving South, against employer opposition, citing Paul Weiler's finding that unfair labor practice violations tripled in the 1970s, and weak legal protection.[^s4] Economists Henry Farber, Daniel Herbst, Ilyana Kuziemko and Suresh Naidu find that unions narrowed inequality, accounting for about a quarter of the fall in the Gini coefficient between 1936 and 1968.[^s16]

## Connections

The movement took shape during the Gilded Age, with Samuel Gompers leading the AFL until 1924 and A. Philip Randolph leading a pioneering Black union.[^s1][^s8] It led to Labor Day and to May Day, which began as an American eight-hour strike before spreading worldwide.[^s5][^s6] Through the high unionization of its peak decades it affected income inequality in the United States.[^s16]
