# George Washington

George Washington was a Virginia planter, surveyor and soldier who led the Continental Army through the American Revolutionary War, presided over the convention that wrote the Constitution, and served as the first president of the United States from 1789 to 1797.[^s1][^s2] His fortune, like much of his public career, rested on the forced labor of hundreds of enslaved people and on the seizure of Native land between the Atlantic seaboard and the Mississippi.[^s1]

## Life and work

Washington was born on February 22, 1732 (February 11 under the Julian calendar then in use), at Popes Creek farm in Westmoreland County, Virginia.[^s4][^s5] His father, Augustine, died when George was eleven, and with help from the powerful Fairfax family he took up surveying, completing nearly 200 surveys within a few years.[^s1][^s2]

As a young Virginia officer he was sent into the Ohio Country, where his surrender of Fort Necessity on July 3, 1754, helped open the Seven Years' War (the French and Indian War). He served as an aide to General Edward Braddock and commanded the Virginia Regiment until the end of 1758.[^s2][^s1] Early in 1759 he married Martha Dandridge Custis, a wealthy widow, and took his seat in the House of Burgesses; they raised her two children but had none together.[^s2][^s7] He had leased Mount Vernon after his half-brother Lawrence died in 1752 and inherited it in 1761.[^s1][^s2]

Washington attended the First Continental Congress in 1774 and returned to the Second after fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord. Congress created the Continental Army on June 14, 1775, and named him commander in chief the next day.[^s1][^s2][^s4] He lost more battles than he won, but led the army for eight years and won key victories at Trenton in December 1776 and at Yorktown, where Lord Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, 1781.[^s1][^s7][^s4] He first barred Black men from the army, accepted free Black recruits in 1776, and later approved recruiting Black soldiers in Rhode Island.[^s4] On December 23, 1783, he returned his commission to Congress at Annapolis.[^s4][^s2]

Worried by the weakness of the Articles of Confederation, he was elected president of the Constitutional Convention on May 25, 1787, and attended every session.[^s2][^s4] Presidential electors chose him unanimously on February 4, 1789, and he took the oath at Federal Hall in New York City on April 30; by then he had one tooth of his own left.[^s4][^s8] Aware that each act would set a precedent, he wrote, "I walk on untrodden ground."[^s4] His administration created the cabinet and a federal financial system, restored national credit, and began executive privilege and the annual message to Congress.[^s1] He was re-elected unanimously in December 1792.[^s4]

Abroad, Washington proclaimed neutrality in April 1793 when France and Britain went to war. John Jay's treaty with Britain, signed on November 19, 1794, and ratified by the Senate in June 1795, secured British withdrawal from western forts but divided the country.[^s3] At home, Washington called out a force as large as his wartime army to suppress the Whiskey Rebellion (1791–1794), an armed revolt in the Pennsylvania backcountry against a federal excise tax on whiskey.[^s4]

In the Northwest, Washington sent armies against the Western Confederacy of the Miami, Shawnee, Lenape, Potawatomi and other nations. On November 4, 1791, warriors led by the Miami leader Little Turtle routed Arthur St. Clair's army on the Wabash; of about 930 soldiers more than 630 were killed. Washington and Congress then built the Legion of the United States, which defeated the confederacy at Fallen Timbers on August 20, 1794. The Treaty of Greenville of August 3, 1795, took nearly two-thirds of present-day Ohio.[^s10]

Washington declined a third term. His Farewell Address, first printed in a Philadelphia newspaper on September 19, 1796, urged Americans to guard the Union against sectional and party divisions and to "steer clear of permanent Alliances" abroad.[^s11] He left office on March 4, 1797, and died at Mount Vernon on December 14, 1799, of an acute throat infection.[^s4][^s5]

## Slavery at Mount Vernon

In 1799, 317 enslaved people lived at Mount Vernon. Washington owned 123 of them; 153 were "dower" slaves from the estate of Martha's first husband, whom neither spouse could legally free, and 40 were rented from a neighbor.[^s6] His will freed the 123 after Martha's death, supported the elderly for life, and required that orphaned children be taught to read, write and learn a trade. Martha signed a deed of manumission in December 1800, and they became free on January 1, 1801; the dower slaves passed to the Custis grandchildren in bondage.[^s6] Even his dental care touched slavery: a 1784 entry in Lund Washington's Mount Vernon journal records "Cash pd Negroes for 9 Teeth," charged to the account of the dentist Jean Pierre Le Mayeur.[^s9] Mount Vernon finds no definitive evidence that Le Mayeur performed tooth transplants on Washington.[^s8]

## Legacy

Washington twice gave up power of his own accord: he resigned his command in 1783, and in 1796 he declined a third term, explaining in the Farewell Address that the nation's affairs were settled enough for him to retire.[^s4][^s11] Popular memory has also wrapped him in invention: Mason Locke Weems made up the cherry-tree story, and his dentures, of human teeth, ivory and metal, were never wooden.[^s7][^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

One debate concerns partisanship: Mount Vernon stresses that he joined no party and warned against party spirit, while Encyclopedia Virginia argues that, despite claiming to stand above faction, he sided with Hamilton against Jefferson and led a Federalist faction that voters rejected in 1800.[^s7][^s4] Scholars also still debate his generalship.[^s4] A third question is slavery: Encyclopedia Virginia traces a growing sense of its injustice from the late 1780s, while the Miller Center emphasizes that his wealth and public service depended on enslaved labor throughout his life.[^s4][^s1]

## Connections

Washington **held the office** of president for two terms and was a **member of** the Second Continental Congress.[^s1] He **participated in** the American Revolutionary War and the Constitutional Convention, and as president in the Northwest Indian War and the suppression of the Whiskey Rebellion.[^s2][^s10][^s4] His foreign policy ties him to the Neutrality Proclamation, the Jay Treaty and the Farewell Address.[^s3][^s11]
