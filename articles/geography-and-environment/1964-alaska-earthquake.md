# 1964 Alaska earthquake

At 5:36 p.m. on Good Friday, March 27, 1964, the ground beneath Prince William Sound in south-central Alaska began to shake, and it did not stop for minutes. The magnitude 9.2 earthquake was the largest ever recorded in the United States and the second-largest measured with modern instruments.[^s1][^s2] It was felt across most of mainland Alaska and as far as Seattle, registered on water-level recorders in 47 states, and set the whole Earth vibrating like a bell.[^s1]

## Background

Southern Alaska sits above the eastern end of the Aleutian Trench, where the Pacific sea floor dives beneath the North American continent; most of the state's people, ports, roads and other infrastructure lie close to it.[^s1] In 1964 the idea that Earth's surface is made of moving plates was still a hypothesis awaiting real-world proof.[^s12] Alaska had become a state only five years earlier, and many coastal towns—Valdez among them—had grown up on loose, water-soaked sediments; an engineer had warned of the Valdez site's instability as early as 1899.[^s10]

## What happened

**The rupture.** The fault broke along about 580 miles in roughly 240 seconds, starting some 15 miles below the surface; the USGS puts the shaking at about four and a half minutes, while the duration estimated at Anchorage is about three.[^s2][^s3] Land rose as much as 38 feet in some places and sank as much as 8 feet in others, and much of coastal Alaska lurched at least 50 feet seaward.[^s2] Plafker's survey found vertical change over roughly 170,000 to 200,000 square kilometers, with the sea floor lifted as much as 15 meters.[^s4]

**Anchorage.** About 30 blocks of downtown buildings were damaged or destroyed, the six-story Four Seasons apartment building collapsed, and several schools were wrecked.[^s3] At Turnagain Heights a bluff above Knik Arm failed, sliding across about 130 acres and destroying roughly 75 houses.[^s3]

**The coast.** The deadliest blows came from water. At Valdez, liquefied waterfront soil gave way in an underwater landslide of about 97 million cubic yards, which swept away the docks; around 30 people died there, including crew of the ship *Chena*, which survived being dragged to the harbor bottom.[^s10][^s5][^s6] Seward and Whittier were wrecked by landslide waves, subsidence and burning oil.[^s2][^s5][^s6] At Chenega, a village of 68 people who spoke English and the Sugpiaq dialect of Alutiiq, a wave tens of feet high destroyed nearly everything but the schoolhouse on the hill; sources give the dead as 23 or 26, more than a third of the village.[^s5][^s11][^s6]

**Across the Pacific.** The tsunami raised by the uplifted sea floor reached Oregon about four hours after the quake. It swept four children out to sea at Beverly Beach near Newport and smashed wharves, houses and bridges from Seaside to Gold Beach; at Crescent City, California, it killed 12 people.[^s9] It also damaged Hawaii and Port Alberni in British Columbia.[^s3]

**The toll.** Counts differ slightly: a 2014 USGS fact sheet gives 129 deaths, while other USGS pages give 131, of whom 115 died in Alaska and 16 in Oregon and California.[^s1][^s2] Tsunamis caused about 119 to 122 of those deaths, many from local landslide-generated waves rather than the main ocean-crossing wave.[^s2][^s3][^s9] Property losses were about $311 million in 1964 dollars (USGS, about $3.1 billion in 2024 dollars).[^s3][^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

**Relief and rebuilding.** On March 28 President Lyndon B. Johnson declared Alaska a disaster area, directed federal agencies and the Alaska military command to assist, and promised that Americans would "pull together to meet and overcome this blow"; $5 million in federal relief was announced on April 4.[^s7] On April 2, Executive Order 11150 created a federal commission to plan reconstruction and Alaska's longer-term economic development.[^s8] Geologists judged the old Valdez waterfront unstable, and the town was rebuilt a few miles west on bedrock near Mineral Creek.[^s10][^s5] Chenega's survivors never returned to their old village; twenty years later they founded Chenega Bay on Evans Island.[^s11]

**Science.** Searching for a fault big enough to explain the damage, USGS geologist George Plafker found none at the surface and concluded the quake had struck a subduction zone, giving some of the first real proof that tectonic plates move.[^s12][^s4] His pattern—coast lifted above the shallow, seaward part of the rupture and lowered farther inland—became a key to recognizing subduction earthquakes worldwide.[^s2]

**Warnings.** The disaster led to the founding of the West Coast/Alaska Tsunami Warning Center at Palmer in 1967; its duties later grew to the whole U.S. West Coast and British Columbia, and it was renamed the National Tsunami Warning Center in 2013.[^s9] The USGS also credits the event with spurring its Earthquake Hazards Program, the Alaska Earthquake Center and national and global seismic networks.[^s2]
