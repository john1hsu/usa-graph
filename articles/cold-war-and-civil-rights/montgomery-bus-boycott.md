# Montgomery bus boycott

The Montgomery bus boycott was a mass protest in which Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama, refused to ride the city's segregated buses from December 5, 1955, until December 20, 1956 — 381 days.[^s4] Organized by the Women's Political Council and the newly formed Montgomery Improvement Association (MIA), led by a young minister, Martin Luther King Jr., it ended only after federal courts struck down Alabama's bus segregation laws in *Browder v. Gayle*.[^s1] It showed that sustained, nonviolent mass protest could defeat legally enforced segregation and made King a national figure.[^s1]

## Background

Under Montgomery's segregation ordinance, Black passengers sat at the back of the bus, and drivers routinely ordered them to surrender seats to white riders and could have those who refused arrested.[^s2] Black residents made up roughly three-quarters of regular riders, so the system depended on the people it humiliated.[^s6] The Women's Political Council, a group of Black professionals founded in 1946 and led by the teacher Jo Ann Robinson, met Mayor W. A. Gayle in March 1954 to ask for an end to standing over empty seats and to the practice of making Black riders pay at the front and board at the rear; in a May 1954 letter Robinson warned that local groups were discussing a boycott.[^s1] On March 2, 1955, fifteen-year-old Claudette Colvin was forcibly removed from a bus and arrested for defying segregation,[^s2] and eighteen-year-old Mary Louise Smith was arrested later that year for refusing to give up her seat; neither arrest led to a mass protest.[^s1]

## What happened

On December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks, a 42-year-old seamstress and secretary of the Montgomery NAACP branch, refused to give her seat to a white passenger and was arrested.[^s6] E. D. Nixon, a former head of the local NAACP, helped arrange her bail with Clifford and Virginia Durr, while Robinson and the council produced leaflets calling for a one-day boycott on December 5.[^s1] About 90 percent of Montgomery's Black riders stayed off the buses that day. That afternoon local leaders formed the MIA and elected King, a newly arrived preacher, as its president,[^s2] and an evening mass meeting at Holt Street Baptist Church voted to continue.[^s1] The MIA's first demands were modest — courteous treatment, first-come seating within a segregated system, and Black drivers on mainly Black routes — and the city refused them.[^s1]

Sustaining the protest took enormous daily effort. Some boycotters walked up to eight miles a day.[^s2] When the city penalized Black taxi drivers for carrying boycotters at bus fare and pressed insurers to drop Black car owners,[^s6] the MIA organized a carpool of about 300 cars on the model of the 1953 Baton Rouge bus boycott,[^s1] with roughly 100 pick-up stations funded by collections at church mass meetings.[^s2] The NPS estimates the bus company lost 30,000 to 40,000 fares a day.[^s2] Women such as Robinson, Johnnie Carr and Irene West kept the committees and volunteer networks running, and council member Mary Fair Burks credited "the nameless cooks and maids who walked endless miles for a year."[^s1]

Opponents answered with violence and the law. Segregationists bombed King's house on January 30, 1956, and later Nixon's.[^s1][^s3] Police ticketed carpool drivers for minor violations,[^s2] and in February more than 80 leaders were indicted under a 1921 state law against boycotts; King was convicted on March 22 and sentenced to pay $500 or serve 386 days.[^s1] Bayard Rustin and Glenn Smiley came to advise King on Gandhian nonviolence, and Rustin, Ella Baker and Stanley Levison raised funds in the North.[^s1]

On February 1, 1956, MIA attorneys Fred Gray and Charles D. Langford filed *Browder v. Gayle* in federal court for Aurelia Browder, Susie McDonald, Claudette Colvin, Mary Louise Smith and Jeanetta Reese, who withdrew under pressure.[^s4][^s5] Parks was left out because her own case was still in the state courts and Gray wanted the federal court to face a single question.[^s3] On June 5, 1956, a three-judge court ruled 2–1, citing *Brown v. Board of Education*, that bus segregation violated the Equal Protection Clause, with Judge Seybourn Lynne dissenting.[^s3] The city appealed and kept the buses segregated.[^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

The Supreme Court affirmed the ruling on November 13, 1956, and rejected requests to reconsider on December 17. When the order reached Montgomery on December 20, the MIA voted to end the boycott, and the buses were integrated the next morning.[^s4] Parks, who helped organize the protest, later said the only tired she felt was "tired of giving in."[^s7] The boycott became a model for nonviolent campaigns across the South, and the National Park Service describes it as the start of the modern civil rights movement, with later campaigns in Birmingham, Selma and Memphis following its example.[^s1][^s2] Its leaders built on earlier efforts such as the Baton Rouge boycott and the hope that *Brown* had raised.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

The boycott **occurred in** Montgomery, Alabama, and drew on the precedent of *Brown v. Board of Education*, by which it was **influenced**.[^s2] It was **led by** Martin Luther King Jr. as president of the MIA and was **influenced by** the 1953 Baton Rouge boycott's carpool.[^s1] Its legal arm **led to** *Browder v. Gayle*, which ended bus segregation in Alabama.[^s2] It resisted the segregation established by Jim Crow laws and forms part of the wider civil rights movement, alongside activists such as Rosa Parks, Claudette Colvin, Jo Ann Robinson, E. D. Nixon and Fred Gray.[^s1][^s3]
