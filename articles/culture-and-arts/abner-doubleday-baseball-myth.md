# Abner Doubleday baseball myth

For much of the twentieth century Americans were taught that baseball was invented in a single summer, in 1839, by a young Abner Doubleday in the village of Cooperstown, New York. The story is still repeated, but the National Park Service describes Doubleday as a soldier "mistakenly credited as the inventor of baseball," and the National Baseball Hall of Fame in Cooperstown itself calls the Doubleday story a refuted myth.[^s1][^s3]

## The myth

The tale took shape in a quarrel over national origins. In 1903 Henry Chadwick, the English-born writer who edited Spalding's *Official Base Ball Guide*, argued that baseball had grown out of the English game of rounders.[^s4][^s5] Albert G. Spalding, a former star pitcher, sporting-goods magnate and part owner of the Chicago White Stockings, rejected that view; in a letter he insisted the game must have an "American Dad."[^s6] To settle the matter he appointed a commission in 1905, chaired by former National League president Abraham G. Mills and including figures such as Morgan G. Bulkeley and the amateur athletics official James E. Sullivan, who gathered the evidence.[^s4][^s5]

The decisive testimony came from Abner Graves, a 71-year-old mining engineer living in Denver, who wrote to an Akron newspaper and to Spalding that Doubleday had devised the game at Cooperstown and that the two had been schoolmates there.[^s3][^s6][^s4] In a report dated December 30, 1907—in substance an informal letter from Mills to Sullivan—Mills concluded that the first diagram of the field was "drawn by Abner Doubleday in Cooperstown, N.Y. in 1839."[^s4] Spalding published the findings in 1908, and Cooperstown became the game's official birthplace.[^s5]

## What the evidence shows

Doubleday's own life rules him out. Born in Ballston Spa, New York, on June 26, 1819, he had been a cadet at the U.S. Military Academy at West Point since September 1, 1838, and was not in Cooperstown in 1839; his family had left the village two years earlier.[^s1][^s4] He graduated in 1842 and made a name in the Civil War: he aimed the first U.S. shot returned at Fort Sumter on April 12, 1861, and on the first day at Gettysburg took command after General John Reynolds was killed, slowing the Confederate advance.[^s1][^s6] He died on January 26, 1893.[^s1] Doubleday left 67 diaries and wrote articles in retirement, and none of them mentions baseball; his only recorded link to the game is an 1871 request, as an officer in Texas, to buy baseball equipment for his soldiers.[^s4][^s5]

The commission's witness was weak. Graves was born on February 27, 1834, so he was five years old in the summer he described.[^s4] Mills had known Doubleday for some thirty years yet first heard of the claim more than a decade after his friend's death, and the letters the commission received were destroyed in a fire in 1911, so whatever else it relied on cannot be checked.[^s4] SABR's biographers put it bluntly: "No serious historian of baseball believes Doubleday established the rules."[^s5]

The game itself is older than 1839. The Library of Congress holds a diary entry of March 22, 1786, in which a College of New Jersey student recorded playing "baste ball" on campus, and an American edition (1787) of *A Little Pretty Pocket-Book*, a children's book first printed in London in 1744, whose rhyme and woodcut titled "Base-Ball" show players at bases.[^s2] Organized play is documented in New York, where the Knickerbocker club adopted written rules in 1845, and the first recorded game under them was played at the Elysian Fields in Hoboken, New Jersey, on June 19, 1846.[^s4] Most baseball historians now trace the sport to English and other bat-and-ball games that changed gradually, rather than to any single inventor.[^s5]

## Why it persists

The story met a need. A sport being promoted as the national pastime gained a native-born father who was also a Union war hero, and the claim came wrapped in the authority of an official-sounding commission.[^s6][^s5] Cooperstown then built its identity around it. In 1935 a battered homemade ball found in a farmhouse in nearby Fly Creek was bought for five dollars by Stephen C. Clark, a wealthy Cooperstown resident eager to draw visitors, and became the museum's first accessioned item, the "Doubleday Baseball."[^s4][^s3] Clark worked with National League president Ford Frick to found a hall of fame; its first class was elected in 1936, and the museum opened on June 12, 1939, as the sport paused for what was billed as "Baseball's Centennial."[^s3]

Even in 1939 the record was pushing back. A grandson of Alexander Cartwright wrote that his grandfather, a founding member of the Knickerbockers, had drawn the first diamond and written the 1845 rules, and organizers added a Cartwright day to the centennial and gave him a plaque in the Hall.[^s4] The Hall today states plainly that Doubleday was at West Point in 1839, while acknowledging that the myth remains woven into Cooperstown's sense of itself.[^s3]

## Connections

The myth is a story about **baseball**'s origins and depends on the game's history for context.[^s5] It was **introduced by Abner Graves**, whose 1905 letter was the commission's main evidence.[^s6] It is **challenged by** *A Little Pretty Pocket-Book*, whose "Base-Ball" rhyme predates 1839 by nearly a century, and by the **Knickerbocker rules** of 1845, the first written rules of the modern game.[^s2][^s4] It **led to** the founding of the **National Baseball Hall of Fame and Museum** in Cooperstown and its 1939 opening.[^s3] It involves the real Union officer **Abner Doubleday**, whose service at **Fort Sumter** and **Gettysburg** in the **Civil War** made him an attractive hero for the story, and the rival claims of **Albert Spalding** and **Henry Chadwick** about whether baseball was English or American—a debate about **American national identity**.[^s1][^s6][^s4] Like the **Betsy Ross flag myth**, it is a founding legend that entered public memory decades after the events it describes, from a single late recollection.[^s4]
