# Hawaii

Hawaii (Hawaiʻi) is the fiftieth state of the United States, proclaimed on August 21, 1959.[^s1] Its islands were home to a Polynesian people and then to an independent kingdom that the United States recognized by treaty; in 1893 that kingdom was overthrown with the help of the U.S. minister and U.S. naval forces, and in 1993 Congress formally apologized for that role.[^s2][^s3]

## Geography

The Hawaiian Islands are the young southeastern end of a chain of more than 125 volcanoes stretching about 3,720 miles across the north Pacific.[^s8] The volcanoes form over a stationary hot spot while the Pacific plate carries them northwest, so the islands grow older in that direction: Kauaʻi is roughly 5 to 5.5 million years old, while the Island of Hawaiʻi, home to the active volcanoes Kīlauea and Mauna Loa, is the youngest (as described by the U.S. Geological Survey in 1993).[^s8]

## History

**Polynesian settlement.** Voyagers from the Marquesas Islands crossed about 2,400 miles of open ocean in double-hulled canoes, navigating by sun, stars, winds, currents and seabirds; estimates of their arrival range widely, and recent carbon dating points to about 1000–1200 CE.[^s6] They brought pigs, dogs, chickens, taro, sweet potato, banana and sugar cane, and a distinct Hawaiian society developed, ruled by aliʻi (chiefs) and ordered by kapu, sacred restrictions on conduct and resource use.[^s6]

**The kingdom.** James Cook reached the islands in 1778, and by 1810 Kamehameha I had united them under one rule.[^s3] Kamehameha had built the temple of Puʻukoholā Heiau in 1790–91 during his wars of unification; after his death in 1819 his son Liholiho ended the kapu system.[^s7] The United States signed a treaty with the kingdom in 1826, opened a legation in 1853 and signed a Treaty of Reciprocity in 1875.[^s3] Sugar plantations recruited contract workers from Japan, China, the Philippines, Portugal and elsewhere; until 1900 workers were bound by three- to five-year contracts, and those who left could be jailed.[^s10] Introduced diseases devastated Native Hawaiians: estimates of the 1778 population range from about 200,000 to 1 million, and the census counted just under 24,000 by 1920.[^s13] Native Hawaiians made up 97 percent of the islands' people in 1853 and 16 percent in 1923.[^s10]

**Overthrow and annexation.** In 1887 white landowners and businessmen forced King Kalākaua to accept a constitution that stripped him of power.[^s4] On January 17, 1893, a Committee of Safety deposed Queen Liliʻuokalani, a day after U.S. naval forces landed near ʻIolani Palace; she yielded to the United States under protest, expecting her authority to be restored.[^s2] President Cleveland withdrew a first annexation treaty, and his investigator found the overthrow illegal, but the new government declared the Republic of Hawaii in 1894.[^s1] A second treaty was signed in 1897. Native Hawaiians answered with petitions bearing 21,269 signatures, more than half the Native Hawaiians counted that year, and the treaty failed in the Senate.[^s1] The women's petition, signed September 11, 1897, was written in English and ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi and declared that its signers "earnestly protest against the annexation."[^s5] During the Spanish–American War, Congress instead passed the Newlands Resolution by simple majorities; McKinley signed it on July 7, 1898, and formal annexation followed on August 12.[^s1][^s3] The Republic ceded about 1.8 million acres of crown, government and public lands.[^s2]

**Territory and war.** As a territory from 1900, Hawaiʻi had one non-voting delegate in Congress; its legislature asked for statehood in 1903 and was refused.[^s4][^s10] Japanese, Filipino, Chinese, Spanish and Portuguese workers formed the islands' first multiethnic union in 1920, and by 1923 Japanese Americans were the largest group.[^s10] On December 7, 1941, the day of the Pearl Harbor attack, the army pressed the territorial governor into declaring martial law, which lasted until late October 1944; military provost courts convicted defendants in 99 percent of 22,480 Honolulu trials in 1942–43, and in 1946 the Supreme Court ruled 6–2 that the tribunals lacked legal authority.[^s11]

**Statehood.** A 1937 congressional committee found Hawaiʻi qualified for statehood, but white plantation owners used their power to keep it a territory, and the Pearl Harbor attack halted talks.[^s4] After the war the House passed several statehood bills that died in the Senate, and Congress came to pair Hawaiʻi with Alaska; Alaska's admission in early 1959 opened the way.[^s4] Congress passed the statehood act in March 1959, island voters approved it in a June referendum, and Eisenhower proclaimed the new state on August 21.[^s1]

## Today

The 2020 census counted 1,455,271 residents, up 7.0 percent since 2010 (State of Hawaii, 2021).[^s9] Since 1978 the state constitution has made English and Hawaiian the official languages.[^s14] The 1993 Apology Resolution acknowledged that U.S. agents and citizens took part in the overthrow and denied Native Hawaiians self-determination, while stating that it settles no claims against the United States.[^s2] The rediscovery of the 1897 petitions in the National Archives, by scholar Noenoe Silva, has encouraged Hawaiian political activism; Silva says they refute the "myth that they passively gave up their country."[^s12]
