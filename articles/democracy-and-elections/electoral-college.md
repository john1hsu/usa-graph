    answer: The president is chosen by state-allotted electors, and 48 states and the District of Columbia give all their electors to the statewide winner, so narrow wins in some states can outweigh large margins elsewhere, as in 1876, 1888, 2000 and 2016.

# Electoral College

The Electoral College is the method the Constitution uses to choose the president and vice president. Americans do not vote for president directly: in each state they choose a slate of electors, and those electors cast the votes that decide the election.[^s1] There are 538 electors, one for each member of the House of Representatives and Senate plus three for the District of Columbia, and a candidate needs a majority of 270 to win.[^s1] The phrase "electoral college" itself never appears in the Constitution, and changing the system would require a constitutional amendment.[^s3]

## Origins

The delegates at the 1787 Constitutional Convention repeatedly voted for Congress to pick the executive and twice rejected direct popular election before settling on electors late in the summer, as a compromise between the two.[^s4] Many delegates doubted that voters across a large country could judge national candidates, and Alexander Hamilton defended the plan in *Federalist* 68 as one that drew on the sense of the people while leaving the final choice to a small body chosen for the task.[^s9] Supporters also feared that a president chosen by Congress would be subservient to it.[^s13]

Slavery shaped the debate. On July 19, 1787, James Madison said the people at large were the best choosers in principle, but that suffrage was far broader in the North, so the South "could have no influence in the election on the score of the Negroes"; electors, he concluded, raised the fewest objections.[^s7] Because each state's electors equal its seats in Congress, and House seats counted three-fifths of enslaved people, slaveholding states gained electoral votes for people who could not vote.[^s12]

The original rules soon failed. Each elector cast two votes without saying which was for president, so in 1796 the Federalist John Adams became president with his rival Thomas Jefferson as vice president.[^s9] In 1800 Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr tied at 73 votes each, sending the choice to the Federalist-controlled House, which elected Jefferson on the 36th ballot on February 17, 1801.[^s8] Congress then proposed the Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, which gave electors separate votes for president and vice president.[^s9][^s5]

## Powers and structure

Article II leaves the manner of appointing electors to each state legislature. Early legislatures often chose electors themselves, but by 1832 almost every state let its voters choose, and political parties came to name each slate.[^s4] Today 48 states and the District of Columbia give all their electors to the statewide winner, while Maine and Nebraska use a district-based variation.[^s2][^s1] The Twenty-third Amendment treats the District of Columbia like a state for this purpose.[^s1]

After the November election each state's governor sends a Certificate of Ascertainment naming its electors. The electors meet in their own states in mid-December, cast separate ballots for president and vice president, and send Certificates of Vote to Congress, which counts them in a joint session, usually on January 6, with the vice president presiding in a ministerial role.[^s1]

If no candidate wins a majority, the House chooses the president from the top three, with each state delegation casting one vote and 26 needed to win; the Senate chooses the vice president from the top two.[^s2][^s5] The House last did so in 1825, choosing John Quincy Adams although Andrew Jackson had led with 99 electoral votes to Adams's 84.[^s10] The Senate has picked a vice president only once, electing Richard M. Johnson 33 to 16.[^s3]

No clause of the Constitution or federal statute requires electors to follow the popular vote, but many states do.[^s2] Electors have cast about 180 "faithless" votes since the founding, out of more than 23,000, and over a third came in 1872 after the candidate Horace Greeley died.[^s6] In *Chiafalo v. Washington* (2020), a case about three Washington electors fined $1,000 for breaking their 2016 pledges, the Supreme Court held that states may enforce such pledges.[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians disagree about how much slavery explains the system's origins. The legal scholar Akhil Reed Amar has argued that Madison's July 19 reasoning shows the South needed an indirect system that counted enslaved people,[^s11] and that a slave state gained electoral votes as it held more people in bondage; the American studies scholar Christopher Petrella describes the design as protecting the political power of slaveholders; after the 1800 census, he notes, Pennsylvania had about 10 percent more free people than Virginia but about 20 percent fewer electoral votes.[^s12] The Princeton historian Sean Wilentz calls the "proslavery ploy" thesis a myth: the real alternative to electors was selection by Congress, which also counted the three-fifths bonus; direct election had been defeated two days before Madison spoke; and lower-South delegates actually opposed electors.[^s11] Both sides agree that the three-fifths rule increased slaveholding states' weight once the system was in place.[^s11][^s12]

Present-day arguments are also divided. Defenders such as the political scientist James Ceaser say the system preserves state-based federalism and pushes candidates to build support across the country; critics such as Jamin Raskin say it concentrates campaigns in a few swing states and can elect a candidate who lost the national popular vote, as happened in 1824, 1876, 1888, 2000 and 2016.[^s13] Congress has seen more than 700 proposals to reform or abolish it.[^s3]

## Connections

The Electoral College was established by the [[United States Constitution]] in Article II.[^s4] Its two-vote rule produced the tied [[election of 1800]], and that crisis led Congress to propose the [[Twelfth Amendment]].[^s5][^s9] Because electors follow congressional apportionment, it was shaped by the [[three-fifths clause]] and by [[slavery in the United States]], which Madison cited when he favored electors.[^s7][^s12] It also links to the [[House of Representatives]], which decides contingent elections, and to [[Chiafalo v. Washington]], which upheld state pledge laws.[^s2][^s6]
