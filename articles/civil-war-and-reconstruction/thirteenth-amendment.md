# Thirteenth Amendment

The Thirteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution abolished slavery and involuntary servitude everywhere in the United States and in any place under its jurisdiction, with an exception for punishment of a person duly convicted of a crime. Passed by Congress in January 1865 and ratified by the required three-fourths of the states on December 6, 1865, it was the first of the three Civil War, or Reconstruction, amendments, followed by the Fourteenth and Fifteenth, which together greatly expanded civil rights in the United States.[^s1] It also superseded part of Article IV, Section 2, of the original Constitution.[^s1]

## Background

On the eve of the Civil War, Congress was moving in the opposite direction. In 1861 both houses passed the Corwin Amendment, which would have shielded slavery inside the states from federal interference; President James Buchanan signed the joint resolution, but secession ended its chances and it was never ratified. Had it been, it would have become the Thirteenth Amendment.[^s5]

The war changed the question. Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation took effect on January 1, 1863, declaring free the people held as slaves in areas then in rebellion. It did not reach the loyal border states, and it exempted Confederate areas already under Union control.[^s1][^s5] Because it rested on the president's war powers, many doubted it would survive once the seceded states returned, and Congress's power to abolish slavery by ordinary statute was also in doubt; Lincoln concluded that only a constitutional amendment could end slavery for good.[^s1]

Pressure came from outside Congress as well. After the proclamation, Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony founded the Women's National Loyal League, whose members gathered nearly half a million signatures on petitions asking Congress to free all people of African descent held in bondage. Senator Charles Sumner let the petitions travel under his congressional frank, accepted a large batch in February 1864, and credited the League as a principal force behind the amendment.[^s4]

## Passage and ratification

In the Senate, Sumner proposed an amendment that would also have declared all persons equal before the law, but Judiciary Committee chairman Lyman Trumbull insisted that his committee handle the proposals. The committee chose narrower language and reported it on February 10, 1864, and the Senate passed the amendment on April 8, 1864, by 38 to 6, a coalition of 30 Republicans, four border-state Democrats and four Union Democrats.[^s3] The House twice fell short of the two-thirds majority, and the issue was set aside until after the 1864 election.[^s3] Lincoln had the amendment added to the Republican platform, and after his reelection a lame-duck House passed it on January 31, 1865, by 119 to 56.[^s1]

Lincoln approved the joint resolution on February 1, 1865, sending it to the states, although his signature was legally unnecessary: the Supreme Court had held in *Hollingsworth v. Virginia* (1798) that the president has no formal role in amending the Constitution.[^s1][^s5] Eighteen states ratified within a month, but momentum slowed after Lincoln's assassination in April 1865. President Andrew Johnson then made abolition a condition for Southern states seeking readmission to the Union, and Georgia's ratification on December 6, 1865, made it the 27th state, completing the three-fourths.[^s5][^s2] Secretary of State William H. Seward proclaimed the amendment ratified on December 18, 1865.[^s2]

## What it says

The amendment has two short sections. Section 1 provides that "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime" for which a person has been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States or any place subject to its jurisdiction. Section 2 gives Congress power to enforce the article by appropriate legislation.[^s1] By the time it took effect, wartime measures had already ended slavery in most of the country, but the amendment freed people still enslaved in Delaware and Kentucky and made abolition permanent and national rather than dependent on war powers or state law.[^s2]

## Legacy

The amendment was the nation's final constitutional answer to slavery and opened the way to the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.[^s1] Its exception for criminal punishment, however, left room for coerced labor. In Georgia, the state began leasing prisoners to private companies in 1868, starting with 100 African American prisoners sent to a railroad, 16 of whom died within the year; leased prisoners were overworked, whipped and killed, and the profitable system lasted until the legislature outlawed it in 1908, after which chain gangs took its place.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

Legal scholars and historians disagree about what the punishment clause was meant to allow. The prevailing reading, accepted by courts, holds that a criminal conviction removes a person's protection against slavery and involuntary servitude; legal scholar James Gray Pope traces that reading to former slaveholders and their Democratic allies in Congress. Pope argues instead that the amendment's Republican framers believed convicted people remained protected against servitude imposed for revenue, private profit or the control of Black labor, pointing out that the Thirty-Ninth Congress outlawed race-based convict leasing within months of ratification, and that this reading faded when Reconstruction ended rather than because it was rejected on the merits.[^s6]

## Connections

The amendment **amends** the United States Constitution and was **enacted by** the United States Congress, which passed it as a joint resolution before sending it to the states.[^s1] It **protects** freedom from slavery and involuntary servitude, the right its first section guarantees.[^s1] It was **signed by** Abraham Lincoln, even though no presidential signature was required.[^s5] It was **influenced by** the Emancipation Proclamation, whose limits convinced Lincoln that an amendment was needed.[^s1] It belongs to the Reconstruction amendments with the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, and its punishment exception is tied to the history of Black Codes and convict leasing in the postwar South.[^s1][^s7]
