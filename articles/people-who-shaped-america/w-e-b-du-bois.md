# W. E. B. Du Bois

William Edward Burghardt Du Bois (1868–1963) was a scholar and activist whose career ran from the end of Reconstruction to the eve of the March on Washington.[^s1] He was the first African American to earn a doctorate from Harvard, wrote sixteen books on sociology, history, politics and race, and is counted among the principal architects of the civil rights movement.[^s3][^s2] He argued that the color line was his century's defining problem and built organizations to fight it.[^s5][^s4]

## Life and work

Du Bois was born on February 23, 1868, in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, and in 1884 became the first African American to graduate from the town's high school.[^s1] He studied at Fisk University in Nashville, then entered Harvard as a junior, earning a BA in 1890 and an MA in 1891.[^s1] After two years at the University of Berlin, where he absorbed the new science of sociology, he received a Harvard PhD in 1895; his dissertation on the suppression of the African slave trade to the United States was published in 1896.[^s1][^s2]

He taught at Wilberforce University in Ohio, married Nina Gomer in 1896, and spent 1896–1897 studying Philadelphia's Seventh Ward, published as *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899).[^s1][^s2] From December 1897 he taught at Atlanta University and directed empirical studies of Black life in the South.[^s1] Personal loss and racial violence pushed him toward public action: his son Burghardt died of diphtheria in 1899 after being denied the best care because of race, and the same year he came close to witnessing the aftermath of a lynching.[^s2] In 1900 he exhibited data visualizations of Black American life at the Paris Exposition.[^s2]

*The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) made him nationally known.[^s6] Its forethought declared that "the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line," and its first essay described "double-consciousness," the sense of always seeing oneself through others' eyes.[^s5][^s6] One chapter challenged Booker T. Washington, the most powerful Black leader of the day, arguing that Washington's program asked Black Americans to give up political power, insistence on civil rights and higher education for their young.[^s5][^s9]

In 1905 Du Bois and William Monroe Trotter led twenty-nine activists to a meeting at Fort Erie, Ontario, that founded the Niagara Movement, whose declaration called for "persistent manly agitation" for equal rights.[^s7] Its 1906 meeting at Storer College in Harpers Ferry, honoring John Brown, was one Du Bois remembered as among the greatest Black meetings ever held.[^s3][^s7] The movement foundered amid disputes over women's role, Trotter's departure and money problems, and folded within a few years.[^s7] After the 1908 race riot in Springfield, Illinois, an interracial group called the National Negro Conference of May 31–June 1, 1909, where Du Bois spoke; the organization took the name National Association for the Advancement of Colored People in 1910, and Du Bois edited its monthly, *The Crisis*, from its launch that November.[^s4] He was the only Black member of the NAACP's original board.[^s2]

During the First World War Du Bois traveled to France to document the experiences of Black American soldiers, and afterward he helped organize Pan-African congresses, including one in Paris in 1919 on the future of Africa's colonies.[^s2][^s1] Debates in its pages over Marxist ideas and Black economic cooperatives deepened conflict with NAACP leader Walter White, and Du Bois resigned in June 1934.[^s1] Back at Atlanta University he published *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935), which argued that enslaved people helped free themselves and attacked the Dunning School historians who portrayed freed people as docile, unprepared for freedom and racially inferior.[^s1][^s10] He returned to the NAACP in 1944 and was forced out again in 1948.[^s1]

In 1950 he co-founded the Peace Information Center, which circulated the antinuclear Stockholm Appeal; the Justice Department demanded that its officers register as foreign agents, and on February 9, 1951, Du Bois was indicted with them.[^s1] He was acquitted that November, but he and his second wife, the writer Shirley Graham, were denied passports from 1952 to 1958.[^s1][^s2] In October 1961 he applied to join the Communist Party and left for Ghana at Kwame Nkrumah's invitation to direct the *Encyclopedia Africana*.[^s1] He became a Ghanaian citizen in 1963 and died in Ghana on August 27, the day before the March on Washington; Ghana gave him a state funeral.[^s1][^s2]

## Legacy

Du Bois's phrases, the color line, double consciousness and the veil, became touchstones for thinking about race in America.[^s6] The NAACP he helped found went on to campaign against lynching, documenting more than 3,400 lynchings between 1889 and 1922 in a 1922 newspaper campaign.[^s4] *Black Reconstruction*'s central thesis, ignored for decades, was taken up from the 1960s on and shaped later histories of emancipation.[^s10] Historian Thomas C. Holt calls him the most important modern African American intellectual, while also describing his later years as those of an unrepentant apologist for Joseph Stalin, a reminder that his politics remained controversial.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

The Washington–Du Bois conflict is often told as a clean split between accommodation and protest. Du Bois's own critique framed it that way, charging that Washington's program had helped bring disfranchisement and civil inferiority.[^s5][^s6] Gibson's account agrees the two men differed fundamentally over methods, and that Washington's "Tuskegee Machine" suppressed critics, but notes they shared the goal of full citizenship and that Washington secretly financed court challenges to segregation and voting restrictions.[^s9] On the "Talented Tenth," Gates notes that Du Bois took the idea of an educated vanguard from Henry Lyman Morehouse and used it to rebut Washington's emphasis on industrial training.[^s8] On *Black Reconstruction*, Mount describes broad later support for Du Bois's self-emancipation thesis, while noting that historians such as Eric Foner and Eugene Genovese doubted how far enslaved people freed themselves.[^s10]

## Connections

Du Bois **took part in** the **Niagara Movement** as its co-founder and leading voice.[^s7] He was a **member of** the **NAACP** from its founding and edited *The Crisis*.[^s4] The National Park Service counts him a principal architect of the **civil rights movement**.[^s3] He **took part in** the **Pan-African congresses** from 1900 into the 1940s.[^s1] His strategy **contrasts with** that of **Booker T. Washington**.[^s9] His work responded to the **Jim Crow** order and reinterpreted the **Reconstruction era**, and his death fell on the eve of the **March on Washington**.[^s5][^s10][^s1]
