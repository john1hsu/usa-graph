# Frederick Douglass

Frederick Douglass (c. 1818–1895) escaped slavery in Maryland as a young man and became the best-known Black American of the nineteenth century: a lecturer and autobiographer whose own life was evidence against slavery, an editor and political organizer, a Civil War recruiter, and later a federal official.[^s1][^s2] He argued throughout his life that freedom meant full citizenship, including the vote, for Black Americans and for women.[^s2][^s8]

## Life and work

He was born Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey on Maryland's Eastern Shore, in Talbot County, to Harriet Bailey; his enslaver, Aaron Anthony, was rumored to be his father.[^s1] A ledger of Anthony's notes his birth in February 1818, though Douglass believed he was born about 1817, and he later chose February 14 as his birthday, linking it to a memory of his mother.[^s3][^s4] At about eight he was sent to Hugh and Sophia Auld in Baltimore, where Sophia began teaching him to read until her husband stopped the lessons; he kept learning on his own and, at about twelve, bought *The Columbian Orator*, a book of speeches on natural rights.[^s1][^s2] Back on the Eastern Shore, he was hired out in 1834 to Edward Covey, a "slave breaker," and fought back against his beatings; a planned escape in 1836 was discovered.[^s1]

On September 3, 1838, dressed as a sailor and carrying a free Black sailor's papers, he boarded a northbound train in Baltimore with a ticket bought by Anna Murray, a free Black woman, and reached New York by train and boat through Philadelphia in under a day.[^s2][^s3] They married there on September 15 and settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts, taking the name Douglass.[^s1] In 1841 he spoke at a Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society convention and was hired as a lecturer, becoming an ally of William Lloyd Garrison; in 1843 a mob in Indiana broke his right hand.[^s1]

His *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* (1845) became a bestseller and answered doubters who questioned whether he had been enslaved, but exposed him to recapture.[^s2] He spent almost two years lecturing in Britain and Ireland, where supporters paid Hugh Auld $711.66 for his freedom.[^s1][^s2] Back home in 1847, he moved to Rochester, New York, founded the weekly *North Star* with a press bought with British and Irish donations, and sheltered people escaping on the Underground Railroad.[^s1][^s2] In July 1848 he attended the Seneca Falls women's rights convention and seconded Elizabeth Cady Stanton's resolution for women's suffrage, which passed by a slim margin.[^s8]

On July 5, 1852, at Corinthian Hall in Rochester, he gave the oration later titled "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?"[^s6] He praised the Declaration of Independence's principles while asking his audience, "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?"[^s6] By then he had broken with Garrison by reading the Constitution as an antislavery document.[^s1][^s5] After John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry in 1859, Douglass fled to Canada and England to avoid arrest as an accomplice.[^s1]

During the Civil War he recruited Black men for the Union army, including two of his sons for the 54th Massachusetts Infantry.[^s1][^s2] He met President Lincoln on August 10, 1863, to protest unequal pay, no promotions and no protection for captured Black soldiers, and again on August 19, 1864, when Lincoln, fearing defeat, asked him to plan a way to help enslaved people escape the Confederacy.[^s7] In 1869 he supported the Fifteenth Amendment, arguing at the American Equal Rights Association that for Black Americans in the former slave states the vote was a matter of life and death, a stand that divided him from Stanton and Susan B. Anthony.[^s9]

After moving to Washington, D.C., in 1872, he served as president of the Freedman's Savings Bank in 1874, shortly before it failed, as U.S. marshal for the District (1877–1881), as its recorder of deeds (1881–1886) and as minister resident and consul general to Haiti (1889–1891).[^s1][^s2] In 1872 the Equal Rights Party named him as Victoria Woodhull's running mate without his consent, making him the first African American nominated for vice president.[^s2] After Anna's death in 1882 he married Helen Pitts, a white woman, in 1884, a marriage that drew controversy.[^s1][^s2] He died at Cedar Hill, his Anacostia home, on February 20, 1895, after attending a meeting of the National Council of Women, and is buried in Rochester.[^s1][^s2]

## Legacy

Helen Pitts Douglass preserved Cedar Hill, which is now a National Historic Site.[^s2] Black communities soon marked his birthday: schools held a "Douglass Day" in 1897, and by 1901 Chicago and other cities observed it.[^s4] His three autobiographies, the last being *Life and Times of Frederick Douglass* (1881), and his speeches remain central texts of American history.[^s1] His judgment of Lincoln was mixed: at the 1876 dedication of the Emancipation Memorial in Washington he said Lincoln had shared white Americans' prejudices and that Black Americans were at best his stepchildren, yet he kept Lincoln's portrait in his study.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on the facts of his life but read his politics differently. David Blight, who once called Douglass's antislavery reading of the Constitution "dubious" and mainly strategic, now sees it as rooted in natural-rights convictions formed in slavery.[^s5] James Oakes argues that proslavery and antislavery Americans fought over two rival readings of one Constitution, while William Wiecek's older work traced the sources of antislavery constitutionalism; Blight notes that writers across the political spectrum now claim Douglass for their own causes.[^s5]

## Connections

Douglass **took part in** the abolition movement as lecturer, author and editor, and in the **women's suffrage movement** from Seneca Falls on.[^s2][^s8] He **took part in** the **American Civil War** as a recruiter and adviser who pressed Lincoln on Black soldiers' treatment.[^s7] He **held the office** of U.S. minister to Haiti.[^s2] His 1852 oration shows how he was **influenced by** the Declaration of Independence, whose principles he called saving ones.[^s6] He is also linked to the Fifteenth Amendment, the Underground Railroad and the Constitution he reinterpreted.[^s9][^s1][^s5]
