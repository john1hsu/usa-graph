# Great Seal of the United States

The Great Seal is the emblem the federal government uses to certify that a document carries the authority of the United States. Adopted by Congress on June 20, 1782, it has two sides: an obverse with a bald eagle bearing a striped shield, and a reverse with an unfinished pyramid beneath an eye in a triangle.[^s1][^s2] Only the obverse has ever been cut as a die and pressed onto paper; the reverse is familiar mainly because both sides have been printed on the back of the one-dollar bill since 1935.[^s1]

## Origins

On the same day it approved the Declaration of Independence, Congress asked Benjamin Franklin, John Adams and Thomas Jefferson to bring in "a device for a seal of the United States of America."[^s3] Each proposed a scene drawn from scripture or legend — Franklin, Moses parting the sea to overwhelm Pharaoh; Jefferson, the Saxon chiefs Hengist and Horsa — but the committee leaned on its artist consultant, Pierre Eugène Du Simitière.[^s5] His shield of six European nations, ringed by thirteen state shields, was crested by the Eye of Providence. When the report came in on August 20, 1776, Congress tabled it.[^s3][^s5]

A second committee, formed on March 25, 1780, with Francis Hopkinson as consultant, contributed the red-white-and-blue colors, the olive branch and a constellation of thirteen stars, but its report was also sent back.[^s3][^s1] A third committee in May 1782 relied on William Barton, a Philadelphia lawyer, who drew an eagle and designed a reverse showing a thirteen-step pyramid under the first committee's eye.[^s1] On June 13, 1782, Congress gave everything to its secretary, Charles Thomson. Thomson replaced Barton's crested imperial eagle with the native bald eagle, simplified the shield to thirteen vertical stripes under a blue chief, added the Latin mottoes of the reverse, and wrote an explanation of each element. Congress adopted it on June 20.[^s1][^s2]

The first die, of brass, was used on September 16, 1782, on a document authorizing George Washington to negotiate an exchange of prisoners of war.[^s1] When the new federal government organized in 1789, Congress renamed the Department of Foreign Affairs the Department of State, declared the Confederation's seal to be the seal of the United States, and placed it in the Secretary of State's custody. The act forbade affixing it to a commission until the President had signed, or to any other instrument without the President's specific warrant.[^s4]

## Meaning and practice

Thomson's report reads the design as a statement about the union and its government. The thirteen stripes stand for the states, bound together under a blue chief representing Congress; the olive branch and thirteen arrows denote the powers of peace and war, which belonged to Congress alone; the constellation signals a new state taking its place among sovereign powers.[^s1] In Thomson's account the colors carry meanings of their own: white for purity and innocence, red for hardiness and valor, blue for vigilance, perseverance and justice.[^s1]

On the reverse, Thomson wrote that the pyramid signifies "Strength and Duration," while the eye and the motto Annuit Coeptis ("He has favored our undertakings") point to the many interventions of Providence on behalf of the American cause.[^s2][^s1] The date MDCCLXXVI at the base is the year of the Declaration of Independence, and Novus Ordo Seclorum ("a new order of the ages") marks the start of a new American era.[^s1][^s2] The obverse motto, E pluribus unum, survived from the 1776 committee.[^s5]

The physical seal has changed more than its design. A steel die engraved in 1841 showed only six arrows and five-pointed stars, departures from the law that earned it the name "the illegal seal," and an 1877 replacement copied its mistakes until a Tiffany & Co. die of 1885 corrected them.[^s1] A 1904 die served for 26 years, and since 1986 working dies have been made from a master die produced by the Bureau of Engraving and Printing.[^s1] After the President signs and the Secretary of State countersigns, the seal is impressed some 2,000 to 3,000 times a year on treaty ratifications and proclamations, commissions of ambassadors, Cabinet members and other appointees, and envelopes carrying letters that accredit or recall ambassadors. The seal has been on public display in the department's Exhibit Hall since 1961.[^s1]

In 1935 the Bureau of Engraving and Printing sent President Franklin D. Roosevelt a one-dollar design showing both sides; he approved it, switched the order of the two sides and added captions identifying the Great Seal.[^s1] Through the bill the reverse, never used to seal any document, became familiar to the public, and it later became the focus of claims that the eye and pyramid are secret Masonic emblems.[^s1][^s6][^s7] The design record shows the eye entering through Du Simitière and the pyramid through Barton, and the Scottish Rite, a Masonic body, says the combination has no official Masonic meaning and "has never been adopted by any grand lodge as a symbol."[^s1][^s6] Historian Steven Bullock has described the device simply as the Eye of Providence, "the eye of God, in some general way."[^s7]

## Connections

The design was **introduced by** [[Charles Thomson]], who combined three committees' work and introduced the bald eagle and the reverse mottoes, and by William Barton, who drafted the eagle and the pyramid.[^s1] It was **influenced by** Pierre Eugène Du Simitière, whose 1776 design supplied the Eye of Providence and the motto.[^s5] Thomson's report says it **symbolizes** the [[United States]] as a union of states under Congress, and its reverse **commemorates** the [[Declaration of Independence]] through the date at the pyramid's base.[^s2][^s1] Its motto, [[E pluribus unum]], is a separate node, as is the [[bald eagle]] that Thomson placed at its center.[^s1] The 1789 statute that made the [[United States Secretary of State]] its keeper ties it to the federal executive.[^s4] The claim that the reverse is Masonic is treated as its own myth node.[^s6]
