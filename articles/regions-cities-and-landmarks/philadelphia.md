# Philadelphia

Philadelphia is the largest city in Pennsylvania, founded by the Quaker proprietor William Penn on Lenape land in the 1680s. It was where the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution were written, served as the nation's capital in the 1790s, and later became a great manufacturing center; the 2020 census counted 1,603,797 residents.[^s7][^s16]

## Geography

The city sits on the west bank of the Delaware River, where Penn's colonists landed in 1682 and where the city became the region's center of trade by 1701.[^s1] The Lenape name for the land is Coaquannock, glossed as the grove of long pine trees, within the wider homeland of Lënapehòkink, the land of the Lenape.[^s2] Penn borrowed the name Philadelphia, which he likely knew from the Bible, from an ancient city in what is now Turkey; its root means brotherly love, and the nickname City of Brotherly Love was one of Penn's coinages.[^s2][^s3] Since the Consolidation Act of 1854 the city and Philadelphia County have shared the same borders, covering nearly 130 square miles.[^s11]

## History

**Founding.** About 2,500 Lenape lived on both sides of the Delaware when Penn's colonists came, after Dutch traders (1624) and the Swedish and Finnish settlers of New Sweden (1638). Penn bought land on the west bank from the Lenape, whose food and shelter helped the newcomers avoid a starving time. Penn landed on October 28, 1682, and about 4,000 settlers followed within two years.[^s1] In 1683 Penn and surveyor Thomas Holme laid out a grid of wide streets and large lots with five public squares. Remembering London's plague of 1665 and fire of 1666, Penn promised a "greene Country Towne which will never be burnt."[^s3]

**Revolution and capital.** By 1776 Philadelphia's port was second only to London's in the volume and value of the goods passing through it.[^s4] In the Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, the Second Continental Congress approved the Declaration of Independence on July 4, 1776, and the Constitutional Convention finished its work on September 17, 1787.[^s7] Under the Residence Act of 1790, Congress moved from New York to Philadelphia, which was the capital for ten years, with Congress meeting in Congress Hall.[^s5][^s6] Presidents Washington and Adams lived in the President's House at Sixth and Market Streets.[^s5] Nine people enslaved by Washington lived or worked there. Some were sent back to Virginia to keep them from gaining freedom under Pennsylvania's gradual abolition law; Oney Judge escaped from the house in 1796 and the cook Hercules fled in 1797.[^s10]

**Free Black Philadelphia.** Pennsylvania's 1780 law freed no one at once, but by the end of the Revolution the city had more than 1,000 free Black residents. In 1787 Richard Allen, Absalom Jones and others founded the Free African Society, and in 1794 Allen founded Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church ("Mother Bethel"). Black Philadelphians faced mob violence, and the state constitution of 1838 restricted voting to white men; Black men did not regain the vote until 1870.[^s8] When yellow fever killed an estimated 5,000 people in 1793 and government officials fled, Black volunteers cared for the sick and the dead; publisher Mathew Carey then criticized them, and Allen and Jones published a rebuttal.[^s9]

**Workshop of the World.** As New York captured its trade in the early 1800s, Philadelphia turned to manufacturing, specializing in high-quality goods made by many small and mid-sized family firms; in 1906 Baldwin Locomotive Works built 2,666 locomotives and employed more than 18,000 people.[^s4] The 1854 consolidation gave the city one mayor and a uniformed police force after the riots of 1844 and 1849, but also fed party patronage machines.[^s11] The 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Fairmount Park drew almost ten million paid admissions; a Women's Pavilion was built and run entirely by women, although women's art was barred from the main art exhibit.[^s12] In the twentieth century, wartime jobs drew Black southerners in the Great Migration, who built churches and institutions in North and West Philadelphia.[^s13]

## Today

Manufacturing employed 359,000 Philadelphians, 45% of the workforce, in 1953; Walter Licht attributes much of its decline to changing consumer tastes rather than offshoring, and factories now employ only a small share of workers.[^s4][^s14] Pew reports that the city has shrunk nearly 30% since 1950; in 2022 census data about 39% of residents were Black, 16% Hispanic and 8% Asian, and the foreign-born share (15% in 2021) was the highest since the 1940s.[^s14] On May 13, 1985, with authorization from Mayor Wilson Goode and the police commissioner, police dropped a bomb on the house of the MOVE organization on Osage Avenue; eleven people, five of them children, died, and more than 60 homes burned. A mayoral commission called the act unconscionable, but no one was prosecuted.[^s15] UNESCO listed Independence Hall as a World Heritage Site in 1979.[^s7]
