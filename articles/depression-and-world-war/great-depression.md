  - question: How did the gold standard help spread and deepen the Depression?
    answer: Because gold linked interest rates and prices across countries, U.S. tightening forced other gold-standard countries to deflate too; countries that left gold early, like Britain, began recovering sooner, while those that stayed on longest, like France, suffered longer.

# Great Depression

The Great Depression was the worst economic collapse in American history. A Federal Reserve history calls it the "longest and deepest downturn in the history of the United States." It began in August 1929, reached bottom in March 1933, dipped again in 1937–38, and ended only when World War II brought full employment in 1941.[^s1][^s6] At its worst, in 1933, about 12.8 million people, 24.9 percent of the labor force, were out of work, and wages for those who kept their jobs had fallen 42.5 percent since 1929.[^s6]

## Background

The prosperity of the 1920s rested on weak foundations. Speculation and buying stocks on borrowed money pushed the Dow Jones average to a peak of 381 in September 1929, while about 80 percent of families had almost no savings, more than 90 percent of banks had money in the stock market, and Europe struggled with unpaid war debts and reparations.[^s2] In 1928 and 1929 the Federal Reserve raised interest rates to cool stock speculation even though there was no inflation, and production, wholesale prices and personal income were already falling in the two months before the crash.[^s5][^s1]

## Key developments

**The crash and the bank panics.** On Black Thursday, October 24, 1929, the New York Stock Exchange lost 11 percent of its value; on Black Tuesday, October 29, more than 16 million shares were traded and over $14 billion in wealth vanished. Between September and late November the market lost more than half its value.[^s2] The crash was one of a series of financial crises. Regional bank panics struck in 1930–31 and national and international ones in 1931–33. When Britain left the gold standard in 1931, the New York Fed raised its discount rate to protect the dollar, and 522 banks closed that October alone.[^s1][^s5] Between 1929 and 1933 about 5,000 banks failed, and the money supply and average prices each fell by nearly 30 percent.[^s4][^s1]

**Hardship.** Unemployment tripled during 1930, from about 1.5 million to 4.5 million, and passed 12 million, nearly a quarter of the workforce, in 1932.[^s2][^s4] From 1929 to 1933 an average of 100,000 jobs disappeared each week and one farm in four went into foreclosure.[^s4] Wheat fell from about $2 to 40 cents a bushel; more than three-quarters of the banks that closed in 1930–32 were small rural banks; and New York City alone had more than 50,000 homeless residents by late 1932.[^s3] Black Americans faced unemployment of about 50 percent by 1932, and lynchings in the South rose from 8 in 1932 to 28 in 1933.[^s3] Beginning in 1929, government authorities and private companies pressed people of Mexican ancestry to leave for Mexico through raids and threats. In a 2005 apology law, California's legislature found that about 2 million people of Mexican ancestry were relocated to Mexico, about 1.2 million of them born in the United States, and that many citizens were wrongly treated as illegal aliens.[^s9] Drought and dust storms on the Southern Plains made rural suffering worse.[^s3]

**Hoover's response.** Herbert Hoover asked business leaders not to cut wages and relied mainly on voluntary cooperation and private relief. He signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff in June 1930, which did little to help, and in 1932 backed the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, which lent to banks, railroads and farm groups.[^s4] In July 1932 troops under Douglas MacArthur, going beyond Hoover's orders, burned the camps of the Bonus Army, unemployed World War I veterans who had come to Washington seeking early payment of a promised bonus; one marcher was killed.[^s4]

**The New Deal.** Franklin D. Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, closed the banks in a national bank holiday, and launched the New Deal.[^s6][^s1] Banking laws reorganized finance, and in 1935 Congress created Social Security, protected union bargaining through the Wagner Act, and funded the Works Progress Administration, which employed more than 8 million people between 1935 and 1943.[^s1][^s7] The New Deal was uneven. Social Security left out farm and domestic workers, many of them Black, though Black workers made up nearly 15 percent of the WPA's workforce. The 1934 Indian Reorganization Act ended the allotment policy of the Dawes Act and encouraged tribal self-government.[^s7] Unemployment fell to about 14 percent by early 1937, but spending cuts brought a sharp recession that year, and unemployment was still near 15 percent in 1940.[^s7] Wartime demand finally restored full employment in 1941.[^s6][^s1]

## Legacy

The Depression permanently enlarged the federal government's role in the economy, through federal relief, Social Security, a minimum wage set in 1938, and new banking laws.[^s7][^s1] It also changed how economists think: in 2002 Federal Reserve Governor Ben Bernanke told Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, "You're right, we did it. We're very sorry."[^s5] He added that, thanks to their work, the Fed would not repeat the mistake.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what happened but disagree about why. Friedman and Schwartz's monetary view, which Bernanke called the most persuasive explanation, blames the Federal Reserve for letting banks fail and the money supply collapse.[^s5][^s8] A related view stresses the gold standard: countries that left gold early, such as Britain, recovered sooner, while those that held on, such as France, sank longer.[^s5] Keynesian economists see a collapse in total spending that called for government spending to fill the gap.[^s8] Others point to deeper weaknesses of the 1920s: unequal incomes, speculation and fragile international finance.[^s2] At the time, some officials, including Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon, held a liquidationist view that weak banks and firms should simply be allowed to fail.[^s1] Historians also still debate the causes of the 1937 recession.[^s7]

## Connections

- The Depression took place across the **United States**, and is part of the country's **economic history**.[^s1]
- It led to Hoover's **Reconstruction Finance Corporation** and to the **Bonus Army** march of 1932.[^s4]
- It led to Roosevelt's **New Deal**, including the **Social Security Act** and the **Indian Reorganization Act**.[^s6][^s7]
- It hit **agriculture** and **African Americans** especially hard; the **Dust Bowl** and the **Okies** migration happened during it.[^s3]
- It ended when **World War II** demand restored full employment in 1941.[^s6]
