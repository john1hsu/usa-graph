# Detroit

Detroit is a Michigan city on the Detroit River and was the state's first capital. Founded by the French in 1701, it became the center of the American automobile industry, the "arsenal of democracy" of World War II and a center of Black migration, music and labor, before decades of decline ended in the largest municipal bankruptcy in U.S. history in 2013.[^s1][^s6][^s8][^s9][^s14] The 2020 census counted 639,111 residents.[^s17]

## Geography

The city lies on the Detroit River, with Canada across the water; the French fort's name, Fort Pontchartrain du détroit, gave the city its own.[^s1][^s3] For freedom seekers the river crossing was treacherous, and which way it led to freedom changed over time.[^s3] Its Indigenous names include Waawiiyaataanong, "where the river bends."[^s1] After a fire in 1805 Territorial Judge Augustus Woodward laid out a new plan modeled on Washington, D.C., with diagonal avenues radiating like the spokes of a wheel.[^s5]

## History

Anishinaabe peoples had lived along the river for thousands of years before Europeans arrived.[^s1] Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac, a French officer and trader, won royal backing for a settlement to forestall British expansion and support the fur trade; on July 24, 1701, his party chose the site of Fort Pontchartrain du détroit.[^s1] Odawa, Bodewadmi (Potawatomi) and Ojibwe families were encouraged to settle around the fort, Native and French families intermarried, and Native people far outnumbered Europeans in the region.[^s1] Britain took over in 1760 during the French and Indian War.[^s1] In 1763 the Odawa leader Pontiac, known in his village as Obwandiyag, attacked and then besieged the British fort as part of a wider Native war against British rule; the fort was never taken.[^s2]

British garrisons stayed until July 11, 1796, when Britain handed the territory to the United States.[^s3] After a fire on June 11, 1805, destroyed nearly the whole town without loss of life, words attributed to the priest Gabriel Richard became the city motto: *Speramus meliora; resurget cineribus*, "We hope for better things; it will arise from the ashes."[^s5] Although the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 banned slavery, slavery persisted in Detroit: in 1807 Judge Woodward ruled that Peter Denison, an enslaved man, remained his owner's property. While Canada was still phasing out slavery, enslaved people crossed the river into Michigan Territory seeking freedom; after the War of 1812 the route to freedom ran north into Canada.[^s3] On August 16, 1812, General William Hull, also Michigan's territorial governor, surrendered Fort Detroit and about 2,500 men to British Major General Isaac Brock.[^s4] When Michigan entered the Union on January 26, 1837, Detroit became its first state capital; in 1847 the legislature moved the capital to Lansing.[^s6]

Nearby coal, iron and copper and good water and rail links helped Detroit win the early auto industry.[^s8] Ford introduced the Model T in 1908; its Highland Park plant, opened in 1910, used a moving assembly line from 1913, and in 1914 Ford began paying five dollars for an eight-hour day.[^s7][^s8] A city that ranked 13th in population in 1903 was the nation's fifth-largest by 1950, with nearly two million people.[^s8] In the 1930s the United Auto Workers used sit-down strikes, beginning in Flint, to win recognition, and by 1941 it had contracts with every major automaker.[^s8]

Franklin D. Roosevelt coined the phrase "Arsenal of Democracy" in a fireside chat on December 29, 1940, and Detroit embodied it: its plants turned from civilian cars to tanks, jeeps, helmets and bombers, and by summer 1944 Ford's Willow Run plant finished a B-24 bomber every hour.[^s9] About 350,000 workers, many from the South, arrived in the 18 months after Pearl Harbor; Black workers, once largely limited to the most dangerous factory jobs, were hired in far greater numbers, but restrictive covenants confined Black families to the city's poorest housing.[^s9] In 1942 a mob of more than 1,000 white people, some armed, picketed a Black housing project, and on June 20, 1943, fighting on Belle Isle set off a riot that killed 34 people, 25 of them Black; police killed 17 Black people and no white people.[^s10] In 1959 Berry Gordy Jr. used an $800 family loan to found Tamla Records and then Motown, home to the Supremes, Marvin Gaye and Stevie Wonder, before the company moved to Los Angeles in 1972.[^s11]

Behind the boom, the city was losing ground. Automakers moved production to suburbs, small Midwestern towns and the Sunbelt and automated their plants, and Detroit lost more than 130,000 manufacturing jobs between 1948 and 1967 while white residents left for the suburbs.[^s8] Early on July 23, 1967, a police raid on an unlicensed bar at 12th Street and Clairmount set off five days of unrest, variously called the Uprising of 1967, the Detroit Rebellion or the 12th Street Riot; 43 people died, about 1,700 fires were set and more than 7,000 people were arrested before federal troops restored order.[^s12] Its roots lay in housing and school segregation, deindustrialization, the demolition of the Black neighborhoods of Black Bottom and Paradise Valley, and tension with the police.[^s12] In 1973 Detroit elected Coleman A. Young, a former Tuskegee Airman, its first Black mayor; he served five terms.[^s13]

## Today

The 1970s oil crisis, foreign competition and continued decentralization weakened the Big Three automakers; historian Thomas Sugrue describes a city worn down by decades of disinvestment and depopulation.[^s8] On July 18, 2013, under state-appointed emergency manager Kevyn Orr, Detroit filed the largest Chapter 9 municipal bankruptcy in U.S. history, with liabilities that included about $3.5 billion in pensions and $5.7 billion in other retiree benefits.[^s14][^s15] Judge Steven Rhodes confirmed the city's exit plan on November 7, 2014.[^s16] The Census Bureau estimated the population at 649,095 on July 1, 2025, up from the 2020 count, and about three-quarters of residents are Black (QuickFacts, accessed 2026).[^s17]
