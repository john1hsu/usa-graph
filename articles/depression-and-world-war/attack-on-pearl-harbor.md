# Attack on Pearl Harbor

On the morning of Sunday, December 7, 1941, aircraft from six Japanese carriers struck the U.S. Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor and the military airfields of Oʻahu, then a U.S. territory. In about 75 minutes the attack killed 2,403 Americans and wounded 1,178.[^s3] The next day the United States declared war on Japan, and within days it was at war with Germany and Italy as well.[^s1]

## Background

Through the 1930s U.S. officials opposed Japan's expansion in China but saw no vital interest worth a war. After full-scale fighting broke out at the Marco Polo Bridge in July 1937, American opinion swung toward China, and Washington expanded credits and aid to the Chinese government.[^s1] Japan announced a "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere" and signed the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy in September 1940.[^s1] When Japanese troops moved into French Indochina in July 1941, the United States and Britain imposed a full oil embargo and Washington froze Japanese assets; Japan, which faced running out of fuel, finalized its decision for war that September.[^s1][^s6]

Pearl Harbor was a means to an end. Japan's main objective was the oil of the Dutch East Indies, which required seizing Malaya and the Philippines and therefore fighting Britain and the United States. Japanese planners doubted they could win a long war against American industry, so they aimed to disable the fleet and win quickly.[^s6]

## What happened

Japan's First Air Fleet launched its strike from about 230 miles north of Hawaiʻi. The first wave of 183 aircraft arrived about 7:55 a.m., and a second wave of 170 about 8:50 a.m.[^s3] The fleet's three aircraft carriers were at sea, so the bombers concentrated on the battleships at anchor.[^s3][^s6] A bomb that passed through a magazine of USS *Arizona* set off an explosion that killed 1,177 of the 1,512 people aboard.[^s5] *Arizona* and *Utah* were permanently lost; altogether 20 U.S. ships were destroyed or damaged and 347 aircraft destroyed or damaged.[^s3][^s11]

The museum's tally of the dead counts 2,008 Navy, 218 Army and 109 Marine Corps personnel and 68 civilians.[^s3] The National Park Service records 49 civilians killed on Oʻahu, ranging from a three-month-old infant to a 66-year-old; many died when U.S. antiaircraft shells fell on Honolulu and nearby towns, others in strafing and in attacks on sugar mill communities.[^s4] On the island of Niʻihau, home to about 130 Native Hawaiians, a downed Japanese pilot and a local resident who aided him detained residents before islanders overcame them.[^s4] Japan lost 29 aircraft, five midget submarines and 129 men.[^s3] Aboard USS *West Virginia*, Mess Attendant Doris Miller, a Black sailor from Waco, Texas, acted with such courage that he received the Navy Cross in May 1942.[^s10]

The strike on Hawaiʻi was one of several that day: Roosevelt's war message also named attacks on Malaya, Hong Kong, Guam, the Philippines, Wake Island and Midway.[^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

At 12:30 p.m. on December 8, Franklin D. Roosevelt told a joint session of Congress that December 7 was "a date which will live in infamy." Congress declared war with a single dissenting vote, by Representative Jeannette Rankin of Montana—82–0 in the Senate and 388–1 in the House—and Roosevelt signed the declaration at 4 p.m.[^s2][^s6] Germany declared war on the United States on December 11.[^s6] The attack fell short of its larger aims: the carriers, fuel stores and repair yards survived, many ships were raised and returned to service, and in June 1942 U.S. carriers sank four Japanese carriers at Midway.[^s3]

The attack also turned suspicion on Japanese Americans. The FBI immediately arrested people on prewar watch lists, and Executive Order 9066, signed February 19, 1942, led to the mass incarceration of Japanese Americans on the West Coast, about two-thirds of whom were U.S. citizens by birth.[^s9]

Pearl Harbor is remembered at the USS Arizona Memorial, dedicated in 1962 above the sunken ship, whose marble wall lists the names of those who died there.[^s8] In 1994 Congress designated December 7 as National Pearl Harbor Remembrance Day.[^s11]

## Debates and interpretations

**Who was responsible.** Eight investigations ran from 1941 to 1946. The Roberts Commission blamed the Hawaiʻi commanders, Admiral Husband Kimmel and General Walter Short, while a Naval Court of Inquiry cleared Kimmel and faulted the Chief of Naval Operations for failing to warn him. The Joint Congressional Committee's 1946 majority report spread blame among the local commanders and the War and Navy Departments, and a minority report also censured Roosevelt; responsibility was never conclusively fixed.[^s7]

**Foreknowledge.** Codebreakers were reading Japan's diplomatic messages, which kept Roosevelt informed of Tokyo's policy but did not reveal military plans; Japan's naval codes largely resisted American cryptologists. Claims that Roosevelt, Churchill or others suppressed warnings appeared mainly in later revisionist accounts defending the local commanders, which the NSA's own history of the investigations treats with open skepticism.[^s7]

**Surprise or culmination.** Popular memory has long framed the day as a "sneak" attack, stressing Japanese duplicity and casting the United States purely as a victim. Historians writing for Ohio State's *Origins* instead present it as the product of a decade of escalating conflict, arguing that Washington overestimated what embargoes could achieve while Tokyo underestimated American resolve.[^s6] The State Department's historians likewise trace it to Japan's shortages under the embargo and its belief that talks had reached a dead end.[^s1]
