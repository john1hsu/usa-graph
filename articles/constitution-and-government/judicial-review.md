# Judicial review

Judicial review is the power of courts in the United States to decide whether a statute or other act of government conflicts with the Constitution and, if it does, to treat it as void in the case before them. The Constitution never states this power in so many words.[^s1] The Supreme Court first used it against an act of Congress in Marbury v. Madison (1803), and the National Archives describes the decision as completing the system of checks and balances by defining a role for the courts that the Constitution had left open.[^s3] Americans have argued ever since about how far it reaches and who has the last word on the Constitution's meaning.[^s1]

## Origins

Britain's Privy Council had examined colonial laws against colonial charters and struck some down, to many colonists' resentment.[^s2] An English case of 1610 hinted that judges might void acts of Parliament, but by 1776 English courts generally could not do so.[^s2] After independence, several state courts refused to enforce laws that conflicted with their new state constitutions, cases the framers knew.[^s1]

At the Constitutional Convention of 1787, nearly every delegate who spoke to the question assumed or welcomed judicial review of legislation; the only recorded opposition came from Mercer, weakly seconded by Dickinson.[^s1] Those debates were secret, so the state ratifying conventions could not consult them; there, supporters ranged from Hamilton and James Madison to Patrick Henry and George Mason, and John Marshall told Virginia's delegates that judges would have to void laws beyond Congress's enumerated powers.[^s1]

The best-known defense came in Federalist No. 78, where Alexander Hamilton called the judiciary the least dangerous branch because it has "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment."[^s4] When a statute conflicts with the Constitution, he wrote, judges must prefer the Constitution, the people's intention, over that of their agents; this implied not that judges outrank legislators but that the people's power is superior to both.[^s4]

Federal judges acted on the principle before Marbury. In Hayburn's Case (1792), justices riding circuit refused to carry out a pension act they considered unconstitutional, and in Hylton v. United States (1796) the Supreme Court heard argument on whether a federal law was constitutional and upheld it.[^s1]

## How it works

Judicial review happens inside real lawsuits. William Marbury, named a District of Columbia justice of the peace by President John Adams in March 1801, sued Secretary of State James Madison after the new Jefferson administration withheld his commission.[^s2] On February 24, 1803, Chief Justice Marshall, writing for a unanimous Court, held that Marbury had a right to the commission but that the provision of the Judiciary Act of 1789 letting the Court order its delivery in an original case was unconstitutional.[^s2] He reasoned that a written constitution limits the legislature and that it is "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."[^s3] When a statute and the Constitution conflict, the courts must apply the higher law.[^s3]

The power reaches beyond Congress. The National Archives describes Marbury as establishing the courts' right to judge the constitutionality of the other two branches' actions, and it says the Court's power to invalidate federal and state laws has never been seriously challenged.[^s3] Under Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, the Supreme Court could also review state-court decisions on federal questions.[^s1]

The Court did not void another federal statute until Scott v. Sandford (1857), when Chief Justice Roger Taney's opinion struck down the Missouri Compromise of 1820.[^s2] In Cooper v. Aaron (1958), after Arkansas's governor and legislature resisted desegregation and federal troops had to restore order when nine Black students entered Little Rock's Central High School, the unanimous Court refused to delay integration and declared that "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution."[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

**Did the founders intend it?** The Constitution Annotated concludes that most framers who expressed a view expected judicial review, and that the absence of opposition suggests the rest acquiesced, though supporters were not a recorded majority.[^s1] William Crosskey attacked the power's historical basis in 1953; Henry Hart answered him in the *Harvard Law Review* in 1954.[^s1]

**Was Marbury principle or politics?** Marbury arose from the transfer of power after the election of 1800, when the defeated Federalists rushed to fill new judgeships. The Federal Judicial Center notes that the critic Edward Corwin saw in the opinion the marks of a deliberate partisan coup, even as it ranks Marbury among the most important opinions the Court has issued.[^s2]

**Who has the final word?** Thomas Jefferson, in a letter of June 12, 1823, called the parts of Marshall's opinion beyond what the case required very irregular and censurable and wrote that "the ultimate arbiter is the people of the Union."[^s2] After the Dred Scott decision, Abraham Lincoln argued in his first inaugural address that Supreme Court decisions bind the parties to a suit, but that if national policy on vital questions were "irrevocably fixed by decisions of the Supreme Court," the people would cease to be their own rulers.[^s5] At the other end stands Cooper v. Aaron, which treated the Court's reading in Brown as the supreme law of the land under Article VI, binding every state legislator, executive and judicial officer sworn to support the Constitution.[^s6] Scholars including Learned Hand, Herbert Wechsler and Alexander Bickel have continued to debate the power's legitimacy and proper scope.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Marbury v. Madison:** the 1803 case in which the Supreme Court first held an act of Congress void, establishing the principle in practice.[^s3]
- **Checks and balances and separation of powers:** the National Archives calls judicial review an important addition to the checks the three branches hold on one another.[^s3]
- **Federalist Papers and the Constitutional Convention:** Hamilton (Nos. 78 and 81) and Madison (No. 39) defended the power, as did most delegates who addressed it.[^s1]
- **Dred Scott v. Sandford:** the Court's second voiding of a federal statute, which prompted Lincoln's warning.[^s2][^s5]
- **Cooper v. Aaron and Brown v. Board of Education:** the Court invoked interpretive supremacy to enforce desegregation against state resistance.[^s6]
