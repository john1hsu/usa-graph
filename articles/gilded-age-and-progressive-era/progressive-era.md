  myth: Theodore Roosevelt was the Progressive Era's leading "trust-buster."

review:
  - question: Why did Progressive reformers push for the direct election of U.S. senators?
    answer: Legislatures often deadlocked over Senate seats (after an 1895 deadlock Delaware had no Senate representation for two years), and exposés portrayed senators as tools of industrialists; reformers wanted voters, not legislators, to choose, and the Seventeenth Amendment was ratified on April 8, 1913.
  - question: How did the Progressive Era fall short for Black Americans even as it expanded democracy for others?
    answer: Southern states used poll taxes and literacy tests to disfranchise Black men, many white reformers treated segregation as a "Progressive solution," the Wilson administration segregated federal offices, and many Black women still could not vote after 1920; in response Black activists built the Niagara Movement and the NAACP.

# Progressive Era

The Progressive Era is the name historians give to the decades around the turn of the twentieth century, roughly the 1890s to about 1920, when a new generation of mostly middle-class reformers set out to fix the problems of an industrial, urban nation. Reform, as *The American Yawp* puts it, became the word of the age: activists campaigned against monopolies, corrupt city governments, unsafe factories, slum housing, child labor and alcohol.[^s1] They believed in democracy but also in efficiency, science and professional expertise, and while they distrusted party politics they increasingly looked to government to regulate the market economy.[^s2] The Library of Congress groups the years 1900–1929 as "Progressive Era to New Era," with reform giving way in the 1920s.[^s5]

## Background

The era grew out of the [[us:gilded-age]]. Between 1897 and 1904 more than 4,000 companies were consolidated into 257 firms, while the Sherman Anti-Trust Act of 1890 reached only "unreasonable" restraints of trade and was often turned against labor unions.[^s1] Cities swelled with immigrants from southern and eastern Europe living in crowded tenements.[^s5] Journalists whom Theodore Roosevelt dubbed [[us:muckrakers]] made these problems vivid: Jacob Riis photographed New York's tenements in *How the Other Half Lives* (1890), Ida Tarbell exposed Standard Oil, and Lincoln Steffens documented municipal corruption.[^s2] Reform began locally and in the states before it reached Washington.[^s2]

## Key developments

**Grassroots reform.** Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull House in Chicago in 1889, and settlement workers such as Florence Kelley pressed for an eight-hour day for women and children.[^s1] The Woman's Christian Temperance Union, women's clubs and, from 1896, the National Association of Colored Women organized nationwide.[^s1] The 1911 [[us:triangle-shirtwaist-factory-fire]] in Manhattan killed 146 garment workers, and the owners were acquitted of manslaughter.[^s1]

**Remaking democracy.** Reformers adopted the initiative, referendum and recall and pressed for the popular election of senators.[^s5] State legislatures that chose senators often deadlocked (Delaware's 1895 deadlock lasted 217 ballots), and by 1912 as many as 29 states let voters effectively choose senators through primaries or general elections; the Seventeenth Amendment was ratified on April 8, 1913.[^s6]

**Progressivism in the White House.** Roosevelt promised a "Square Deal," won the breakup of the Northern Securities railroad trust (upheld 5–4 in 1904), signed the Hepburn, Meat Inspection and Pure Food and Drug Acts, and set aside about 230 million acres of public land.[^s4] His successor, William Howard Taft, initiated twice as many antitrust suits.[^s4] In 1912 Roosevelt's Progressive Party split the Republican vote, and Woodrow Wilson won with 42 percent of the popular vote.[^s4] Wilson's "New Freedom" lowered tariffs, levied an income tax under the new Sixteenth Amendment, and created twelve regional reserve banks under the Federal Reserve Act of 1913.[^s4] The Miller Center calls the Federal Reserve "probably the most important domestic achievement" of his presidency; the Clayton Act and the Federal Trade Commission followed in 1914.[^s8] In 1916 the Keating-Owen Act restricted child labor (later struck down by the Supreme Court) and the Adamson Act gave railroad workers an eight-hour day.[^s4]

**Women's suffrage.** By 1911 six western states had enfranchised women.[^s1] Alice Paul's National Woman's Party picketed the White House from January 1917, and more than 150 women were arrested.[^s1] Congress approved the Nineteenth Amendment on June 4, 1919, and Tennessee's ratification on August 18, 1920, made it law.[^s7] Many women, especially women of color, still faced discriminatory state voting laws.[^s7]

**Race and the limits of reform.** Progressive Era democracy was, for many, whites-only. Mississippi's 1890 constitution pioneered poll taxes and literacy tests; in Alabama in 1900 only 3,742 of 121,159 literate Black men of voting age were registered.[^s1] Many Southern reformers saw segregation as a "Progressive solution" to racial violence, and eugenicists were influential among middle-class reformers.[^s3] After 1913 Wilson's cabinet segregated departments such as the Treasury and Post Office, and Black federal employees were downgraded or dismissed.[^s8] Black activists answered with the Niagara Movement (1905), which demanded immediate equality, and the NAACP (1909), founded by W. E. B. Du Bois, Ida B. Wells-Barnett, Mary Church Terrell and others.[^s3] Native professionals, sometimes called "Red Progressives," founded the Society of American Indians in 1911, the first Native rights organization conceived and run by Native people; it debated U.S. citizenship, which Native people as a group did not receive until 1924.[^s11]

## Legacy

The era's federal reforms (a national income tax, the Federal Reserve, food and drug regulation, antitrust agencies and four constitutional amendments) greatly expanded the federal government's role in regulating the economy.[^s2][^s4] The United States entered [[us:world-war-i]] in 1917, and after the war most Americans turned away from foreign affairs; in the prosperous 1920s many reform activities declined.[^s5] Some historians, such as John Buenker, trace twentieth-century American liberalism partly back to Progressive Era reform coalitions, including urban political machines.[^s9]

## Debates and interpretations

**Who were the progressives?** In the 1950s George Mowry and Richard Hofstadter portrayed reformers as an older, privileged middle class; Hofstadter's *The Age of Reform* (1955) attributed their discontent to "status anxiety."[^s9] Gabriel Kolko's *The Triumph of Conservatism* (1963) argued instead that business leaders sought regulation to shield themselves from competition, while Robert Wiebe's *The Search for Order* (1967) saw a new middle class of professionals building national institutions to match a national economy.[^s9]

**A people's movement?** J. Joseph Huthmacher, John Buenker and David Thelen found reform energy among immigrant workers, urban political machines and broad consumer coalitions, and women's historians showed how women used reform to defend families and expand their public roles.[^s9]

**Was there a movement at all?** Peter Filene argued in 1970 that "progressivism" had outlived its usefulness as a concept; Daniel Rodgers replied in "In Search of Progressivism" (1982) that the movement's diversity was the source of its impact.[^s9] Rodgers later noted that the field has grown far beyond what was visible when he wrote that essay.[^s10]

## Connections

- **Occurred in** the [[us:united-states]], at home and, under the banner of making the world safe for democracy, abroad.[^s5]
- **Requires** the [[us:gilded-age]] as background: corporate consolidation and urban poverty created the problems reformers attacked.[^s1]
- **Led to** the [[us:seventeenth-amendment]], after years of legislative deadlocks and state experiments with popular choice.[^s6]
- **Led to** the [[us:nineteenth-amendment]], as Progressive ideals strengthened the [[us:womens-suffrage-movement]].[^s3]
- **Led to** the [[us:federal-reserve-system]], created by Wilson's 1913 banking reform.[^s4]
- **Contrasts with** the [[us:roaring-twenties]], the "new era" in which many reform activities declined.[^s5]
