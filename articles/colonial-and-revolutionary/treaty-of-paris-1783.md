# Treaty of Paris (1783)

The Treaty of Paris, formally the "Definitive Treaty of Peace," was signed in Paris on September 3, 1783, by David Hartley, a member of Parliament, for Great Britain and by John Adams, Benjamin Franklin and John Jay for the United States. It ended the American Revolutionary War, by then a wider conflict also involving France, Spain and the Netherlands, and Britain formally recognized the United States as independent.[^s1] The Confederation Congress ratified it on January 14, 1784.[^s4] Its ten articles settled independence and boundaries, but the promises it made about debts, Loyalists and enslaved people, and its silence about Native nations, shaped American politics for years afterward.[^s4][^s5]

## Background

The British defeat at Yorktown in October 1781 brought down Lord North's government, and the new ministry under Rockingham opened talks with the American commissioners in Paris.[^s7] Franklin turned down early British offers that would have left the states some self-rule inside the empire. He insisted on recognition of independence and refused to make a separate peace behind the back of France, America's ally since 1778. Formal negotiations began on September 27, 1782, with the government of Lord Shelburne, which rejected Franklin's demand for Canada.[^s2]

After about two months the two sides signed preliminary articles on November 30, 1782, covering independence, boundaries, fishing rights, prewar debts, Loyalist property and the evacuation of British forces.[^s2] Under the French alliance these terms could not take effect until Britain and France reached their own agreement, and Spain's failed attempt to retake Gibraltar in September 1782 pushed France and Spain toward compromise.[^s7] Britain and France signed preliminaries on January 20, 1783, and an armistice followed.[^s2][^s7]

The final signing took place at about 10:30 a.m. in Hartley's rooms at the Hôtel de York, while British envoys signed separate treaties with France and Spain at Versailles the same day. The definitive treaty repeated the nine provisional articles almost word for word and added a tenth on ratification; Adams grumbled that the Americans had "negotiated here, these Six Months for nothing."[^s3]

## What it says

- **Article 1** recognized the thirteen states, each named, as free, sovereign and independent, and gave up British claims to govern them. Some online transcriptions leave out Delaware, but the original lists it.[^s1]
- **Article 2** drew the boundaries: in the north along the St. Croix River, the highlands and through the Great Lakes to the Lake of the Woods; in the west down the Mississippi; and in the south along the 31st parallel and the Apalachicola, Flint and St. Marys rivers.[^s1]
- **Article 3** kept Americans' right to fish on the Grand Banks and in the Gulf of St. Lawrence and to dry fish on unsettled coasts of Nova Scotia, the Magdalen Islands and Labrador.[^s1]
- **Article 4** let creditors on both sides recover the full sterling value of good-faith prewar debts.[^s1]
- **Articles 5 and 6** had Congress urge the states to restore property confiscated from Loyalists and barred further confiscations or prosecutions for wartime conduct.[^s1]
- **Article 7** ended hostilities, freed prisoners, and required British forces to leave without "carrying away any Negroes or other property" of Americans.[^s1][^s6]
- **Article 8** declared the Mississippi, from its source to the ocean, open to both British subjects and U.S. citizens; **Article 9** returned any territory either side conquered before the provisional articles reached America; and **Article 10** required ratifications within six months.[^s1]

## Legacy

Several promises went unkept. Virginians, who owed nearly half of the roughly £5 million in prewar debts to British merchants, objected to repaying in sterling, and the state closed its courts to British creditors, pointing to Britain's own violations. An estimated 60,000 to 80,000 Loyalists left the United States.[^s4] On Article 7, Sir Guy Carleton refused to treat Black people who had already joined the British as returnable property. About 3,000 people in New York received passes recorded in the "Book of Negroes," and upwards of 9,000 people of African descent left New York and other ports with the British. Many settled in Nova Scotia on poorer land than white Loyalists received, and some later moved on to Sierra Leone.[^s6]

For Native nations the treaty was a peace made without them. It held no negotiated terms for Britain's Native allies, who each had to make a separate peace. At Fort Stanwix in October 1784, U.S. commissioners dictated terms to the Six Nations and treated the Mohawk, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca as defeated enemies, "which they were not."[^s5] North of the Ohio River, a confederacy in which the Mohawk leader Joseph Brant, the Shawnee leader Blue Jacket and the Miami leader Little Turtle played leading roles resisted settlement and defeated U.S. armies in 1790 and 1791.[^s4] Spain, whose conquest of West Florida gave it both banks of the lower river, closed the Mississippi below Natchez to American ships and goods in 1784, despite Article 8.[^s4]

These troubles fed the case for a stronger union. British officials in Canada kept garrisons at Detroit, Niagara and five other posts that Article 2 had placed inside the United States, and Federalists argued that only a Congress able to tax and raise an army could make Britain leave. Virginia's debt cases dragged on until *Ware v. Hylton* in 1794, the first settled in a British creditor's favor, with the defendant's last appeal exhausted two years later.[^s4]

## Connections

The treaty was **signed by** John Adams, Benjamin Franklin and John Jay, the American commissioners, opposite Britain's David Hartley.[^s1] It closed the American Revolutionary War, and its terms **affected** the Mississippi River, whose navigation Article 8 declared open.[^s1] It **affected** the Loyalists, whose property and safety Articles 5 and 6 addressed and tens of thousands of whom left the country.[^s4] It **affected** the Haudenosaunee and other Native nations by leaving them out, and it **led to** the 1784 Treaty of Fort Stanwix, the separate peace the Six Nations were pressed to accept.[^s5] Its unkept terms became arguments for replacing the Articles of Confederation with the Constitution.[^s4]
