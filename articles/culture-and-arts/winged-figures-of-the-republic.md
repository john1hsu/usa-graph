# Winged Figures of the Republic

The Winged Figures of the Republic are a pair of identical bronze winged figures, each about 30 feet tall, that sit on polished black diorite blocks on either side of a 142-foot flagpole at the Nevada end of Hoover Dam.[^s1][^s2] Their arms are raised almost straight up, and they rest on a terrazzo floor inlaid with a star chart.[^s2] Designed by the sculptor Oskar J. W. Hansen (1892–1971) and owned by the Bureau of Reclamation, they are the centerpiece of a monument plaza meant to commemorate the building of the dam.[^s2][^s3]

## Background

When the Bureau of Reclamation finished Hoover Dam in the mid-1930s, its architect Gordon B. Kaufmann gave the structure a spare modern look, keeping exterior decoration to a few places.[^s3] Interior Secretary Harold L. Ickes, after a national competition, appointed Hansen, a Norwegian immigrant, as the project's consulting sculptor.[^s1] Hansen designed the plaza on the Nevada side and also made ten concrete bas-reliefs for the two elevator towers on the crest: the Nevada tower's five panels show the dam's purposes of flood control, navigation, irrigation, water storage and power, while the Arizona tower's honor the Native nations of the surrounding mountains and plains.[^s3][^s1] Hansen also designed the bronze plaque near the figures that memorializes the 96 men officially counted as dying during construction.[^s1]

The dates of the work are easy to confuse. The Smithsonian's sculpture inventory dates it to 1935, the year President Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicated the dam on September 30.[^s2] But Reclamation photographs held by the Boulder City/Hoover Dam Museum show Hansen still checking fabrication of the star map's Circle of Precession in April 1938, and posing at the figures that August with Carl J. Hambro, president of the Norwegian Parliament.[^s4][^s5] The plaza was completed in 1938.[^s7][^s4]

## About the work

Each figure contains more than four tons of statuary bronze, cast from sand molds weighing about 492 tons, with the metal heated to roughly 2,500°F and poured in one continuous stream.[^s6] The Smithsonian records the founder as Roman Bronze Works.[^s2] To keep the polished stone from being scarred, the diorite bases were set on blocks of ice and guided into place as the ice melted.[^s6]

Hansen gave the figures a civic meaning. Reclamation's history of the dam reports that he said they stood for "that eternal vigilance which is the price of liberty."[^s1] He also wrote that they embodied "the immutable calm of intellectual resolution," and the Boulder City Museum and Historical Association describes them as honoring the more than 5,000 men who built the dam.[^s6] The vigilance phrase is often credited to Thomas Jefferson, but Monticello's researchers have found no evidence he said or wrote it; it traces to John Philpot Curran, whose idea circulated in American newspapers and was first pinned on Jefferson in print in 1834.[^s9]

The terrazzo star map records the sky at the moment of the dam's dedication on September 30, 1935.[^s1] Hansen set the celestial positions within a Circle of Precession, which depicts the Platonic Year, the very slow wobble of Earth's axis that shifts the equinoxes against the stars, so that the alignment itself fixes the date.[^s4] A raised compass of rock and brass and several plaques complete the plaza.[^s7]

## Reception and legacy

The plaza has been hard to keep intact. Its terrazzo was first repaired in 1948, and in 1958 Hansen himself supervised a rebuilding of the floor; later cracking pushed the brass inlays of the star chart upward.[^s7] Hansen wanted the bronze to dull with age, but visitors rub the figures' toes for luck and keep them polished.[^s7]

Visitor access closed in March 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic. In early 2022 Reclamation contractors began a full restoration: the figures were sandblasted and given a new artificial patina, and the old star map was demolished that summer so drainage could be built beneath new terrazzo.[^s8] When that work stalled, preservationists launched a Save the Star Map campaign in July 2023, and a Reclamation spokesman said the agency was committed to restoring all elements of the monument.[^s8] The Boulder City Review has called the star map the most-photographed terrazzo work in the world, and the bureau notes that the monument is part of a National Historic Landmark.[^s8]
