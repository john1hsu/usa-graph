## civil-war-and-reconstruction

### [American Civil War](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/american-civil-war.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Civil War was fought over states' rights or tariffs, not slavery. | The seceding states said otherwise at the time. Mississippi declared its position "thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery," South Carolina complained that Northern states would not enforce the Fugitive Slave Act, and Confederate Vice President Alexander Stephens rested the new government on Black inequality. The only state right at stake was the right to protect slavery. |

**Why it matters:** The postwar "Lost Cause" story recast secession as a defense of constitutional principle. Believing it hides what the war was about and why emancipation and the Reconstruction amendments followed from it.

- **Q:** Why did Deep South states secede after the 1860 election, before Lincoln had taken office?
  **A:** They saw the victory of a party opposed to slavery's expansion as a threat to slavery itself. Mississippi's declaration said its position was thoroughly identified with slavery, and South Carolina's centered on Northern refusal to return fugitives from slavery.
- **Q:** How did enslaved people help turn a war for the Union into a war against slavery?
  **A:** By fleeing to Union lines from 1861 on, they forced the army and Congress to decide their status, which led to contraband policy, the Confiscation Acts, abolition in Washington, D.C., the Emancipation Proclamation, and the enlistment of about 179,000 Black soldiers.
- **Q:** Why do historians now cite about 750,000 deaths instead of the long-used 620,000?
  **A:** J. David Hacker compared census survival rates for 1860–1870 with the decades before and after, and the shortfall pointed to about 750,000 deaths (range 650,000–850,000), since earlier counts relied on incomplete military records.

### [Siege of Vicksburg](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/siege-of-vicksburg.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Lincoln's line that the Father of Waters again flowed "unvexed to the sea" was his spontaneous reaction to the news from Vicksburg. | The sentence appears in a public letter he wrote to James C. Conkling on August 26, 1863, nearly two months after the surrender, in a passage that also credits soldiers from many regions and Black troops. |

**Why it matters:** Detaching the line from the letter loses Lincoln's purpose, which was to defend emancipation and Black enlistment to Northern critics by pointing to victories like Vicksburg.

- **Q:** Why did both sides consider Vicksburg so important?
  **A:** Confederate guns on its bluffs blocked Union traffic on the Mississippi, on which Midwestern farmers depended, and the city linked the eastern Confederacy to the supplies and men of Louisiana, Arkansas and Texas; taking it would reopen the river and split the South.
- **Q:** How did Grant finally get his army into position to besiege Vicksburg after months of failure?
  **A:** He crossed the river south of the city on April 30–May 1, 1863, won a string of battles inland through May 17 that drove Pemberton back into the city, and after two failed assaults on May 19 and 22 settled into a siege.
- **Q:** Why did Pemberton surrender on July 4 rather than fight his way out?
  **A:** After about six weeks of siege, hunger and disease had worn down his garrison; his commanders judged a breakout impossible, and Grant's offer to parole the men rather than imprison them was the best term available.
- **Q:** How did the fall of Vicksburg lead to Union control of the entire Mississippi?
  **A:** The garrison at Port Hudson, Louisiana, the last Confederate stronghold on the river, surrendered on July 9, 1863, after learning Vicksburg had fallen, opening the river from its source to New Orleans.

### [Thirteenth Amendment](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/thirteenth-amendment.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Emancipation Proclamation ended slavery throughout the United States. | The 1863 proclamation applied only to areas in rebellion and left slavery legal in loyal border states; slavery ended nationwide only with the Thirteenth Amendment, which freed people still enslaved in Delaware and Kentucky in December 1865. |

**Why it matters:** Confusing a wartime executive order with a constitutional amendment hides why abolitionists, Congress and Lincoln fought for an amendment, and how close slavery came to surviving the war.

- **Q:** Why did Lincoln and Congress believe a constitutional amendment was needed even after the Emancipation Proclamation?
  **A:** The proclamation rested on Lincoln's war powers and applied only to areas in rebellion, not to loyal border states; its legal force after the war was doubtful, so an amendment was the only secure way to end slavery everywhere.
- **Q:** How did the 1864 election and the end of the war help the amendment pass and be ratified?
  **A:** After the House first failed to reach two-thirds, Lincoln's reelection campaign put the amendment on the Republican platform and a lame-duck session passed it in January 1865; later, President Andrew Johnson required Southern states seeking readmission to abolish slavery, and Georgia's ratification in December 1865 completed the three-fourths.
- **Q:** Why is the amendment's exception "as a punishment for crime" significant?
  **A:** Southern states used criminal convictions to lease mostly Black prisoners to private companies, as Georgia did from 1868 to 1908, and scholars still debate whether the framers meant the clause to permit that kind of forced labor.

## cold-war-and-civil-rights

### [Brown v. Board of Education](../articles/cold-war-and-civil-rights/brown-v-board-of-education.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The case began when Oliver Brown alone sued after his daughter Linda was turned away from a nearby white school and forced to walk far to a Black school. | The Topeka NAACP deliberately organized a class action with 13 parents on behalf of 20 children; Oliver Brown was recruited to join and named because he was the only man in the group, and the Supreme Court decided Kansas's case together with cases from South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware and the District of Columbia involving nearly 200 people. |

**Why it matters:** Reducing Brown to one family's story hides the years of organized legal strategy and the many students, parents, teachers and lawyers, in the South and beyond it, who took risks to bring the cases.

- **Q:** Why did the NAACP's lawyers value the Kansas case even though Topeka's Black and white schools were roughly comparable in buildings and resources?
  **A:** Because the facilities were substantially comparable, the Kansas case forced the Court to decide whether segregation itself was unconstitutional rather than merely ordering states to equalize unequal schools, and it showed that school segregation was a national issue and not only a Southern one.
- **Q:** Why was the District of Columbia case, Bolling v. Sharpe, decided separately from the four state cases?
  **A:** The Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause restrains the states, not the federal government, so segregation in the federal capital had to be challenged under the Fifth Amendment instead.
- **Q:** How did the Court's approach to remedies allow segregation to continue for years after 1954?
  **A:** The 1954 ruling did not say how or when schools must desegregate; Brown II in 1955 left it to federal district courts to proceed "with all deliberate speed," and opponents used that vague standard to delay, admit only token numbers of Black students, pass new segregation laws, or, as in Prince Edward County, close public schools from 1959 to 1964.

## colonial-and-revolutionary

### [Articles of Confederation](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/articles-of-confederation.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The government under the Articles of Confederation accomplished nothing and was simply a failure. | Despite having no power to tax, the Confederation Congress saw the Revolutionary War to its end with the 1783 Treaty of Paris, set up departments of Foreign Affairs, War, Marine and Treasury, and passed the Land Ordinances of 1784 and 1785 and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, which created a process for admitting new states on equal terms with the original thirteen. |

**Why it matters:** Because the Constitution replaced the Articles, the Confederation is often remembered only as the failed first draft, a view that overlooks lasting achievements and the real reasons people in the 1770s distrusted a strong central government.

- **Q:** Why did Congress under the Articles struggle to pay its bills, and what did that mean in practice?
  **A:** The Articles gave Congress no power to tax; it could only ask the state legislatures for money, and between 1781 and 1787 it received about $1.5 million of the $10 million it requested, leaving it unable to pay war debts or raise its own army, as Shays' Rebellion showed.
- **Q:** How did the dispute over western lands delay the Articles from taking effect?
  **A:** Ratification had to be unanimous, and states without western land claims, above all Maryland, refused to ratify until states such as Virginia gave up their claims; Maryland ratified only on March 1, 1781, after Virginia agreed to do so.
- **Q:** How did the Articles' clause on Indian affairs lead to conflict at Fort Stanwix in 1784?
  **A:** The Articles gave Congress power over affairs with Native peoples who were not members of any state, but New York claimed the Haudenosaunee as within its jurisdiction and held its own meeting, so Congress and the state competed for land cessions, and New York later sold land it had failed to obtain by treaty.
- **Q:** Why did the framers of the Articles deliberately create a weak central government?
  **A:** Many Americans feared losing power to a central body, as the earlier failure of the Albany Plan showed, so the Articles let each state keep its sovereignty and every power not expressly delegated, gave Congress no taxing power and no independent executive or courts, and required every state to agree to amendments.

### [Declaration of Independence](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/declaration-of-independence.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The delegates signed the Declaration of Independence on July 4, 1776. | Congress approved the text on July 4, but it ordered a parchment copy for signing only on July 19; most delegates signed on August 2, 1776, a few signed later that year, and Thomas McKean signed sometime after January 1777. |

**Why it matters:** Later recollections by Jefferson and Adams placed the signing on July 4, and the copies printed that week carried only the names of John Hancock and Charles Thomson, so the date of approval and the act of signing blurred together in popular memory.

- **Q:** Why did Congress cut Jefferson's passage blaming the king for the slave trade?
  **A:** Jefferson recorded that it was struck to accommodate South Carolina and Georgia, which wanted the slave trade to continue, and that some Northern delegates were uneasy because Northern merchants had carried enslaved people; the deletion kept those delegations behind independence.
- **Q:** How did declaring independence help the American war effort abroad?
  **A:** France would consider a formal alliance only once the colonies declared independence, so the Declaration opened the way to recognition and to the 1778 Treaty of Alliance with France, followed by Dutch recognition in 1782 and British acknowledgement in the 1783 Treaty of Paris.
- **Q:** Why is July 4 not the day the Declaration was signed?
  **A:** July 4 was the day Congress approved the text; the signing copy was only ordered on July 19, and most delegates signed it on August 2, 1776, with others signing later.
- **Q:** How did later reformers use the Declaration's language?
  **A:** The women who met at Seneca Falls in 1848 modelled their Declaration of Sentiments on it, declaring that all men and women are created equal, and Frederick Douglass in 1852 measured the nation's celebration of independence against the slavery it still allowed.

## constitution-and-government

### [Constitutional Convention](../articles/constitution-and-government/constitutional-convention.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The delegates were sent to Philadelphia to write a new constitution. | Congress called the meeting only to revise the Articles of Confederation and report proposed alterations to Congress and the states; the delegates decided on their own, by mid-June 1787, to draft an entirely new frame of government, and their Constitution took effect once nine state conventions had ratified it. |

**Why it matters:** Because the convention's product replaced the Articles completely and is commemorated on September 17, it is easy to assume a new constitution was the assignment from the start, which hides how bold, and how contested, the delegates' decision was.

- **Q:** Why did the convention end up writing a whole new constitution when Congress had asked only for revisions?
  **A:** Many delegates believed the Articles left the central government too weak; Randolph's Virginia Plan on May 29 proposed a new national legislature, executive and judiciary, and by mid-June the delegates had accepted that they were drafting a new frame of government rather than amending the old one.
- **Q:** How did the Great Compromise settle the quarrel between large and small states?
  **A:** It gave the House of Representatives seats in proportion to each state's population and gave every state equal representation in the Senate; it passed on July 16, 1787, by a single state's margin.
- **Q:** How did the convention's decisions strengthen slaveholding states?
  **A:** Counting three-fifths of enslaved people added to slave states' seats in the House and, through the electoral college, their weight in presidential elections; the convention also barred Congress from ending the Atlantic slave trade before 1808 and added a fugitive slave clause.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about whether the Constitution was a proslavery document?
  **A:** Paul Finkelman argues its many protections for slavery made it a proslavery bargain, while Sean Wilentz argues that by refusing to recognise property in people the framers left an opening later antislavery politicians used; critics of Wilentz note that his key Madison quotation may have been added to Madison's notes later.

### [United States Congress](../articles/constitution-and-government/united-states-congress.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Constitution fixes the House of Representatives at 435 members. | The Constitution set only the House's first size (65 members) and a minimum of one representative per state; Congress set the number by law, enlarging the House after most censuses, fixing it at 433 plus two in 1911 and capping it at 435 from 1929, apart from a temporary 437 after Alaska and Hawaii joined. |

**Why it matters:** Because 435 has not changed for about a century, it is easy to mistake an ordinary statute that Congress could amend for a constitutional rule.

- **Q:** Why does Congress have two chambers with different bases of representation?
  **A:** The framers created a bicameral legislature in July 1787 in which House seats are apportioned among the states by population while every state has two senators, balancing the interests of populous and small states.
- **Q:** How did the Seventeenth Amendment change the Senate, and why was it adopted?
  **A:** It replaced election of senators by state legislatures with election by the people; legislative selection had produced long deadlocks (45 in 20 states between 1891 and 1905), vacant seats and bribery cases, and many states had already moved to popular choice through primaries before the amendment was ratified in 1913.
- **Q:** How did the original Constitution's formula for apportioning House seats involve slavery?
  **A:** Article I counted the whole number of free persons, excluded "Indians not taxed," and added three-fifths of "all other Persons," meaning enslaved people, who could not vote, still added to the representation of the states that held them.
- **Q:** Why was the First Congress unable to start work on March 4, 1789?
  **A:** Too few members had arrived in New York; only 8 of 22 senators and 13 of 59 representatives were present, so neither chamber had a quorum until April 1 (House) and April 6 (Senate).

### [United States Constitution](../articles/constitution-and-government/united-states-constitution.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Constitution declares that "all men are created equal." | That phrase comes from the Declaration of Independence. The original Constitution contained no such promise and instead counted three-fifths of enslaved people for apportionment; an equality guarantee entered the text only with the Fourteenth Amendment after the Civil War. |

**Why it matters:** Running the two founding documents together hides how the 1787 text accommodated slavery and how much the Reconstruction amendments changed it.

- **Q:** How does the Constitution try to keep any one part of the government from becoming too powerful?
  **A:** It separates legislative, executive and judicial power among Congress, the president and the courts, gives each checks on the others such as the veto, veto override, Senate confirmation, impeachment and judicial review, and divides power between the national government and the states.
- **Q:** Why did several states ratify only after being promised amendments, and what came of that promise?
  **A:** Anti-Federalists feared a strong central government could threaten individual liberties and demanded a bill of rights; the First Congress proposed twelve amendments in September 1789, and ten were ratified on December 15, 1791, as the Bill of Rights.
- **Q:** How did the original Constitution protect slavery without using the word "slave"?
  **A:** It used euphemisms such as other persons and persons held to service or labor; it counted three-fifths of enslaved people for representation and taxes, barred Congress from banning their importation before 1808, and required the return of people who escaped slavery into another state.
- **Q:** How did Charles Beard's interpretation of the Constitution differ from that of later ideological historians?
  **A:** Beard argued in 1913 that the framers and ratifiers acted mainly to protect their economic interests, while historians such as Bernard Bailyn and Gordon Wood argued that political ideas, especially republicanism, better explain the framing; later scholarship has largely undermined Beard's account.

## contemporary-america

### [September 11 attacks](../articles/contemporary-america/september-11-attacks.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Twin Towers were brought down by a planned controlled demolition using explosives or thermite rather than by the hijacked planes. | The federal engineering investigation by NIST found that the impacts severed columns and stripped fireproofing, and that multi-floor fires weakened floors that pulled the perimeter columns inward until they failed; it found no corroborating evidence for explosives, and seismic signals came from falling debris after each collapse began. |

**Why it matters:** The towers' sudden, total collapse was unprecedented and hard to grasp, and demolition stories replace a documented chain of failures and warnings with a hidden plot that the engineering and investigative record does not support.

- **Q:** Why did the 9/11 Commission call imagination the most important failure before the attacks?
  **A:** Officials had warnings about al Qaeda and earlier attacks such as the 1998 embassy bombings and the 2000 USS Cole attack, but no one treated the use of hijacked airliners as weapons as a real threat; the Commission found that U.S. measures between 1998 and 2001 never disturbed or delayed the plot.
- **Q:** How did the actions of the people aboard United 93 change the outcome of the attacks?
  **A:** After learning by phone of the other hijackings, the passengers and crew voted to fight back and forced the plane down in a Pennsylvania field about 20 minutes' flying time from Washington, so it never reached its probable target, the U.S. Capitol or the White House.
- **Q:** How did the attacks reshape the federal government at home?
  **A:** Within weeks the president created a White House Office of Homeland Security, Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act and created the TSA in 2001, and the Homeland Security Act of 2002 merged 22 agencies into a new Cabinet department; the 9/11 Commission's 2004 report then led to proposals such as a National Counterterrorism Center and a national intelligence director.

## culture-and-arts

### [Jazz](../articles/culture-and-arts/jazz.md) — Tradition

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Jazz was born in the brothels of Storyville, and its musicians scattered north when the district was closed in 1917. | Jazz grew out of New Orleans neighborhood life — brass bands, parades, dance halls, social clubs and street music — and Storyville was only one of many places musicians worked. Research by Tulane's Hogan Jazz Archive curator Bruce Raeburn on musicians who left the city between 1902 and 1922 found that they went for better pay, working conditions, racial equality and safety, not because the district closed; several had left years before 1917. |

**Why it matters:** The red-light story is colorful and easy to tell, but it hides the African American and Creole communities, institutions and traditions that actually produced the music, and the segregation and police harassment that drove many musicians away.

- **Q:** How did the segregation laws of the 1890s help shape early jazz in New Orleans?
  **A:** They stripped Creoles of color of their separate social status and pushed them together with uptown Black musicians, combining the Creoles' formal training and discipline with the improvised, blues-based playing of uptown bands.
- **Q:** Why is the story that jazz musicians left New Orleans because Storyville closed misleading?
  **A:** Many musicians, such as Jelly Roll Morton, the Original Creole Orchestra and Tom Brown's band, had left before the district closed in November 1917, and research on musicians who left between 1902 and 1922 shows they sought better pay, working conditions, racial equality and safety; King Oliver, for example, had been repeatedly arrested while playing.
- **Q:** How did bebop change the role of jazz compared with the swing era?
  **A:** Swing big bands played largely for dancing, while bebop, developed in Harlem after-hours sessions in the early 1940s, used small groups, fast tempos and improvised solos over chord changes, and was meant mainly for listening.

## democracy-and-elections

### [Fifteenth Amendment](../articles/democracy-and-elections/fifteenth-amendment.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Fifteenth Amendment gave all Black Americans the right to vote in 1870. | The amendment only forbade denying the vote because of race, color or previous servitude; it conferred no right of suffrage on anyone, did not cover sex, so Black women were still excluded, and left states free to impose poll taxes, literacy tests and other qualifications that Southern states used to disfranchise most Black men until federal enforcement under the Voting Rights Act of 1965. |

**Why it matters:** Treating 1870 as the moment Black Americans won the vote hides nearly a century of disfranchisement and explains poorly why the civil rights movement still had to fight for the ballot in the 1960s.

- **Q:** Why could Southern states keep most Black men from voting for decades even though the Fifteenth Amendment was in force?
  **A:** The amendment only barred denial of the vote on account of race, color or previous servitude and left states free to set other qualifications, so states used poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses and discriminatory administration, while 1876 Supreme Court rulings such as United States v. Reese weakened federal enforcement.
- **Q:** How did the amendment divide the women's rights movement?
  **A:** Because it protected against racial but not sex discrimination, Lucy Stone and Frederick Douglass supported it as a first step while Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony opposed any amendment that excluded women; the American Equal Rights Association broke up in 1869 and two rival suffrage organizations formed.
- **Q:** How is the Voting Rights Act of 1965 connected to the Fifteenth Amendment?
  **A:** The Act used the enforcement power in the amendment's Section 2; President Johnson asked Congress for a law that would make it impossible to thwart the amendment, and the Act abolished remaining barriers and authorized federal supervision of registration.

### [Voting Rights Act of 1965](../articles/democracy-and-elections/voting-rights-act-of-1965.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Black Americans' right to vote rests on the Voting Rights Act and will expire unless Congress renews the Act. | The bans on racial discrimination in voting in the Fifteenth Amendment and in the Act's core provisions, such as Section 2, are permanent; only the "special provisions" for certain jurisdictions, such as Section 5 preclearance and federal examiners and observers, were written with expiration dates and periodically renewed. |

**Why it matters:** The rumor, which the Justice Department publicly called false, confuses temporary remedies with the underlying constitutional right, and it can distract from the real debates over how the Act's remedies should work.

- **Q:** Why did Congress single out certain states and counties for preclearance instead of relying only on lawsuits?
  **A:** Case-by-case litigation was slow and easily evaded, so Section 4 used a formula (a voting test or device in 1964 plus registration or turnout under 50 percent) to identify places with a record of disenfranchisement, and Section 5 required them to get federal approval before changing voting rules; the Supreme Court said this shifted the "advantage of time and inertia" from those discriminating to their victims.
- **Q:** How did the Selma campaign help produce the Act?
  **A:** Violence against peaceful marchers, especially on Bloody Sunday in March 1965, drew national outrage; Johnson sent voting rights proposals to Congress within days and later credited the events at Selma with prompting federal action, though he had already been considering reform after the 1964 election.
- **Q:** How did Shelby County v. Holder change the Act without striking down Section 5 itself?
  **A:** The Court held that the Section 4(b) coverage formula relied on decades-old data, so no jurisdiction could be required to seek preclearance until Congress wrote a new formula, which it has not done.
- **Q:** What did the 1982 amendment to Section 2 change, and why?
  **A:** After Mobile v. Bolden (1980) required proof of discriminatory purpose in vote-dilution cases, Congress amended Section 2 so a violation could be shown without proving intent.

## depression-and-world-war

### [Japanese American incarceration during World War II](../articles/depression-and-world-war/japanese-american-incarceration.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Japanese Americans were removed and imprisoned because they were caught spying or committing sabotage, so the camps were a military necessity. | No Japanese American was convicted of serious espionage or sabotage during the war; the rumours cited by the Army were unverified, and Congress later found the policy was driven by racial prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership. |

**Why it matters:** The government justified the removal as military necessity at the time, the Supreme Court accepted that claim in 1944, and the word "evacuation" made forced removal sound like protection.

- **Q:** Why do the National Park Service and many historians prefer "incarceration" and "forced removal" to "internment" and "evacuation"?
  **A:** Internment is a law-of-war term for detaining enemy nationals, which the Justice Department and Army did separately to non-citizens; most people in the ten WRA camps were U.S. citizens held without charges, and "evacuation" wrongly suggests being moved out of danger.
- **Q:** How did the government justify the removal, and what does the evidence show?
  **A:** General DeWitt and President Roosevelt claimed military necessity, citing reports of signals and sabotage; those reports were unverified, the FBI's director mocked the Army's intelligence as hysterical, and no Japanese American was convicted of serious espionage or sabotage.
- **Q:** How did the Supreme Court's two December 1944 decisions differ?
  **A:** In Korematsu it upheld the exclusion order 6–3 as a military necessity, while in Ex parte Endo it ruled that the government could not keep detaining citizens it conceded were loyal; the exclusion orders were lifted effective January 2, 1945.
- **Q:** What did the redress movement achieve, and how?
  **A:** Japanese American organizations pressed Congress, which created a commission in 1980; its findings and recommendations led to the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, which apologized, paid $20,000 to each surviving incarcerated person (82,219 people in all) and funded public education.

## early-republic-and-expansion

### [Louisiana Purchase](../articles/early-republic-and-expansion/louisiana-purchase.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| In 1803 the United States bought the land of the Louisiana Territory from France for a few cents an acre. | France sold what it had, a European claim to sovereignty that gave the United States the exclusive right to acquire land from the Native nations who held and lived on it; the United States then acquired that land over the following decades through hundreds of treaties, agreements and seizures, eventually paying Native nations about $2.6 billion in disbursements by one historian's count. |

**Why it matters:** The famous price makes the purchase look like a single real-estate bargain and erases the Native nations whose lands were the real subject of later negotiation, pressure and war.

- **Q:** Why did Jefferson's envoys end up buying far more land than they were sent to get?
  **A:** They were authorized to pay up to $10 million for New Orleans and the Floridas, but Napoleon, whose army had been devastated in Saint-Domingue and who faced renewed war with Britain, offered all of Louisiana, so Livingston and Monroe exceeded their instructions and agreed to $15 million for the whole territory.
- **Q:** How did the purchase test Jefferson's own view of the Constitution, and how did he resolve it?
  **A:** Jefferson believed the Constitution made no provision for acquiring foreign territory and drafted an amendment, but his Cabinet argued it was unnecessary and Napoleon's deadline loomed, so he let the Senate ratify the treaty without one, comparing himself to a guardian investing a ward's money in adjacent land.
- **Q:** According to Robert Lee, what did the United States actually acquire from France, and why does that change the meaning of the $15 million price?
  **A:** It acquired France's claim of preemption — the exclusive right to obtain land from Native nations — rather than the land itself, so the real cost of the territory was paid later through many treaties and cessions backed by force, adding up to far more than $15 million.

## economy-and-work

### [Great Railroad Strike of 1877](../articles/economy-and-work/great-railroad-strike-of-1877.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The 1877 strike was a planned national uprising, organized and directed by labor unions or radical leaders. | The walkout began on the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad and spread largely on its own along the rail lines; almost none of the strikers belonged to a union and no leaders or organization controlled the strike as a whole, although local groups such as a railroad firemen's organization in West Virginia and socialists in Chicago and St. Louis played real roles in particular places. |

**Why it matters:** The strike's sudden national reach and the visible part socialists played in a few cities made it look coordinated, and treating it as a conspiracy obscures the wage cuts and hard times that drove ordinary workers and their neighbours into the streets.

- **Q:** Why did a wage cut on one railroad turn into a crisis that stretched from Baltimore to St. Louis?
  **A:** Railroad workers had already suffered years of depression and repeated wage cuts since 1873, so when the Baltimore and Ohio announced another ten percent cut, the walkout at Martinsburg spread along the connected trunk lines, drew in unemployed people and workers in other trades, and local police and militias could not or would not stop it.
- **Q:** How did the federal government become involved, and what limits did President Hayes set on that role?
  **A:** Governors who said they could not keep order formally asked for federal help, and Hayes issued proclamations and sent Army troops; he refused the Pennsylvania Railroad's request to act directly for the companies and did not use soldiers to run trains, describing his aim as restoring order, though the deployment set a precedent for later presidents.
- **Q:** What lessons did workers and employers each draw from the strike?
  **A:** Many workers concluded they needed lasting, organized unions, while businesses concluded they needed more political influence and government help, including better-prepared state forces to deal with future labor unrest.

### [Lattimer massacre](../articles/economy-and-work/lattimer-massacre.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The "Lattimer riots" were a violent uprising by armed foreign miners that deputies had to put down. | The marchers had agreed not to carry firearms or clubs, the state's historical marker and a National Park Service study describe them as unarmed, and over a dozen marchers were shot in the back while fleeing; exactly how the first shots came is still debated. |

**Why it matters:** Early newspaper accounts came largely from the sheriff and coal-company sympathizers, the trial records have been lost, and nativist hostility toward Slavic immigrants made the "riot" label easy to accept, so some newspapers still used it in the 1930s.

- **Q:** Why were the Lattimer marchers on strike, and why were immigrant miners especially vulnerable in 1897?
  **A:** They were protesting low wages, high prices for supplies the company sold them and abuses by company-town staff. As recent Polish, Slovak and Lithuanian immigrants, none of them citizens, they faced nativist hostility, a new state tax on employers of non-citizens, and a sheriff's posse drawn from Anglo-American townspeople and company agents.
- **Q:** How did a massacre that ended in acquittals end up strengthening the miners' union?
  **A:** The killings united the region's immigrant communities and disproved the idea that Slavic and Italian workers were docile; within four months more than 15,000 anthracite workers joined the United Mine Workers, which under John Mitchell led the 1900 and 1902 strikes that brought presidential intervention.
- **Q:** Why did the Lattimer massacre fade from national memory, and how was it revived?
  **A:** Scholars point to the victims' ethnicity, nativist attitudes, the site's rural remoteness and coal operators' interest in forgetting. Interest revived in the civil rights era, leading to a 1972 memorial dedicated with Cesar Chavez, centennial markers in 1997 and an archaeological survey in 2010.

### [Panic of 1873](../articles/economy-and-work/panic-of-1873.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Panic of 1873 was a purely American crash that began on Wall Street when Jay Cooke's bank failed. | Cooke's failure turned the strain into a U.S. panic, but stock market crashes in Vienna in May and September 1873 had already led European investors to dump American railroad bonds, cutting off the credit that overbuilt U.S. railroads needed. |

**Why it matters:** Seeing the transatlantic origins shows how closely American railroads and banks already depended on European capital, and why a crash abroad could ruin firms, workers and depositors across the United States.

- **Q:** How did a stock market crash in Europe help cause a banking panic in the United States?
  **A:** After the Vienna crashes of 1873, European investors sold American securities, especially railroad bonds; that cut off the borrowing that overbuilt railroads depended on, many defaulted on their bank loans, and when Jay Cooke and Company, deeply invested in the Northern Pacific Railway, failed on September 18, depositors rushed to withdraw their money from banks across the country.
- **Q:** Why did the panic fall so hard on Black depositors of the Freedman's Savings Bank?
  **A:** After an 1870 charter change, the bank's trustees had put depositors' savings into railroads, real estate and risky loans; the panic made most of those investments worthless, runs drained its cash, and when it closed in June 1874 its deposits had no federal protection, so only about half of the depositors ever received anything, and they recovered roughly three-fifths of their balances.
- **Q:** Why do historians link the depression that followed the panic to the end of Reconstruction?
  **A:** The downturn pushed economic issues ahead of Reconstruction on the national agenda, eroded Northern willingness to keep intervening in the South, and helped Democrats win the House in 1874, while white Southern Democrats were regaining power state by state; historians differ on how much weight to give the economy versus Southern political violence.

## foreign-relations-and-military

### [War in Afghanistan (2001–2021)](../articles/foreign-relations-and-military/war-in-afghanistan.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Afghanistan's army collapsed in 2021 simply because its soldiers would not fight. | At least 66,000 Afghan troops were killed in the war (SIGAR, 2021), and Congressional researchers traced the collapse to structural causes — corruption that left soldiers unpaid or unfed, dependence on departing U.S. contractors for maintenance, intelligence and air support, overstretched rural outposts, and demoralization after the 2020 U.S.-Taliban agreement. |

**Why it matters:** The speed of the Taliban's August 2021 advance made a single explanation tempting, but it hides two decades of heavy Afghan losses and the design choices that left the force unable to sustain itself once foreign support ended.

- **Q:** Why did the United States go to war in Afghanistan in 2001?
  **A:** After the September 11 attacks Congress authorized force against those who planned them and anyone who harbored them; al Qaeda was based in Afghanistan under Taliban protection, so U.S. strikes began on October 7, 2001, to destroy al Qaeda's base and drive the Taliban from power.
- **Q:** How did the war in Iraq affect the war in Afghanistan?
  **A:** From 2003 U.S. attention and resources shifted to Iraq, where about 150,000–170,000 U.S. troops served against about 20,000 in Afghanistan; officials later said Afghanistan was under-managed in those years as the Taliban regrouped and violence rose from about 2005–2006.
- **Q:** How did the 2020 Doha agreement contribute to the events of August 2021?
  **A:** It committed the United States to withdraw all forces within 14 months without conditioning that on reduced Taliban violence; Congressional researchers reported it demoralized Afghan forces, and the later departure of contractors who maintained aircraft and supplied air support helped the Afghan military collapse as the Taliban took Kabul on August 15, 2021.

## geography-and-environment

### [Mississippi River](../articles/geography-and-environment/mississippi-river.md) — NaturalFeature

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Mississippi is the longest river in the United States. | The U.S. Geological Survey lists the Missouri River, the Mississippi's largest tributary, at 2,540 miles against the Mississippi's 2,340, and the National Park Service also describes the Missouri as about 100 miles longer. |

**Why it matters:** The Mississippi carries the larger flow and the larger name, and the two rivers are often added together as one system, so its length record gets assumed rather than checked.

- **Q:** Why is it inaccurate to call the Mississippi the longest river in the United States, and why do published lengths for it differ?
  **A:** The Missouri, which flows into it, is longer (2,540 miles against 2,340 in the USGS list, or about 100 miles longer by the Park Service's count); figures for the Mississippi itself range from about 2,300 to 2,350 miles because agencies measure a shifting channel differently.
- **Q:** Why did Union leaders consider capturing Vicksburg essential?
  **A:** The Confederacy had closed the Mississippi to Northern commerce; taking Vicksburg would reopen the river to Midwestern farm exports, split the South in two and cut a vital Confederate supply line.
- **Q:** How did the Great Flood of 1927 change federal flood-control policy, and who bore its heaviest costs?
  **A:** It showed that levees alone could not hold the river, so the 1928 Flood Control Act added spillways, floodways and diversion works; the heaviest costs fell on displaced residents, including many Black tenant farmers who were segregated in relief shelters and later left for northern cities.

## gilded-age-and-progressive-era

### [Jim Crow laws](../articles/gilded-age-and-progressive-era/jim-crow-laws.md) — Policy

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Jim Crow laws existed only in the former Confederate states. | Most Jim Crow laws were passed in the South, but border states adopted them too, Oklahoma used a grandfather clause to bar Black voters until 1915, and Black Northerners met segregation in schools and public accommodations. |

**Why it matters:** Treating segregation as a purely regional custom hides how widely racial discrimination operated and why the civil rights movement sought national laws to end it.

- **Q:** Why did historians argue for decades about when Jim Crow began?
  **A:** C. Vann Woodward held that legal segregation was not inevitable and arrived mainly in the 1890s after a more fluid period, while critics such as Joel Williamson and Howard Rabinowitz found segregation by custom, and in some places by law, already in place during Reconstruction; the dispute shapes whether Jim Crow is seen as a legacy of slavery or a new system built by later choices.
- **Q:** How did southern states keep Black citizens from voting despite the Fifteenth Amendment?
  **A:** They used poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, white primaries and new state constitutions such as Louisiana's of 1898 and Alabama's of 1901, enforced alongside economic intimidation and violence including lynching.
- **Q:** Which federal actions dismantled the Jim Crow system?
  **A:** Court rulings such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954), Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections (1966) and Loving v. Virginia (1967), together with the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and the Civil Rights Act of 1968.

### [Plessy v. Ferguson](../articles/gilded-age-and-progressive-era/plessy-v-ferguson.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Supreme Court's majority opinion in Plessy announced the phrase "separate but equal." | The majority opinion speaks of the statute's "equal but separate" accommodations; the words "separate but equal" appear in Justice Harlan's dissent, and the label for the doctrine became common afterward. |

**Why it matters:** Knowing the exact wording shows that the Court was approving a state statute's own formula, and that the promise of equality was a legislative claim the majority accepted rather than tested.

- **Q:** Why did Homer Plessy board a whites-only car on a train that stayed inside Louisiana rather than an interstate train?
  **A:** Weeks earlier, in Abbott v. Hicks, the Louisiana Supreme Court had ruled that the Separate Car Act did not apply to interstate passengers, so the Comité des Citoyens needed an intrastate trip to test the law's constitutionality directly.
- **Q:** How did Justice Brown's majority answer the argument that forced separation stamped Black citizens as inferior?
  **A:** He said any such stigma did not come from the law itself but from how Black Americans chose to interpret it, and he treated segregation as a reasonable use of a state's police power that could take established customs into account.
- **Q:** Why is Plessy considered so consequential even though it concerned railroad cars?
  **A:** By holding that state-mandated separation did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment if accommodations were nominally equal, it gave legal cover to segregation in schools, transportation and public life for more than half a century, until Brown v. Board of Education rejected the doctrine in public education in 1954.

## native-nations

### [Indian Removal Act](../articles/native-nations/indian-removal-act.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Indian Removal Act itself ordered the army to drive Native nations out of the East. | The act authorized the president to offer western lands in exchange for eastern homelands to nations that chose to make the exchange, and appropriated $500,000; the force came afterwards, through treaties obtained under heavy pressure — the Cherokee treaty was signed by a faction without the authority of the nation's government — and through troops sent to enforce them. |

**Why it matters:** Knowing that the law was framed as voluntary exchange shows how coercion operated through treaties, state laws and enforcement decisions, and why the Cherokee and others fought removal in the courts and in Congress rather than simply in the field.

- **Q:** If the Indian Removal Act was written as a voluntary exchange of lands, how did it lead to the forced removal of the Cherokee Nation?
  **A:** The act let the president negotiate exchange treaties. When the Cherokee government under John Ross refused, federal agents concluded the Treaty of New Echota with a minority Treaty Party that had no authority to sign it; the Senate ratified it by a single vote, and in 1838 President Van Buren sent the army to round Cherokees into stockades and march them west.
- **Q:** Why did the Supreme Court's ruling in Worcester v. Georgia fail to stop removal?
  **A:** The Court held that Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory, but neither Georgia nor President Jackson enforced the decision, so state pressure on Cherokee land continued.
- **Q:** Why do estimates of how many people were removed range from about 50,000 to about 88,000?
  **A:** The figures count different things — about 50,000 eastern Native people removed under Jackson's treaties, about 70,000 from the five southeastern nations over several decades, and about 88,000 including northern nations removed in the 1830s and 1840s.

## people-who-shaped-america

### [Frederick Douglass](../articles/people-who-shaped-america/frederick-douglass.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Douglass was born on February 14, 1818, a birthday recorded at the time. | No record gives the day. Douglass believed he was born about 1817; a ledger kept by his enslaver Aaron Anthony notes February 1818, possibly added later, and Douglass chose February 14 to celebrate. |

**Why it matters:** Enslaved people were routinely denied knowledge of their own birthdays, and the uncertainty is itself evidence of what slavery took from them.

- **Q:** Why did publishing his Narrative in 1845 put Douglass in danger, and how was that danger resolved?
  **A:** The book named the people and places of his enslavement, exposing him as a fugitive who could be seized; he toured Britain and Ireland, where supporters raised $711.66 to buy his freedom from Hugh Auld, so he returned to the United States legally free in 1847.
- **Q:** How did Douglass's view of the Constitution change, and why does it matter?
  **A:** He first held William Lloyd Garrison's view that the Constitution was a proslavery compact, but between about 1848 and 1852 came to read it as an antislavery document; that let him work through politics and the law against slavery and broke his alliance with Garrison.
- **Q:** Why did Douglass support the Fifteenth Amendment even though it did not enfranchise women?
  **A:** He backed women's suffrage, but argued in 1869 that for Black Americans in the former slave states the vote was a matter of life and death, so Black men's voting rights could not wait for a separate women's amendment.

## presidents-and-founders

### [Abraham Lincoln](../articles/presidents-and-founders/abraham-lincoln.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Lincoln dashed off the Gettysburg Address on the back of an envelope on the train to Gettysburg. | Lincoln worked on the speech in the weeks before November 19, 1863; a Library of Congress copy begins in ink on Executive Mansion stationery written in Washington, and the drafts are in steady handwriting unlike a moving train's. |

**Why it matters:** The envelope story turns a carefully revised speech into a lucky improvisation and hides how deliberately Lincoln shaped his public words.

- **Q:** Why did Lincoln win the presidency in 1860 with less than 40 percent of the popular vote?
  **A:** Four major candidates split the vote; Lincoln carried every free state, which gave him 180 of 303 electoral votes, a clear Electoral College majority, even though he was not on the ballot in much of the South.
- **Q:** How did the actions of enslaved people shape Lincoln's emancipation policy, according to historians such as Ira Berlin?
  **A:** By fleeing to Union lines and offering labor and military service, enslaved people forced Union officers, Congress and the president to decide their status; Lincoln, who had first favored gradual, compensated emancipation and colonization, moved toward the Emancipation Proclamation as that pressure grew.
- **Q:** Why do historians debate Lincoln's title of "Great Emancipator"?
  **A:** Some see his leadership as the decisive force that ended slavery, while others argue he was a reluctant emancipator and that enslaved people were the prime movers; the debate is about how to weigh presidential leadership against the agency of the enslaved.

### [George Washington](../articles/presidents-and-founders/george-washington.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| George Washington wore wooden false teeth. | Mount Vernon finds no wood in any of his dentures; they were made of human teeth, ivory, metal and probably cow and horse teeth, and staining could make them look like wood. |

**Why it matters:** The myth hides a harder story, since some of the human teeth available to him may have come from enslaved people at Mount Vernon.

- **Q:** Why did Washington's will free only 123 of the 317 enslaved people living at Mount Vernon in 1799?
  **A:** Only 123 belonged to him; 153 were "dower" slaves from the estate of Martha Washington's first husband, which neither spouse could legally free and which passed to the Custis grandchildren, and 40 more were rented from a neighbor.
- **Q:** How did the Western Confederacy's victory over Arthur St. Clair in 1791 change Washington's frontier policy?
  **A:** After more than 630 of St. Clair's roughly 930 soldiers were killed, Washington and Congress built a larger, professionally trained Legion of the United States under Anthony Wayne, which won at Fallen Timbers in 1794 and forced the 1795 Treaty of Greenville cessions.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about whether Washington stood above party politics?
  **A:** He warned in his Farewell Address against the spirit of party and never joined a party, yet he sided with Hamilton against Jefferson and, in Encyclopedia Virginia's account, led a Federalist faction that voters rejected in 1800.

### [Thomas Jefferson](../articles/presidents-and-founders/thomas-jefferson.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Thomas Jefferson wrote that the government is best which governs least. | The Thomas Jefferson Foundation finds no such line in his writings; the earliest known printing of the wording is John L. O'Sullivan's 1837 introduction to the Democratic Review, and the earliest known attribution to Jefferson dates only from 1853. |

**Why it matters:** The phrase was long associated with the political heirs of Jefferson's Democratic-Republican party, so it drifted onto the party's founder, and a familiar name makes a slogan sound authoritative.

- **Q:** Why did the House of Representatives, not the voters or the electors, decide the presidential election of 1800?
  **A:** Electors then cast two undifferentiated votes, so Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr tied at 73 electoral votes each; the Federalist-controlled House deadlocked for 35 ballots before choosing Jefferson on the 36th, on February 17, 1801.
- **Q:** How did the Louisiana Purchase test Jefferson's own reading of the Constitution?
  **A:** Jefferson doubted that the Constitution gave the president power to acquire territory and considered an amendment, but public support and the land's value led him to set aside his strict reading, a choice that helped build the principle of implied federal powers.
- **Q:** How did Jefferson's 1803 letter to William Henry Harrison propose to obtain Native land?
  **A:** He proposed government trading houses that would draw Native leaders into debt, which they would pay off by ceding land, and he expected Native nations either to be absorbed as citizens or to move west of the Mississippi.

## regions-cities-and-landmarks

### [New Orleans](../articles/regions-cities-and-landmarks/new-orleans.md) — City

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| All of New Orleans lies below sea level. | A 2005 USGS statement to Congress put more than half of the city below sea level, some areas by as much as 10 feet, while the original city sat on natural river levees about 5 to 10 feet above sea level, and those old levees and sandy ridges remain above it. |

**Why it matters:** Katrina's floodwaters covering most of the city made the "bowl" image memorable, but the oldest neighborhoods stand on the high ground the founders chose, and the low areas are largely drained swamp that has sunk since the early 1900s.

- **Q:** Why did the French choose the site of New Orleans, and what Indigenous knowledge shaped that choice?
  **A:** Bienville was told to build a city near the Mississippi's mouth to keep British and Spanish rivals from moving up the river; Native people had told his brother Iberville's 1699 expedition of a ridge (now Esplanade Ridge) linking the river to Bayou St. John and Lake Pontchartrain, which made the spot strategically valuable.
- **Q:** Why did Jefferson want New Orleans, and how did that lead to the Louisiana Purchase?
  **A:** Its port was the outlet for produce from more than a third of the country, and Spain's refusal in 1802 to let Americans deposit goods there angered westerners; Jefferson told Livingston to buy the city, and the negotiations ended in the April 30, 1803 treaty for all of Louisiana.
- **Q:** How did events in New Orleans shape Reconstruction and the fight against segregation?
  **A:** The 1866 Mechanics' Institute massacre helped bring about Radical Reconstruction and stronger congressional action, including the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship guarantees; in 1891 the city's Comité des Citoyens organized and funded the legal challenge to the Separate Car Law that became Plessy v. Ferguson.
- **Q:** Why did so much of the city flood in 2005 when its oldest districts stayed relatively high?
  **A:** Most of the city was built on drained swamp that has subsided since the early 1900s, creating a bowl below sea level; when levees separating the city from Lake Pontchartrain broke during Katrina, at least 80 percent of New Orleans flooded, while the original city on natural levees stood 5 to 10 feet above sea level.

## rights-and-law

### [Equal protection](../articles/rights-and-law/equal-protection.md) — Right

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Equal Protection Clause forbids government from ever treating people differently. | Governments classify people constantly, by age, income, or occupation, and most such lines stand if they are "rationally related to a legitimate state interest"; only suspect classifications such as race, or laws burdening fundamental rights, face the demanding test of strict scrutiny. |

**Why it matters:** Equal protection requires equal treatment of those who are "similarly situated," not identical treatment of everyone, so the real question a court asks is which differences a law may lawfully draw on.

- **Q:** Why did the framers of the Fourteenth Amendment write an equal protection guarantee into the Constitution rather than rely on ordinary legislation?
  **A:** They wanted to give civil rights measures such as the Civil Rights Act of 1866 a constitutional footing that a later simple majority in Congress could not repeal, so that the equal legal standing of formerly enslaved people could not be undone by shifting politics.
- **Q:** How can two laws that both classify people be judged so differently under the Equal Protection Clause?
  **A:** Courts apply different levels of review depending on the classification. Most laws need only a rational relationship to a legitimate interest, but a law that sorts people by race (a suspect classification) or burdens a fundamental right must survive strict scrutiny, meaning it must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest.

### [Fourteenth Amendment](../articles/rights-and-law/fourteenth-amendment.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| By making women citizens, the Fourteenth Amendment gave them the right to vote. | Section 2 penalized states only for denying the vote to male citizens, and in Minor v. Happersett (1875) the Supreme Court held that citizenship did not carry the right to vote; women won a constitutional guarantee only with the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920. |

**Why it matters:** Assuming citizenship and voting rights always went together hides how long women, and Native Americans after the 1924 citizenship act, were citizens who could still be kept from the polls.

- **Q:** What problems was the amendment meant to solve after the Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery?
  **A:** Southern states were passing Black Codes to limit freed people's rights while white southerners terrorized them, and Dred Scott's rule still denied Black Americans citizenship; the amendment made all persons born or naturalized in the country citizens, barred states from denying due process or equal protection, and gave Congress power to enforce those guarantees.
- **Q:** How did the Slaughter-House Cases change what the amendment meant in practice?
  **A:** The Court held that the Privileges or Immunities Clause protected only rights of national citizenship, leaving most rights to the states and turning the clause into a practical nullity, so later protection of Bill of Rights guarantees against the states came instead through the Due Process Clause, case by case.
- **Q:** Why were former Confederate states required to ratify the amendment?
  **A:** White southerners rejected it almost unanimously in 1866 and 1867; after Northern voters backed Republicans in the 1866 elections, Congress made ratification a condition for restoring a state's representation, and Southern ratifications in 1868 completed the three-fourths.
- **Q:** Who was left out of the citizenship clause at first, and how did that change?
  **A:** Native people under tribal rather than U.S. authority were excluded, and Elk v. Wilkins (1884) held that Native Americans were not U.S. citizens even after leaving their nations; Congress made all Native Americans citizens only with the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924.

## science-and-innovation

### [Morrill Land-Grant College Act](../articles/science-and-innovation/morrill-land-grant-college-act.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Land-grant universities are so called because the federal government gave them the land their campuses stand on. | The act gave states land or scrip — vouchers for distant federal land — to sell; the proceeds became a permanent endowment that could not be spent on buildings, and most of that land had been taken from Native nations through cessions, many of them coerced. |

**Why it matters:** The campus story hides where the money came from — New York's scrip, sold to fund Cornell, and Dakota land ceded in Minnesota endowed colleges far from the land itself.

- **Q:** Why could the Morrill Act pass in 1862 when a similar bill had failed in 1859?
  **A:** President Buchanan vetoed the 1859 bill on fiscal and constitutional grounds; after Southern states seceded, Morrill reintroduced it with added emphasis on military instruction and Lincoln signed it on July 2, 1862.
- **Q:** How did a law about colleges in the East depend on Native land in the West?
  **A:** States without enough federal land received scrip to claim and sell Western land, and Western states selected land within their borders; researchers traced about 10.7 million acres to cessions from nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities, for which the United States paid less than $400,000, often nothing.
- **Q:** How did the 1890 Morrill Act reshape the system the 1862 act began?
  **A:** It added annual federal funding and barred racial discrimination in admissions, but let states meet that rule with separate institutions "of like character," which produced the 19 historically Black 1890 Institutions in the segregated South.

## society-and-people

### [Slavery in the United States](../articles/society-and-people/slavery-in-the-united-states.md) — Concept

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Slavery was a purely Southern institution that the North never practised. | Slavery was legal in the North too; enslaved people were 30 percent of Kings County (Brooklyn) residents in 1790, New York's final emancipation came only in 1827, and about 16 people in New Jersey were still legally enslaved in December 1865. |

**Why it matters:** Treating slavery as only Southern hides how gradual and incomplete northern emancipation was, and how northern banks and merchants profited from the cotton economy.

- **Q:** Why did Virginia's 1662 law matter so much for slavery's growth?
  **A:** It made a child's status follow the mother's rather than the father's, so every child of an enslaved woman was born enslaved; slavery became hereditary and could grow through births rather than only through imports.
- **Q:** How did the enslaved population grow from about 700,000 in 1790 to nearly 4 million in 1860 even though importing enslaved people was banned from 1808?
  **A:** Because children of enslaved mothers were born enslaved, the population grew through births, and the domestic slave trade then moved about a million people from the Upper South to the cotton-growing Deep South.
- **Q:** How did slavery reach into the free states before the Civil War?
  **A:** The Constitution's fugitive slave clause required people escaping bondage to be returned, and the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 used federal commissioners without juries to send about 1,000 Black Americans South, provoking resistance in northern communities.

## states-and-territories

### [Pennsylvania](../articles/states-and-territories/pennsylvania.md) — State

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Penn's "Great Treaty" with the Lenape under the elm at Shackamaxon was the one treaty never broken, so colonial Pennsylvania dealt honestly with Native people. | No original written record of a 1682 treaty exists and historians debate whether and when it happened; and in 1737 Thomas Penn and agent James Logan used an unsigned draft deed in the Walking Purchase to take a vast tract of Lenape land, which a scholarly encyclopedia describes as defrauding the Delawares. |

**Why it matters:** Voltaire's praise and Benjamin West's 1771–72 painting turned a possible meeting into a founding legend that still appears in textbooks, while the dispossession that followed is less visible in public memory.

- **Q:** Why did Charles II grant Pennsylvania to William Penn, and how did Penn's Quaker beliefs shape the colony's government?
  **A:** The most plausible explanation is that the king owed Penn a large debt inherited from his father, Admiral Penn, and paid it in land. Penn's Frame of Government created an elected assembly, and the 1701 Charter of Privileges guaranteed worship to anyone who believed in one God, making Pennsylvania an early leader in separating religion from government, although Quakers were always a minority.
- **Q:** How did the Walking Purchase change the Lenape's place in Pennsylvania, and what did that lead to?
  **A:** Using an unsigned 1686 draft deed, proprietary agents had pre-scouted walkers cover more than 60 miles in a day and a half and angled the boundary to take perhaps over a million acres. Most Delawares in eastern Pennsylvania moved west to the Ohio Valley, traded with the French, and many sided with France in the Seven Years' War; the Lenape were later pushed on to Indiana, Kansas and Indian Territory.
- **Q:** Why did slavery linger in Pennsylvania for decades after the 1780 abolition law?
  **A:** The act freed no one at once; children born to enslaved mothers after it passed were bound as servants until age 28, and enslavers could keep registered people enslaved. The state recorded its last known enslaved person in the 1850s, and enslavers like President Washington rotated enslaved people in and out of Philadelphia to evade the residency rules.
- **Q:** How did Pennsylvania's resources make it the "Arsenal of America," and what labor conflicts came with that growth?
  **A:** Coal, oil (from 1859 near Titusville), more than 10,000 miles of railroad at its peak, and steel (60 percent of the nation's output in 1900) made it an industrial center that produced almost a third of U.S. steel in World War II. Growth brought violent conflicts such as the 1877 railroad strike in Pittsburgh, the 1892 Homestead Strike, and the 1897 Lattimer massacre of Slavic miners.
