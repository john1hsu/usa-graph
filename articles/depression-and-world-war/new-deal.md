# New Deal

The New Deal was the burst of federal legislation, agencies and programs through which President Franklin D. Roosevelt and a Democratic Congress responded to the Great Depression between 1933 and 1938. Textbooks often sort it into "relief, recovery, and reform": immediate aid for the jobless, measures to revive farming and industry, and lasting changes to banking, labor and social insurance.[^s2] Its programs reached into almost every community, and several of them, from Social Security to deposit insurance and the minimum wage, still shape American life.[^s3]

## Origins

Roosevelt named the program before he had one. Accepting the Democratic nomination in Chicago on July 2, 1932, he told delegates: "I pledge you, I pledge myself, to a new deal for the American people."[^s1] When he took office in March 1933, more than a quarter of the workforce, over 12 million people, was unemployed.[^s4] Within 48 hours of his inauguration he declared a bank holiday, and on March 9 he signed the Emergency Banking Act just hours after Congress first saw it; examiners then reopened banks judged sound, and after his first fireside chat on March 12 Americans redeposited close to $1 billion.[^s2]

The special session that followed, the "Hundred Days" of March to June 1933, produced a flood of laws.[^s4] The Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA) paid farmers to reduce production to lift prices; the National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA) created industry codes under the National Recovery Administration and $3.3 billion in public works; the Civilian Conservation Corps put young men from relief families to work for $30 a month; the Tennessee Valley Authority built dams for flood control and electricity, displacing about 15,000 families; and the Glass-Steagall Act separated commercial from investment banking and created the FDIC to insure deposits.[^s2]

## How it works

Historians distinguish a First New Deal (1933–35), aimed at stabilizing finance and partnering with business, from a Second New Deal (1935–38) that moved left, though Roosevelt never used the labels and the line between them was porous.[^s4] The shift came under pressure from two directions. The Supreme Court struck down the NIRA in the *Schechter* case in May 1935 and the AAA's tax in *United States v. Butler* in 1936.[^s4] Meanwhile critics such as Senator Huey Long, with his "Share Our Wealth" plan, Father Charles Coughlin and Dr. Francis Townsend demanded more, while the business-backed American Liberty League attacked the New Deal as a threat to capitalism.[^s4][^s3]

In 1935 Congress passed a $4.8 billion relief appropriation, then the largest in U.S. history. About a third went to the Works Progress Administration, which employed more than 8 million people over its life and built thousands of schools and hospitals and some 570,000 miles of road.[^s3] The Social Security Act created old-age pensions paid for by a payroll tax, plus unemployment insurance and programs for unwed mothers and for people who were blind, deaf or disabled; the National Labor Relations (Wagner) Act protected workers' right to organize and bargain collectively.[^s3] After winning re-election in 1936, Roosevelt proposed in February 1937 to add up to six justices to the Court. The plan failed, but the Court began upholding New Deal laws that spring.[^s4] Budget cuts in 1937 helped trigger a sharp recession in which industrial production fell by a third, and the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, with its 45-cent minimum wage and 40-hour week, was the last major New Deal law.[^s3][^s4]

The New Deal's benefits were uneven. Federal job programs often discriminated in hiring, and Social Security left out farm and domestic workers, groups that included many African Americans and women; the WPA did employ about 350,000 Black Americans a year, and Mary McLeod Bethune and Robert Weaver were among the members of Roosevelt's informal "Black Cabinet."[^s3] AAA payments went largely to landowners rather than to the tenant farmers and sharecroppers, Black and white, who lost work when acreage was cut.[^s4][^s6] For Native nations, the 1934 Indian Reorganization Act ended the allotment policy of the Dawes Act, and nearly two million acres were returned to tribes under Commissioner John Collier.[^s3] Frances Perkins, as Secretary of Labor, became the first woman in a presidential cabinet.[^s3]

## Debates and interpretations

**Did it work?** OpenStax reports that most historians and economists judge the New Deal a great success, yet notes that unemployment was still around 15 percent in 1940.[^s3] William Leuchtenburg concludes that World War II, not the New Deal, ended the Depression; many economists think deficit spending was sound in principle but far too small.[^s4] Economic historian Price Fishback's review of local data finds that each dollar of public works and relief raised incomes by roughly a dollar but added little private employment, and that federal outlays rose only about 8 percent of 1929 GDP by 1939, with tax increases keeping deficits small. He notes rival views: Harold Cole and Lee Ohanian blame the NRA and pro-union policies for about half of the slow growth after 1935, while Gauti Eggertsson credits the NRA with breaking deflationary expectations.[^s6]

**Why were farm and domestic workers excluded?** Scholars including Robert Lieberman and Linda Gordon argue that Southern Democrats in Congress excluded these jobs to keep Black workers out of Social Security; 65 percent of Black workers held such jobs in 1930. Social Security Administration historian Larry DeWitt counters that the record points to Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau's concern about collecting payroll taxes from scattered workers, notes that about three-quarters of those excluded were white, and points out that coverage was extended in 1950 and 1954.[^s5]
