# Plessy v. Ferguson

*Plessy v. Ferguson* is the U.S. Supreme Court decision of May 18, 1896, that upheld a Louisiana statute requiring railroads to provide "equal but separate accommodations for the white and colored races."[^s2] By a vote of 7 to 1, the Court held that state-mandated separation of the races did not violate the Thirteenth or Fourteenth Amendments, so long as the separate facilities were supposedly equal.[^s3] The ruling gave constitutional cover to racial segregation for more than half a century, although the separate facilities it allowed were rarely equal in practice.[^s5]

## Background

During Reconstruction, Black Louisianans won rights that included integrated schools and access to public transit, but many of those gains were stripped away after federal troops withdrew in 1877.[^s5] Nationally, the Supreme Court narrowed the reach of the Reconstruction Amendments: in the *Civil Rights Cases* of 1883 it held that the Fourteenth Amendment restrained discrimination by governments rather than private businesses, leaving key parts of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 unenforceable over the lone dissent of Justice John Marshall Harlan.[^s4]

In 1890 Louisiana passed the Separate Car Act (Act No. 111).[^s1] Violators faced a $25 fine or 20 days in jail.[^s2] In 1891 eighteen New Orleans residents, many of them from the city's Black Creole elite and including Republican activists, writers, lawyers, businessmen, educators and Union army veterans, formed the Comité des Citoyens to fight the law; they raised money, held rallies and hired attorneys, including Albion Winegar Tourgée, who would argue the case in Washington.[^s1]

The committee planned test cases. Musician Daniel Desdunes was arrested on an interstate train in February 1892, but his case ended when the Louisiana Supreme Court ruled in *Abbott v. Hicks* that the act could not govern interstate passengers.[^s1] The committee then turned to a trip within the state. On June 7, 1892, Homer Plessy, a New Orleans shoemaker born in 1863 whose birth record listed both parents as free people of color, bought a first-class ticket to Covington and sat in the coach reserved for whites; he was arrested at the Press Street rail yards.[^s1][^s6] The National Archives notes that Plessy was seven-eighths white and that the East Louisiana Railroad cooperated in the test.[^s2] The National Park Service describes him as a man of mixed race.[^s4]

## The decision

Judge John Howard Ferguson of the Orleans Parish Criminal Court ruled against Plessy on November 18, 1892, and the Louisiana Supreme Court upheld that ruling in December.[^s1] Tourgée argued the appeal before the U.S. Supreme Court in April 1896.[^s4] Justice David Brewer did not take part, and the Court divided seven to one.[^s3]

Writing for the majority, Justice Henry Billings Brown found the Thirteenth Amendment claim too weak to need argument, reasoning that a legal distinction based on race did not revive involuntary servitude.[^s3] On the Fourteenth Amendment, he held that separation laws were a valid exercise of a state's police power if they were reasonable, and that legislatures could judge reasonableness by reference to established customs and traditions.[^s3] He cited the Massachusetts case *Roberts v. City of Boston* (1849), which had upheld separate schools, and argued that if enforced separation marked Black citizens as inferior, that meaning came not from the law but from their own reading of it.[^s3]

Harlan dissented alone. He argued that the law interfered with personal liberty and was not a reasonable use of state power, and he wrote, "Our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens."[^s3][^s4] He predicted the decision would prove as harmful as *Dred Scott*.[^s3] His dissent also contained a disparaging passage about Chinese people, contrasting their exclusion from citizenship with the treatment of Black citizens, a reminder that his defense of equality had limits.[^s3]

## Legacy

The ruling allowed states to continue legal discrimination after slavery's abolition so long as it took a "separate but equal" form.[^s4] Jim Crow laws, passed in a majority of states in both the North and the South, segregated transportation, restricted voting, limited access to education and banned interracial marriage, and they were enforced by individual, community and state violence.[^s5] Plessy himself returned to court on January 11, 1897, pleaded guilty and paid the $25 fine.[^s1] He died in 1925 with the conviction still on his record.[^s6]

In *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954), almost exactly 58 years later, the Supreme Court rejected the doctrine for public schools.[^s4] Descendants of Plessy and Ferguson later formed a nonprofit for civil rights education; a historical marker at the arrest site was dedicated at a ceremony on February 12, 2009, that members of both families attended.[^s1][^s6] On January 5, 2022, at the request of the New Orleans district attorney under a new state law, Governor John Bel Edwards granted Plessy a full posthumous pardon.[^s6]

## Connections

The decision **interprets** the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments, holding that neither barred state-imposed segregation, and it **interprets** Louisiana's Separate Car Act, upholding it as a reasonable exercise of police power.[^s3] The majority opinion was **authored by** Justice Henry Billings Brown, with Justice John Marshall Harlan in dissent.[^s3] The ruling **affected** the Jim Crow laws by sustaining segregation in law for more than fifty years.[^s5] The case was brought by the Comité des Citoyens with Homer Plessy as its plaintiff,[^s1] followed the narrowing of federal civil rights protections in the *Civil Rights Cases*,[^s4] and was overturned in public education by *Brown v. Board of Education*.[^s4]
