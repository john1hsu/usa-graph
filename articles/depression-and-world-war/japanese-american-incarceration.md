# Japanese American incarceration during World War II

During World War II the U.S. government forced more than 110,000 people of Japanese ancestry from their homes on the West Coast and, by the war's end, had incarcerated over 120,000, without charges or trial; about two-thirds were American citizens.[^s2] The National Park Service calls it "the single largest forced relocation of people in U.S. history."[^s2] Most were held in ten War Relocation Authority (WRA) camps in remote parts of California, Arizona, Utah, Idaho, Wyoming, Colorado and Arkansas, from Manzanar and Tule Lake to Heart Mountain, Minidoka and Rohwer.[^s1]

## Background

Japanese immigrants, the *Issei*, were barred by law from becoming naturalized citizens; their American-born children, the *Nisei*, were citizens by birth.[^s9][^s3] After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, a presidential proclamation made Japanese nationals liable to arrest as enemy aliens.[^s4] Rumours of signal lights, radio transmissions and "fifth column" sabotage spread through newspapers and the Army's Western Defense Command, though none was verified.[^s5] Its commander, Lt. Gen. John L. DeWitt, who had doubted mass removal in December, was by February recommending removing everyone of Japanese ancestry, arguing that "the Japanese race is an enemy race."[^s5]

## What happened

On February 19, 1942, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, which let the Secretary of War and military commanders declare military areas from which any person could be excluded.[^s1] Roosevelt, accepting the Army's claim of military necessity, bypassed the Justice Department and gave the Army broad authority.[^s5] In March, Executive Order 9102 created the WRA, Public Law 503 made defying an exclusion order a crime, and the first Civilian Exclusion Order removed families from Bainbridge Island, Washington.[^s4] Public Proclamation No. 4 of March 29, 1942, began forced removal on 48 hours' notice; people were held first in temporary "assembly centers" and then sent to barbed-wire camps far from home.[^s1][^s9] In Hawaii, where people of Japanese ancestry were a large part of the workforce, the military incarcerated over 2,000 people rather than the whole community.[^s2][^s5]

In February 1943 the WRA required adults to answer a so-called loyalty questionnaire; those whose answers the government judged disloyal were segregated at Tule Lake from July 1943.[^s4] The same month the questionnaire began, the Army activated the 442nd Regimental Combat Team, which joined Hawaii's 100th Battalion with Nisei volunteers from the mainland; one of its soldiers, Pfc. Joe M. Nishimoto, received the Medal of Honor.[^s4][^s9] Three young citizens, Minoru Yasui, Gordon Hirabayashi and Fred Korematsu, deliberately challenged the curfew and exclusion orders and were convicted.[^s5] On December 18, 1944, the Supreme Court upheld Korematsu's conviction 6–3; Justice Frank Murphy's dissent called the decision a "legalization of racism."[^s6] The same day, in *Ex parte Endo*, the Court ruled that the government could not detain citizens it conceded were loyal, and the exclusion orders were lifted effective January 2, 1945.[^s4] Tule Lake, the last of the ten camps, closed on March 20, 1946.[^s4]

## Aftermath and legacy

Incarcerated people lost an estimated $1.3 billion in property and $2.7 billion in net income, in 1983 dollars; the Japanese American Evacuation Claims Act of 1948 offered only token payments.[^s1] Decades of organizing by groups such as the Japanese American Citizens League and the National Coalition for Redress/Reparations led Congress to create the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians in 1980.[^s7] Its report, *Personal Justice Denied*, recommended a formal apology, pardons for those convicted of violating the orders, an education fund and $20,000 for each survivor.[^s8] In 1983 a federal court in San Francisco vacated Korematsu's conviction after his lawyers showed that intelligence reports had been withheld from the courts.[^s6] On August 10, 1988, President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act, which found that the incarceration was motivated by "racial prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership."[^s8][^s7] It paid redress to 82,219 people between 1990 and 1993 and also provided restitution to Aleut residents of Alaska's Pribilof and Aleutian Islands.[^s7]

The National Park Service now preserves several wartime confinement sites, among them Tule Lake National Monument, and February 19 is observed in Japanese American communities as a Day of Remembrance.[^s2][^s4][^s9] Historians and the Park Service also question the wartime vocabulary: "evacuation" and "relocation" were government euphemisms for forced removal, and "internment" properly describes the separate Justice Department and Army camps for non-citizen enemy aliens, not the confinement of citizens and their immigrant parents in WRA camps.[^s3]

## Myth and fact

The claim that the camps responded to real spying is not supported by the record. No Japanese American was convicted of serious espionage or sabotage during the war, while 18 white Americans were tried for spying for Japan; FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover ridiculed the Army's intelligence for hysteria and poor judgment, and reported sabotage of power lines turned out to have been caused by cattle.[^s5] The people convicted in the test cases were convicted of breaking curfew or removal orders, not of espionage.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Executive Order 9066** was the legal instrument for the whole program: it authorized military exclusion zones, and Public Law 503 put criminal penalties behind them.[^s1][^s4]
- **Japanese Americans** were the community targeted, Issei and Nisei alike; most of those held were citizens.[^s2][^s3]
- The program took place across the **United States**, at camps such as **Manzanar** and the **Tule Lake** segregation center.[^s1][^s4]
- It led to the Supreme Court case **Korematsu v. United States** (1944) and to the companion ruling **Ex parte Endo**.[^s6][^s4]
- Its injustice was examined by the **Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians** and redressed by the **Civil Liberties Act of 1988**.[^s7][^s8]
- Questions of **due process** and **equal protection** under the **Fourteenth Amendment** and the Constitution run through the court challenges, which turned on whether ancestry alone could justify confining citizens.[^s6]
