# Marbury v. Madison

*Marbury v. Madison* (1803) began as a quarrel over a single undelivered piece of paper: a commission naming William Marbury a justice of the peace for Washington County in the District of Columbia.[^s2] It ended with Chief Justice John Marshall's unanimous Court holding part of an act of Congress unconstitutional, the first time the Supreme Court had done so.[^s3] It is now taught as the starting point of judicial review, though how it earned that place is itself debated.[^s6]

## Background

The dispute grew out of the transfer of power after the election of 1800. In November 1800 Thomas Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans defeated the Federalists for both the presidency and Congress, but the lame-duck Federalists kept control until March 1801.[^s3] On February 13, 1801, the outgoing Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801, which reorganized the circuit courts and created sixteen new judgeships.[^s4] The act also aimed to shrink the Supreme Court to five members and relieve the justices of riding circuit.[^s5] President John Adams filled these and other posts with Federalist allies, who became known as the "midnight judges."[^s3]

John Marshall played two roles. After Chief Justice Oliver Ellsworth resigned, Adams named his secretary of state, Marshall, to replace him, and Marshall kept serving in both posts.[^s3] Adams nominated Marbury on March 2, 1801; the Senate approved him the next day, Adams signed the commission and Marshall affixed the seal of the United States.[^s3] Marshall's brother James was supposed to deliver the commissions but returned several undelivered, including Marbury's.[^s3] When Jefferson took office, his administration refused to hand them over, and Secretary of State James Madison declined to deliver Marbury's.[^s2]

In December 1801 Marbury asked the Supreme Court for a writ of mandamus, an order compelling an official to perform a duty, directing Madison to deliver the commission.[^s5] The new Republican majority then turned to the courts the Federalists had created. On March 8, 1802, Congress repealed the 1801 act and abolished the sixteen circuit judgeships; on April 29 it passed a new Judiciary Act that gave the Supreme Court one annual session beginning in February and cancelled its June 1802 term.[^s4] Federalists charged that this delayed any ruling on the repeal; the justices did not sit again until February 1803.[^s4][^s5]

## The decision

The Court heard the case in February 1803; Madison did not appear.[^s3] On February 24 Marshall announced the opinion, organized around three questions: whether Marbury had a right to the commission, whether the law gave him a remedy, and whether that remedy was a mandamus from the Supreme Court.[^s1][^s3]

On the first two questions Marbury won: the appointment was complete once the President signed the commission and the seal was affixed, so Marbury had a right to the five-year office, and withholding the commission violated it.[^s1]

On the third question he lost. Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 authorized the Court to issue writs of mandamus, but Marshall reasoned that ordering delivery of a paper in a case filed directly with the Supreme Court was an exercise of original jurisdiction, which Article III of the Constitution defines and Congress cannot enlarge.[^s1][^s3] Because the Constitution is paramount law, he wrote, an act of the legislature that conflicts with it is void; the National Archives summarizes the holding as "A Law repugnant to the Constitution is void."[^s1][^s2] It was "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department" to say what the law is, and judges who had sworn an oath to support the Constitution could not enforce a statute contrary to it.[^s1] The motion was denied.[^s1][^s2]

## Legacy

In the short run the ruling avoided a showdown: the Court issued no order the administration could defy, and Marbury left without his commission.[^s1][^s3] The same term, in *Stuart v. Laird* (March 1803), the Court let a case be transferred under the 1802 act, by implication accepting the Republicans' reorganization of the lower courts.[^s4] Pressure on Federalist judges continued: the House impeached Justice Samuel Chase, and the Senate acquitted him on March 1, 1805.[^s5]

The power Marbury asserted was used sparingly at first. No other federal law was struck down until *Dred Scott v. Sandford* in 1857.[^s2][^s3] In the twentieth century it became the standard citation for the courts' power to review the other branches, a role the National Archives says has never been seriously challenged.[^s2][^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

**Marshall as strategist.** A long-standing view sees a political masterstroke: ordering delivery invited defiance and refusing outright handed Jefferson a win, so by ruling against Marbury on jurisdiction, the Supreme Court Historical Society writes, Marshall gave up a minor power to claim a far greater one.[^s5]

**Critics of the reasoning.** In an 1823 letter to Justice William Johnson, Jefferson criticized the opinion as unnecessary and called its treatment of the mandamus question obiter dictum. Later scholars, from James Bradley Thayer (1893) to Jack Rakove (1997), questioned the doctrine's basis or credited the case's prominence to its convenience as a teaching starting point.[^s3]

**A reputation built later.** Keith Whittington and Amanda Rinderle traced citations from 1803 to 1901 and found that courts did cite the case, but mostly on jurisdiction, mandamus and executive appointments; citing earlier research, they report that the Supreme Court first discussed it as a judicial-review case only in 1887.[^s6] They argue that commentators, the 1903 centennial, battles over judicial power in the Lochner era and *Cooper v. Aaron* (1958) turned it into the fountainhead of judicial review, concluding that "Marshall had the benefit of great publicists."[^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** the case is the best-known exercise of judicial review.[^s2][^s3]
- **Author:** John Marshall wrote the unanimous opinion after sealing Marbury's commission as secretary of state.[^s1][^s3]
- **Interprets:** it read Article III of the Constitution as fixing the Court's original jurisdiction and held Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 void insofar as it added to it.[^s1][^s3]
- **Era:** it belongs to the early republic, when the limits of judicial authority were still unsettled.[^s6]
- **Challenged by:** Thomas Jefferson later criticized the opinion as unnecessary dictum.[^s3]
- **Related cases:** *Stuart v. Laird*, *Dred Scott v. Sandford* and *Cooper v. Aaron*, described above.[^s3][^s4][^s6]
