# Jim Crow laws

Jim Crow laws were the state and local laws that enforced racial segregation in the American South from the end of Reconstruction in 1877 until the civil rights era.[^s3] Together with the customs and etiquette that surrounded them, they segregated and demeaned African Americans from roughly the 1870s to the 1960s.[^s1] They separated the races in nearly every public setting, stripped most Black Southerners of the vote, and rested on intimidation, terror and lynching.[^s4][^s5][^s2]

The name came from the stage. The white entertainer Thomas Dartmouth Rice first performed the blackface minstrel routine "Jump Jim Crow" around 1828 and toured it through the 1830s and 1840s, claiming the character was modeled on an enslaved man.[^s3][^s1] The name became a slur for Black Americans and then a label for segregation, though exactly how is unclear.[^s3][^s1]

## Origins

Slavery made segregation laws largely unnecessary in the South, though some cities practiced informal segregation before 1860, and free Black people in the North often faced segregation more rigid than in the South.[^s3] After emancipation, southern states moved quickly to control freed people: Alabama's 1865 constitution banned interracial marriage, and its Black Code of December 1865 controlled Black workers' labor and movement with vagrancy and loitering penalties.[^s5]

Reconstruction briefly opened another path. Alabama's 1868 constitution promised equal protection and votes for men over 21 regardless of race, New Orleans schools were integrated until 1877, and in 1872 Nashville barber Sampson W. Keeble became the first African American elected to Tennessee's General Assembly.[^s5][^s3][^s2] Tennessee's Chapter 130 of 1875 permitted discrimination in hotels, theaters, trains and streetcars, and its 1881 law ordering separate railroad cars is often called the first Jim Crow law.[^s2] In the *Civil Rights Cases* of 1883 the Supreme Court struck down key parts of the Civil Rights Act of 1875, ruling that the Fourteenth Amendment did not reach discrimination by private individuals.[^s2] Between 1887 and 1892 nine states, including Louisiana with its 1890 Separate Car Act, required racial separation on public conveyances, and in 1896 *Plessy v. Ferguson* upheld such laws.[^s3]

## How it works

**Segregation in daily life.** Beginning in the 1890s, southern states systematically separated the races in nearly every public setting, marking facilities "White Only" and "Colored."[^s4] Hospitals, prisons, schools, churches and cemeteries were divided, and Black facilities were often inferior or nonexistent.[^s1] Louisiana's constitution of 1898 mandated segregated public schools, and Alabama's of 1901 required an entirely segregated school system and barred interracial marriage.[^s7][^s5] States also wrote race itself into law: Alabama's 1901 constitution counted anyone with a Black great-grandparent as Black, and in 1927 its legislature moved to a rule that any Black ancestry made a person Black.[^s5] Discrimination reached other groups too; a Dallas restaurant association distributed a sign barring Black and Mexican customers alongside dogs.[^s4]

**Disfranchisement.** Despite the Fifteenth Amendment, southern and border states used poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses and the white primary to keep Black citizens from voting.[^s4] Tennessee adopted a statewide poll tax in 1889–1890 through a legislature that had no African American members, and by 1904 every former Confederate state required some form of poll tax.[^s2] Louisiana's 1898 and Alabama's 1901 constitutions sharply curtailed Black voting; Alabama's also disfranchised many working-class white voters.[^s7][^s5]

**Violence and enforcement.** Some historians stress that force and terror, economic intimidation and psychological control sustained segregation beyond the letter of the law.[^s5] The Tuskegee Institute recorded 3,446 Black and 1,297 white victims of lynching between 1882 and 1968, and lynchings averaged 187 a year in the 1890s.[^s2] The Louisiana State Museum counts more than 4,000 Black Americans lynched in southern states in 1877–1950, 549 in Louisiana.[^s7]

**Resistance and dismantling.** Black Southerners resisted from the start, as in Mobile's 1901–1902 streetcar boycott.[^s5] The NAACP took disfranchisement to federal court: *Guinn v. United States* (1915) struck down Oklahoma's grandfather clause, and later suits rejected Texas white-primary schemes in 1927 and 1932, though Texas Democrats kept finding new ways to exclude Black voters.[^s4] *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) declared school segregation unconstitutional, and the Montgomery Bus Boycott began the next year.[^s3][^s5] The Civil Rights Act signed on July 2, 1964, outlawed segregation in businesses such as theaters, restaurants and hotels and banned employment discrimination.[^s8] President Lyndon Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act on August 6, 1965, and in 1966 the Supreme Court held Virginia's poll tax unconstitutional.[^s9] On June 12, 1967, a unanimous Court in *Loving v. Virginia* struck down Virginia's ban on interracial marriage, and the Civil Rights Act of 1968 barred housing discrimination.[^s10][^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what the laws did but have long disputed when and why they arose. In *The Strange Career of Jim Crow* (1955), C. Vann Woodward argued that legal segregation was not inevitable: race relations stayed relatively fluid after Reconstruction, and Jim Crow statutes came mainly in the 1890s, when northern liberals, southern conservatives and southern radicals all retreated from opposing extreme racism.[^s6] Joel Williamson accepted the 1890s timing for the laws but argued that segregation by custom had settled in much earlier, so the statutes codified existing practice.[^s6] Howard Rabinowitz found both legal and customary segregation during Reconstruction and, drawing on Black perspectives, argued that segregation replaced an earlier policy of outright exclusion, which some Black Southerners saw as an improvement.[^s6] Edward Ayers placed the start in 1880s railroad segregation and saw Jim Crow as a product of modernization rather than a direct legacy of slavery.[^s6] Recent scholarship asks less when segregation began than how and why it persisted, describing it as "a variegated landscape of rules and norms" that people resisted.[^s6][^s11]

## Connections

Jim Crow **originated in** the southern United States.[^s3] It was **established by** measures such as Louisiana's Separate Car Act of 1890,[^s3] Louisiana's constitution of 1898[^s7] and Alabama's constitution of 1901.[^s5] It **affected** African Americans above all.[^s1] It followed the Black Codes, was enabled by the *Civil Rights Cases* and upheld by *Plessy v. Ferguson*,[^s5][^s2][^s3] and was dismantled by *Brown v. Board of Education*, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and *Loving v. Virginia*.[^s3][^s8][^s9][^s10]
