  myth: The Constitution requires the Supreme Court to have nine justices.
  fact: Article III creates "one supreme Court" but sets no size; the Judiciary Act of 1789 gave it six justices, Congress changed the authorized number several times, and the figure of nine dates only from an 1869 statute.

# Supreme Court of the United States

The Supreme Court of the United States is the highest court in the federal judiciary and the head of the third branch of government. Article III of the Constitution declares that the judicial power "shall be vested in one supreme Court" and in lower courts that Congress may create, and gives judges tenure during good behavior, which in practice means life terms.[^s1][^s2] Its rulings on what the Constitution and federal laws mean bind the rest of the country, which has made it a central and often disputed actor in American history, from slavery to the New Deal.[^s5][^s8][^s9]

## Origins

The Constitution named the Court but left almost everything else—its size, sessions and organization—to Congress.[^s7] The first bill introduced in the Senate, the Judiciary Act of 1789, organized it with a chief justice and five associate justices and created a system of district and circuit courts beneath it.[^s2][^s3] For most of its first century the justices also "rode circuit," holding court twice a year in each district.[^s2]

The Court was due to open on February 1, 1790, in the Exchange Building in New York City, then the capital, but some justices could not get there in time, so it first met the next day.[^s2] It followed the capital to Philadelphia, sitting in the State House (Independence Hall) and then City Hall, and in 1800 to Washington, where Congress lent it rooms in the Capitol; from 1860 to 1935 it sat in the Old Senate Chamber.[^s4] Chief Justice William Howard Taft, a former president, persuaded Congress in 1929 to authorize a separate building; Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes laid the cornerstone on October 13, 1932, and the Court moved in during 1935, its 146th year.[^s4]

The decisive step in the Court's rise came in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803). William Marbury sued after Secretary of State James Madison withheld his commission as a justice of the peace. Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that a section of the Judiciary Act conflicted with the Constitution and that courts must follow the higher law, calling it "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."[^s5] It was the first time the Court struck down an act of Congress, the foundation of judicial review.[^s5] The next time came in *Dred Scott v. Sandford*, announced March 6, 1857, in which Chief Justice Roger B. Taney held that Black Americans were not citizens and that Congress could not bar slavery from federal territories—a decision the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship clause later overturned and that legal scholars rank among the Court's worst.[^s5][^s9]

## Powers and structure

**Jurisdiction.** The Court hears a narrow set of cases directly—those involving ambassadors and other foreign officials and those in which a state is a party—and hears everything else on appeal, subject to the exceptions and regulations Congress makes.[^s1] Nearly all of its work now arrives as petitions for a writ of certiorari asking it to review a lower-court decision. Under the "rule of four," four justices must vote to take a case. The Court receives as many as about ten thousand requests a year and accepts fewer than 2 percent, favoring cases on which lower courts disagree.[^s6]

**The term.** By statute its term begins on the first Monday in October. The justices review about 130 petitions a week, hear arguments—usually 30 minutes a side—into April, decide cases in private conferences, and release orders and opinions through the end of the term.[^s12] The solicitor general decides which federal government cases to appeal, and about two-thirds of the Court's cases involve the federal government.[^s6]

**Membership.** The president nominates justices and the Senate confirms them.[^s6] The size of the Court is set by law: six in 1789, briefly cut on paper to five in 1801 (repealed before it took effect), seven in 1807, nine in 1837, ten in 1863, seven in 1866—a cut widely seen as aimed at limiting President Andrew Johnson's appointments—and nine again from April 10, 1869.[^s3][^s7] The Court's own count, as of 2026, is 17 chief justices and 104 associate justices since 1790, with an average tenure of about 16 years.[^s2] Its membership long excluded Black Americans and women. Thurgood Marshall, who had argued *Brown v. Board of Education* for the NAACP, became the first African American justice on October 2, 1967, and served until 1991.[^s10] Sandra Day O'Connor, nominated by President Ronald Reagan and confirmed unanimously, became the first woman justice on September 25, 1981.[^s11]

**The 1937 confrontation.** After the Court struck down a series of New Deal measures, President Franklin D. Roosevelt asked Congress on February 5, 1937, for one new justice for each sitting justice who did not retire at 70, up to six.[^s8] On March 29 the Court upheld a Washington State minimum-wage law for women in *West Coast Hotel v. Parrish*, with Justice Owen Roberts voting differently than in a similar New York case the year before; Justice Willis Van Devanter announced his retirement in May.[^s7][^s8] Ten members of the Senate Judiciary Committee, seven of them Democrats, signed a report urging that the bill be "so emphatically rejected that its parallel will never again be presented," citing judicial independence, and the plan was defeated on July 22, 1937.[^s7][^s8] It was the last time a president asked Congress to change the Court's size.[^s7][^s8]

## Connections

The Court is established by the United States Constitution, which creates it in Article III, and was organized by the Judiciary Act of 1789, which gave it its first six seats.[^s1][^s3] It sits in Washington, D.C., in the building it has occupied since 1935.[^s4] Its relationship with the United States Congress runs both ways: Congress sets the Court's size and regulates its appellate jurisdiction, while the Court can strike down Congress's laws.[^s5][^s7] Its rulings are bound up with landmark cases elsewhere in this graph—*Dred Scott*, *Plessy v. Ferguson*, *Brown v. Board of Education* and *Obergefell v. Hodges*—and with the Fourteenth Amendment, which overturned *Dred Scott*'s denial of Black citizenship.[^s9][^s10]
