# United States House of Representatives

The House of Representatives is the larger of the two chambers of Congress, the lawmaking branch described in Article I of the Constitution. Its 435 voting members are elected from the states, and the whole House stands for election every two years.[^s1][^s2] Because its members are chosen directly "by the People of the several States" on such a short cycle, it is often called the People's House, the part of the federal government designed to answer most quickly to voters.[^s1]

## Origins

The framers wrote the House into Article I, Section 2. Representatives serve two-year terms; anyone entitled to vote for the largest branch of a state's own legislature may vote for them; and a member must be at least 25, a citizen for seven years and an inhabitant of the state that elects them.[^s1] When a seat falls vacant, the state's executive authority issues a writ of election to fill it.[^s1] Seats were divided among the states by population, and the original formula added three-fifths of enslaved people—who had no vote—to the count of free persons, enlarging the delegations of slaveholding states.[^s1]

The chamber first gathered a quorum in New York on April 1, 1789. Its opening business that day was to elect a Speaker, Frederick Augustus Conrad Muhlenberg of Pennsylvania, and then a Clerk, John Beckley of Virginia; the first five Clerks also served as Librarian of Congress until that post was separated in 1815.[^s3]

## Powers and structure

**Constitutional powers.** The Constitution gives the House two powers no other body shares: it "shall chuse their Speaker and other Officers," and it "shall have the sole Power of Impeachment," meaning it alone can bring formal charges against federal officials.[^s1]

**Choosing a president.** When no candidate wins a majority of electoral votes, the House decides the election. Under the Twelfth Amendment it chooses among the top three candidates, and each state delegation casts a single vote.[^s4] The House has done this twice. After Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr tied in 1800, members voted for about a week before electing Jefferson on the 36th ballot, a crisis that led to the Twelfth Amendment about three years later.[^s4] In 1824 Andrew Jackson led with 99 electoral votes, short of the 131 needed, and on February 9, 1825, the House chose John Quincy Adams on its first ballot with the support of Henry Clay, who had finished fourth.[^s4]

**The Speaker.** The Speaker is the only House officer the Constitution names. Elected by the full House, the Speaker is at once its presiding officer, its administrative head and the leader of the majority party, while still representing a home district. The Speaker refers bills to committees, decides when they reach the floor, rules on procedure, appoints committee members and creates select committees, and since 1947 has stood second in the line of presidential succession after the vice president.[^s2]

**Parties and committees.** Each party's conference elects a leader and whips who count votes and enforce discipline; the minority leader heads the opposition.[^s2] Most detailed work happens in about twenty standing committees, such as Rules and Ways and Means, all chaired by the majority party, and House chairs are limited to six consecutive years.[^s2] Because a simple majority can control the floor, political scientists describe the House majority as governing much like a cartel, in contrast to a Senate where most action needs 60 votes.[^s2]

**The revolt against "Czar" Cannon.** Around 1900 Speakers held far more personal power. Joseph Cannon of Illinois appointed every committee member and chair, chaired the Rules Committee that scheduled bills and decided who could speak.[^s5] On March 17, 1910, Nebraska Republican George Norris offered a resolution to remove the Speaker from the Rules Committee. After a session lasting more than a day, the House overruled Cannon's procedural ruling 182 to 163 on March 19 and adopted the resolution 191 to 156; a Democratic motion to remove Cannon as Speaker then failed, 192 to 155.[^s5] The House later took back the power to appoint all its committees, and no later Speaker regained Cannon's degree of control.[^s5]

**Who has served.** Until 1870 every member of the House was a white man. Joseph Hayne Rainey, born enslaved in Georgetown, South Carolina, in 1832, took his seat in December 1870 to fill an unexpired term, becoming the first Black member of the House; he was reelected four times and served until 1879.[^s6] Jeannette Rankin of Montana, elected at large on November 7, 1916, was sworn in on April 2, 1917, as the first woman elected to Congress, four years before the Nineteenth Amendment secured women's right to vote nationwide.[^s7]

## Connections

The House is established by the United States Constitution and is one of the two chambers of the United States Congress, alongside the Senate.[^s1][^s2] Its seats are reallocated after each census, a process covered in census and apportionment.[^s1] It acted as the decisive body in the presidential elections of 1800 and 1824, and its role in those contests ties it to the Electoral College and the Twelfth Amendment.[^s4] Its leadership history runs through the Speaker of the House, Joseph Cannon and George Norris, and its membership history through Reconstruction-era pioneers such as Joseph Rainey and suffrage-era pioneers such as Jeannette Rankin.[^s5][^s6][^s7]
