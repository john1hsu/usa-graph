# Department of Homeland Security

The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) is the executive department responsible for protecting the United States against terrorism and other threats inside its borders, for securing its borders and transportation systems, for administering lawful immigration, and for leading the federal response to disasters. Congress created it in the Homeland Security Act of 2002, which President George W. Bush signed on November 25, 2002, and it came into existence on January 24, 2003.[^s1][^s2] It was built from 22 existing agencies, and Bush called the plan the most extensive federal reorganization "since the 1940s."[^s4][^s2] By the end of fiscal year 2022 it employed more than 200,000 civilians, the third-largest civilian workforce of any federal department, and its gross budget authority for fiscal 2023 was about $95.4 billion (Congressional Research Service, 2023).[^s2]

## Origins

The idea predated the September 11 attacks. In January 2001 the Hart-Rudman Commission on national security proposed a National Homeland Security Agency to consolidate the missions of nearly two dozen scattered agencies.[^s2] After the attacks Bush first chose coordination rather than consolidation: Executive Order 13228 of October 8, 2001, set up a White House Office of Homeland Security and a Homeland Security Council.[^s2] Many observers doubted that a White House office had the authority to make agencies cooperate, and in Congress Senator Joseph Lieberman introduced department bills in October 2001 and May 2002.[^s2][^s3] A political scientist's study of the period notes that the White House initially resisted a new department partly to avoid enlarging the bureaucracy and to keep the strategy under its own control.[^s10]

On June 6, 2002, Bush reversed course and proposed a permanent Cabinet department uniting the Coast Guard, the Border Patrol, the Customs Service, immigration officials, the Transportation Security Administration and the Federal Emergency Management Agency.[^s4][^s2] The House passed H.R. 5005 on July 26, 2002, by 295–132.[^s3] The main sticking point in the Senate was personnel: the administration wanted freedom from civil-service pay and bargaining rules, which federal employee unions opposed and which a bipartisan Senate amendment sought to limit.[^s10] After the November 2002 midterm elections the Senate passed the bill 90–9 on November 19, and the final law kept most of the administration's flexibility while preserving existing collective-bargaining coverage unless the president certified a threat to the mission.[^s3][^s1][^s10] At the signing Bush said he would nominate Tom Ridge as secretary, and Ridge became the department's first secretary.[^s5][^s11]

## Powers and structure

The act states that the department's primary mission is to prevent terrorist attacks within the United States, reduce the nation's vulnerability to terrorism and minimize damage from attacks that occur, and it places DHS under a secretary appointed by the president with Senate consent.[^s1] It moved whole agencies from other departments: the Customs Service and the Federal Law Enforcement Training Center from the Treasury, the TSA from Transportation, and the Federal Protective Service from the General Services Administration, along with the Secret Service, the Coast Guard and FEMA.[^s1] The Coast Guard and Secret Service were to remain distinct entities, and the law protected the Coast Guard's non-security missions such as search and rescue, marine safety and fisheries enforcement.[^s1] FEMA and 21 other organizations formally joined on March 1, 2003.[^s7]

The law abolished the Justice Department's Immigration and Naturalization Service once its functions had moved, and in 2003 that work was divided among three agencies: U.S. Customs and Border Protection, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services.[^s1][^s6] Separating border and interior enforcement from benefits such as visas and naturalization made DHS the center of federal immigration policy, and resources for immigration enforcement have grown steadily since the early 2000s.[^s12] The department's role in enforcement has made it a frequent subject of public and congressional debate.[^s11][^s12]

The structure has kept changing. A 2005 review under Secretary Michael Chertoff produced a major internal reorganization.[^s2] Gaps exposed by the response to Hurricane Katrina in August 2005 led to the Post-Katrina Emergency Management Reform Act, signed October 4, 2006, which reorganized FEMA and gave it substantial new authority.[^s7] On November 16, 2018, a new law created the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency from the department's former National Protection and Programs Directorate.[^s8] Managing so many merged agencies proved difficult: the Government Accountability Office has listed DHS management on its High-Risk List since 2003, and in 2019 found that the department met three of its five criteria for removal.[^s9] Former officials have testified that the department inherited the operations of 22 agencies but not the management systems to support them, and Congress's oversight remained divided among many committees despite the 9/11 Commission's call for one in each chamber.[^s2]

## Connections

The department was established by the Homeland Security Act of 2002 and is part of the federal executive branch.[^s1][^s2] It grew out of the September 11 attacks and drew on the Hart-Rudman Commission's earlier proposal.[^s2] Tom Ridge was its first secretary, and in 2018 Congress created the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency within it.[^s11][^s8] FEMA's performance after Hurricane Katrina reshaped how the department handles disasters.[^s7]
