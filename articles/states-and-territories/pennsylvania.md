  - question: How did the Walking Purchase change the Lenape's place in Pennsylvania, and what did that lead to?
    answer: Using an unsigned 1686 draft deed, proprietary agents had pre-scouted walkers cover more than 60 miles in a day and a half and angled the boundary to take perhaps over a million acres. Most Delawares in eastern Pennsylvania moved west to the Ohio Valley, traded with the French, and many sided with France in the Seven Years' War; the Lenape were later pushed on to Indiana, Kansas and Indian Territory.

# Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania, formally the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, is a U.S. state built on the homeland of the Lenape people. Charles II chartered it to the Quaker William Penn on March 4, 1681, under the spelling "Pensilvania," and the original charter is kept in the State Archives in Harrisburg.[^s1] Philadelphia, its first capital, served as the national capital during much of the Revolution and was where the Constitution was drafted, and on December 12, 1787, Pennsylvania became the second state to ratify the U.S. Constitution.[^s8] The 2020 census counted 13,002,700 Pennsylvanians.[^s11]

## Geography

The charter bounded the colony on the east by the Delaware River and ran it north to the 43rd parallel and five degrees of longitude west, with a southern line around New Castle that left room for later disputes.[^s1] The name honored Admiral Penn and the wooded land it covered, "Penn's woods."[^s2] The long southern quarrel with Maryland was settled when Charles Mason and Jeremiah Dixon surveyed the line in 1767, ending arguments that began with the 1681 charter.[^s3] The state's land area was 44,742 square miles in 2020, according to the Census Bureau.[^s11] Its western rivers mattered as much as its eastern port: the forks of the Ohio at present-day Pittsburgh were fought over in the French and Indian War before General Forbes took the site in 1758.[^s3]

## History

**Lenape homeland and early colonies.** The lower Delaware River was home to Unami-speaking Lenape, whose self-name roughly means "People"; most tribal members today use "Delaware," a name taken from the English Lord de la Warr.[^s4] Dutch traders arrived in 1624 and Swedes and Finns founded New Sweden in 1638; when Penn's colonists came, about 2,500 Lenape and 3,000 Europeans lived along the river.[^s2]

**Penn's "holy experiment."** Charles II's motives are uncertain, but the likeliest explanation is that he paid a debt owed to Penn's father in land.[^s1] Penn arrived in October 1682, laid out Philadelphia, and the Assembly adopted the Great Law that December.[^s3] His Frame of Government created an elected assembly, and the 1701 Charter of Privileges guaranteed worship to anyone who acknowledged one God; Quakers were influential but always a minority and never an established church.[^s2] German immigrants (about a third of the population by the Revolution) and Scots-Irish settlers made the colony the third largest in British America.[^s3] Slavery was part of that growth: about 4,000 enslaved Africans had been brought in by 1730.[^s3]

**Dispossession.** In the 1730s, Penn's son Thomas Penn and the agent James Logan produced an unsigned 1686 draft deed to claim land a man could walk in a day and a half north from Wrightstown, and in 1737 pre-scouted walkers covered over 60 miles; the line was angled to take perhaps over a million acres.[^s5] A scholarly account says the proprietors defrauded the Delawares, most of whom moved west to the Ohio Valley.[^s5] The Lenape were pushed on to Ohio, Indiana, Kansas and, in 1867, the Cherokee Nation in Indian Territory, where about 985 Delaware removed.[^s4]

**Revolution and early republic.** Pennsylvania's 1776 constitution created a one-house assembly with an executive council instead of a governor, and Philadelphia served as the national capital through much of the Revolution and again from 1790 to 1800.[^s8] On March 1, 1780, the Assembly passed the Gradual Abolition Act, the first such law in the United States.[^s7] It freed no one immediately: children born to enslaved mothers afterward were bound as servants until age 28, enslavers who failed to register lost their claims, and President Washington rotated enslaved people between Mount Vernon and Philadelphia to evade the law; the state's last known enslaved person appears in the 1850s.[^s7] The capital moved to Lancaster in 1799 and Harrisburg in 1812, and the 1838 constitution stripped free African American men of the vote.[^s8] Many Pennsylvanians aided freedom seekers, and the term "Underground Railroad" may have originated there.[^s8]

**Civil War and industry.** Nearly 350,000 Pennsylvanians, including about 8,600 African American volunteers, served the Union, and Meade's victory at Gettysburg in July 1863 turned back Lee's invasion.[^s9] Commercial oil drilling began near Titusville in 1859, railroads peaked at more than 10,000 miles, and by 1900 Pennsylvania made 60 percent of the nation's steel.[^s9] Industry brought harsh conflict: twenty men were executed in the Molly Maguires cases, the 1877 railroad strike climaxed in Pittsburgh, the Homestead Strike of 1892 broke the steelworkers' union, and protesting Slavic miners were massacred at Lattimer in 1897.[^s9] Slavic, Italian, Jewish and other immigrants arrived, and Black Southerners moved north after 1917.[^s9] Joblessness reached 37 percent in 1933, and in World War II the state produced almost a third of U.S. steel, earning the name "Arsenal of America."[^s9]

## Today

After 1945, growth shifted to the suburbs: between 1950 and 1960 all of the population increase in the state's urbanized areas occurred outside the central cities, and the state has lost almost half its farmland since the 1950s.[^s10] The Census Bureau estimated 13,059,432 residents in 2025, a modest rise from 2020.[^s11] The 1681 charter, given to the Commonwealth by the Penn family's lawyer in 1812, is kept in a high-security vault in Harrisburg and shown only on special occasions.[^s1]
