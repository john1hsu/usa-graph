# Obergefell v. Hodges

*Obergefell v. Hodges* is the Supreme Court decision of June 26, 2015, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment requires every state to license a marriage between two people of the same sex and to recognize a same-sex marriage lawfully performed in another state.[^s1] Justice Anthony Kennedy wrote for a five-justice majority joined by Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan; Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Antonin Scalia, Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito each wrote a dissent.[^s2] The ruling settled a question that had divided states, courts and voters for more than a decade.[^s4]

## Background

The national debate took shape in the 1990s. After Hawaii's supreme court treated that state's marriage ban as sex discrimination, Congress passed the Defense of Marriage Act (DOMA) in 1996, defining marriage for federal law as between a man and a woman and allowing states to refuse recognition of other states' same-sex marriages; many states adopted their own bans by statute, referendum or constitutional amendment.[^s6][^s4] On November 18, 2003, Massachusetts's highest court ruled 4–3 in *Goodridge v. Department of Public Health* that the state's ban violated its constitution, and on May 17, 2004, Massachusetts became the first state where same-sex couples could marry.[^s6]

Change then came by several routes. Legislators or voters in eleven states and the District of Columbia legalized same-sex marriage, while courts struck down bans elsewhere.[^s7] In *United States v. Windsor* (2013) the Supreme Court required the federal government to treat lawful same-sex marriages equally, and dozens of federal courts afterward invalidated state bans.[^s3][^s6] By the eve of *Obergefell*, a public-radio tally counts 37 states and D.C. where couples could marry.[^s11]

The case itself began in Ohio, where James Obergefell and his husband, John Arthur, sued over the state's refusal to recognize their out-of-state marriage, including on Arthur's death certificate.[^s3] It was joined with suits from Michigan, Kentucky and Tennessee brought by 14 same-sex couples and two men whose same-sex partners had died. Each federal trial court ruled for the plaintiffs, but in November 2014 a divided Sixth Circuit panel reversed, holding that states had no duty to license or recognize such marriages; the Supreme Court took the consolidated cases in January 2015.[^s1][^s2][^s12]

## The decision

The Court answered both questions yes.[^s2] Kennedy's opinion held that marriage is a fundamental right protected by the Due Process Clause and gave four reasons rooted in precedent: choosing whom to marry is central to individual autonomy; marriage is a uniquely important two-person union; it safeguards children and families; and it is a foundation of the social order, so excluding same-sex couples demeans them.[^s1] The opinion added that the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses are connected, each informing the other, drew on *Loving v. Virginia* (1967), *Lawrence v. Texas* (2003) and *Windsor*, and declined to apply the narrow, history-bound method of *Washington v. Glucksberg* to marriage.[^s1] It expressly overruled *Baker v. Nelson* (1972), which had dismissed the issue as lacking a substantial federal question.[^s1] Kennedy also wrote that religious organizations and individuals remain free under the First Amendment to teach and advocate their beliefs about marriage, and closed by saying the petitioners asked for "equal dignity in the eyes of the law."[^s4]

The four dissents centered on who decides. Roberts argued the Constitution leaves the definition of marriage to the people and their legislatures, warned that the ruling raised serious questions about religious liberty, and told supporters they could celebrate but that "the Constitution had nothing to do with it."[^s1] Scalia called the ruling an assertion of legislative power by unelected judges that cut short democratic debate; Thomas argued that liberty means freedom from government action rather than a claim to government benefits; and Alito described the right as a new one, not a deeply rooted tradition.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

Legal scholars have debated the reasoning as well as the result. Some supporters of the outcome wished the Court had rested chiefly on equal protection and declared sexual orientation a suspect or quasi-suspect classification, which would have reached beyond marriage; others questioned the opinion's emphasis on marriage as the source of dignity, arguing it undervalues individual autonomy, including the dignity of people who live alone.[^s7] Critics in line with the dissents argue that the Court substituted its judgment for a democratic process that was already changing laws state by state, while defenders answer, as the majority did, that fundamental rights do not wait on the outcome of elections.[^s7][^s1]

## Legacy

Congress passed the Respect for Marriage Act, signed December 13, 2022, after the House concurred 258–169.[^s5][^s10] It repealed DOMA's federal marriage definition, recognizing for federal purposes any marriage between two individuals valid where it was entered, and barred anyone acting under state law from denying recognition to another state's marriage because of the couple's sex, race, ethnicity or national origin.[^s5] It does not require a state to issue licenses, and it provides that nonprofit religious organizations need not provide goods or services for celebrating a marriage.[^s5] On November 10, 2025, the Supreme Court denied review in *Davis v. Ermold*, a petition from a former Kentucky county clerk that had asked the Court to revisit the ruling.[^s9]

The decision's reach shows in household data: Census Bureau figures analyzed by the Pew Research Center count 774,553 households headed by married same-sex couples in 2023, up from 425,357 in 2015, about 1.3% of all married couples.[^s8]

## Connections

The case **interprets** the Fourteenth Amendment and **protects** both the right to marry and equal protection, building on the amendment's Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses, which a reader should know first.[^s1][^s2] It **overturns** *Baker v. Nelson* and was **authored by** Justice Anthony Kennedy.[^s1][^s2] It was **influenced by** *United States v. Windsor*, decided two years earlier, and draws on *Loving v. Virginia* and *Lawrence v. Texas*.[^s3][^s1] It **affected** LGBTQ Americans and their families, as the growth in married same-sex households shows, and it is closely tied to *Goodridge*, the Defense of Marriage Act and the Respect for Marriage Act that later replaced that law's definition.[^s8][^s6][^s5]
