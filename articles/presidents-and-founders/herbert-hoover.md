# Herbert Hoover

Herbert Hoover (1874–1964) was the 31st president of the United States, serving from March 4, 1929, to March 4, 1933.[^s1] Famous before his presidency as a humanitarian and administrator, he was in office when the Great Depression began, and his name became attached to its shantytowns; his reputation has since been substantially reassessed.[^s4][^s10]

## Life and work

Hoover was born on August 10, 1874, in a two-room cottage in West Branch, Iowa, to a Quaker family; his father was a blacksmith and his mother a seamstress and Quaker minister.[^s14][^s1] Orphaned by age nine, he went in November 1885 to live with an uncle in Oregon, and he entered the newly opened Stanford University, graduating in 1895.[^s1] There he met Lou Henry, a fellow geology student who became his wife; she mastered several languages, and the couple sometimes spoke Mandarin in the White House for privacy.[^s2] As an international mining engineer and financier, Hoover was a millionaire by 1914.[^s1][^s2]

When World War I began, Hoover was in London.[^s1] In October 1914 he founded the Commission for Relief in Belgium, which fed civilians in occupied Belgium and, from 1915, northern France, reaching more than nine million people with the help of some 40,000 Belgian volunteers.[^s7] After the United States entered the war in 1917, Woodrow Wilson put him in charge of the U.S. Food Administration, and from 1919 he directed the American Relief Administration, which shipped more than four million tons of supplies to 23 European countries and during the Russian famine of 1921 was soon feeding nearly 11 million people a day.[^s1][^s7]

Hoover served as secretary of commerce under Warren Harding and Calvin Coolidge from 1921.[^s1] When the Mississippi River flooded in 1927, Coolidge had him chair the relief effort, which relied on the Red Cross and local volunteers rather than direct federal aid; the work earned him the nickname "master of emergencies" and helped launch his presidential campaign.[^s1][^s9] The relief also exposed harsh treatment of Black flood victims. In Greenville, Mississippi, Black families were held in levee camps under National Guard watch, and about 500 Black men were made to repair the levee without pay under threat of losing Red Cross food.[^s8] Hoover named Robert Moton of Tuskegee Institute to head a Colored Advisory Committee, but took no action on its harsh report.[^s8]

As the Republican nominee in 1928, Hoover defeated Alfred E. Smith, a Catholic New Yorker who opposed Prohibition, by 444 electoral votes to 87 and with more than 58 percent of the popular vote.[^s6][^s2] The stock market crashed in October 1929, in the first year of his term.[^s1][^s10] Hoover preferred voluntary cooperation to federal compulsion: in November 1929 he won pledges from industry not to cut wages and from labor not to seek raises, and he urged states and cities to expand public works.[^s3] In June 1930 he signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, which raised duties to historic levels.[^s3] As banks failed by the thousands, he created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation in January 1932 to lend to banks, railroads and farm groups, and that summer signed the Emergency Relief and Construction Act, with $2 billion for public works and $300 million for state relief.[^s1][^s3] He opposed direct federal relief to the unemployed, whose numbers passed 12 million in 1932.[^s1][^s3]

His administration cut immigration to nearly zero by denying visas to people deemed likely to become public charges.[^s11] Meanwhile local governments and employers, most heavily in Los Angeles, pressured tens of thousands of Mexicans, many with U.S.-born children, to leave for Mexico.[^s11][^s12] In July 1932, after Congress rejected the Bonus Army's demand for early payment of veterans' bonuses, Hoover ordered remaining marchers moved peacefully; General Douglas MacArthur exceeded those orders, drove them from Washington with tanks, tear gas and bayonets, and burned their camps, and Hoover publicly took responsibility.[^s3] He lost the 1932 election to Franklin D. Roosevelt.[^s1]

## Legacy

Out of office, Hoover attacked the New Deal, opposed U.S. entry into World War II until Pearl Harbor, and in 1946 coordinated food supply for a famine-stricken world for Harry Truman.[^s5] In 1947 he chaired the first Hoover Commission on executive-branch organization, several of whose proposals went into the 1949 reorganization act, and he later led a second, less successful commission.[^s5][^s1] He founded the Hoover Institution at Stanford in 1919 and published more than 40 books.[^s1] He died in New York City on October 20, 1964, and was buried in West Branch on a hill overlooking the cottage where he was born.[^s14][^s1] Homeless camps of the Depression were called "Hoovervilles," but his reputation as a humanitarian has endured.[^s10]

## Debates and interpretations

For decades Hoover was blamed for the Depression and his response was judged inadequate.[^s4] From the 1970s revisionist historians argued that his voluntarism and faith in expert cooperation came from progressive social thought rather than reactionary conservatism; David E. Hamilton of the Miller Center notes that scholars now see his efforts as extraordinary compared with earlier federal responses, while still faulting him for refusing large-scale relief and misjudging the crisis, a "mixed and perhaps still damning verdict."[^s4][^s2] Some economists criticize him from another direction: Lee Ohanian argues that Hoover's 1929 push to hold wages up caused a labor-market failure that cut output and hours worked by about 20 percent.[^s13] Responsibility for the Mexican deportations is also disputed: Sara A. Ramírez credits Labor Secretary William Doak with policies to repatriate at least 100,000 Mexicans, while Hoover Library archivists say there is no evidence Hoover himself ordered mass deportations.[^s12][^s11]

## Connections

Hoover **held the office** of president from 1929 to 1933 as a **member of** the Republican Party.[^s1][^s2] He **took part in** World War I as head of Belgian relief and the U.S. Food Administration, and in the **1927 Mississippi flood** as chair of relief.[^s7][^s8] He **took part in** the **election of 1928**, which he won, and the **election of 1932**, which he lost to Roosevelt.[^s6][^s1] The Reconstruction Finance Corporation, the Smoot-Hawley Tariff and the Bonus Army tie him to the Great Depression, and the Hoover Commission to the postwar reorganization of the federal government.[^s3][^s5]
