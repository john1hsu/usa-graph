# Monroe Doctrine

The Monroe Doctrine is the set of foreign-policy principles that President James Monroe announced in his annual message to Congress on December 2, 1823, at the opening of the 18th Congress.[^s2] It took up only a few paragraphs of a long, routine report, and it was not called a "doctrine" until the 1850s.[^s3] Yet it became the foundation of U.S. policy toward Latin America for the next two centuries, and its meaning changed sharply as the country's power grew.[^s3][^s1]

## Origins

In the early 1800s most of Spain's colonies in the Americas won their independence, and in March 1822 Monroe formally recognized Argentina, Peru, Colombia, Chile and Mexico.[^s3] Americans feared that Spain, backed by France and other European monarchies, might try to restore colonial rule, and they were also uneasy about Russia, which in 1821 claimed the Pacific coast north of the 51st parallel, south of its Alaska colony.[^s1][^s5] The new policy drew on older American habits of staying out of European quarrels, as urged in George Washington's Farewell Address.[^s1]

Earlier in 1823, British Foreign Minister George Canning proposed that the United States and Britain jointly warn other powers away from Central and South America.[^s1] Secretary of State John Quincy Adams objected. He argued that a joint statement would make the United States look like a follower of British policy and could tie its hands on future expansion, and he doubted Britain's commitment to the new republics.[^s3][^s1] In November 1823 Monroe chose a unilateral declaration, and Adams was largely responsible for the foreign-policy content of the message.[^s4]

## How it works

The message rested on three linked ideas: separate political spheres for Europe and the Americas, no new European colonization, and U.S. non-intervention in Europe.[^s1] Monroe declared that the American continents "are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers."[^s2] He promised not to interfere with existing European colonies, but warned that European attempts to control the independent governments of the hemisphere would be treated as unfriendly to the United States, which would itself stay neutral in Spain's war with its former colonies.[^s2]

The doctrine was a statement of intent, not a law or treaty. European governments paid it little attention when it was issued, and most doubted the United States could back it up.[^s1][^s3] In practice the young republic relied on Britain, which favored the policy, so the United States could in effect free ride on the Royal Navy; London also obtained a French promise not to help Spain recover its colonies.[^s3] At home, Congress and the public received the message well.[^s4]

Over time the principles were stretched. In the mid-1800s the doctrine, joined to ideas of Manifest Destiny, was used to justify U.S. expansion across the continent, and President James K. Polk invoked it.[^s1][^s4] In 1865 the United States backed Mexican President Benito Juárez against Emperor Maximilian, whom France had installed.[^s2] By the late 1800s the country had the economic and military strength to enforce the doctrine itself, and in the 1890s Secretary of State Richard Olney invoked it in a dispute over Venezuela.[^s1][^s5]

In 1904 Theodore Roosevelt announced a corollary asserting a U.S. international police power, and Marines were sent to Santo Domingo in 1904, Nicaragua in 1911 and Haiti in 1915; Latin American nations viewed these interventions with concern.[^s2] The Office of the Historian notes that the corollary "inverted the original meaning of the doctrine," turning a warning against European intervention into a rationale for American intervention.[^s1] Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy moved away from that approach: in December 1933 his administration backed a Montevideo Conference declaration against intervention, and in 1934 it gave up the treaty right to intervene in Cuba.[^s6] In 1962 John F. Kennedy invoked the doctrine during the Cuban Missile Crisis, imposing a quarantine on Cuba with the support of the Organization of American States.[^s2]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians have long disagreed about who deserves credit. Some emphasize Adams, whom the Miller Center describes as primarily responsible for the message's foreign-policy content; others credit Monroe, with help from Thomas Jefferson and James Madison. The Encyclopedia of the New American Nation finds the most persuasive accounts give the two men roughly equal shares, with Monroe supplying idealism and Adams geopolitical realism.[^s4][^s5]

Scholars also debate motive. One view takes the external danger seriously: fears of a Spanish or allied attempt at reconquest and of Russian expansion prompted the statement. Another notes that by December 1823 the European allies had already dropped any such plans, and so stresses personal and political motives, above all the coming 1824 presidential election; the encyclopedia judges that the weight of evidence cuts against a purely political explanation.[^s5] A third debate concerns whether the doctrine was at heart isolationist or interventionist, and the same source observes that Monroe's officials doubted Latin Americans' readiness for democracy even as they pledged to block recolonization — a tension that resurfaced when Latin American nations later viewed U.S. interventions with concern.[^s5][^s2]

## Connections

Monroe introduced the doctrine in his 1823 message, drawing on Washington's Farewell Address and its counsel to avoid European entanglements.[^s2][^s1] It followed Adams's diplomacy with Spain, including the 1819 Adams-Onís Treaty that transferred Florida to the United States.[^s3] Its greatest reinterpretation came with Theodore Roosevelt's 1904 corollary, which led to repeated interventions in the Caribbean and Central America, and it shaped U.S. relations with Mexico from the French intervention of the 1860s onward.[^s2] Franklin Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy and Kennedy's response to Soviet missiles in Cuba show how later presidents both retreated from and revived it.[^s6][^s2]
