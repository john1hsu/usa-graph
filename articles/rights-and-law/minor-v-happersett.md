# Minor v. Happersett

*Minor v. Happersett* (1875) is the Supreme Court case in which Virginia Minor of St. Louis argued that, as a citizen, she already had a constitutional right to vote. On March 29, 1875, the Court unanimously rejected her claim in an opinion by Chief Justice Morrison Waite.[^s6] It agreed that women were citizens, but held that the Constitution "does not confer the right of suffrage upon any one," so Missouri's constitution could limit voting to male citizens.[^s1] The decision ended suffragists' hope of winning the ballot through the courts.[^s2][^s3]

## Background

Virginia Minor, born in Virginia in 1824, married her cousin Francis Minor, a lawyer, in 1843 and later settled in St. Louis.[^s2][^s5] On May 8, 1867, she and four other women founded the Woman Suffrage Association of Missouri, and she was elected its first president; the National Park Service describes it as the first organization anywhere devoted solely to winning the vote for women.[^s2][^s5]

At a suffrage convention in St. Louis in October 1869, Francis Minor drafted resolutions arguing that women, as citizens, were already entitled to vote under the Fourteenth Amendment, whose first section barred states from abridging the privileges or immunities of citizens.[^s2][^s3] The resolutions were printed and circulated nationally.[^s2] The Minors' pamphlet also urged women to oppose the Fifteenth Amendment.[^s6] This strategy, which became known as the "New Departure," led women across the country to go to the polls and demand ballots.[^s3] Victoria Woodhull pressed the same argument on the House Judiciary Committee in 1871, and Susan B. Anthony cast a ballot in Rochester, New York, in the 1872 presidential election; she was prosecuted under federal law and fined $100, which she never paid.[^s4]

On October 15, 1872, Virginia Minor tried to register in St. Louis. Reese Happersett, the registrar, refused because she was not a "male citizen," as Missouri's constitution required.[^s1][^s2] Because Missouri did not let married women sue in their own names until 1889, the civil suit was brought through her husband.[^s2] Her lawyers included Francis Minor and John B. Henderson, whom the National Constitution Center describes as the author of the Thirteenth Amendment.[^s2][^s6] The St. Louis Circuit Court ruled against her after a hearing in February 1873, and the Missouri Supreme Court, hearing the appeal in May 1873 in the Old Courthouse, affirmed.[^s2]

## The decision

Chief Justice Waite began by agreeing with Minor's premise: women could be citizens, and she was one.[^s1] He then asked whether voting was among the privileges and immunities of citizenship, and concluded it was not.[^s1] When the Constitution was adopted, he wrote, no state let all of its citizens vote, and women had been citizens throughout the nation's history without the ballot.[^s1] Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment reduced a state's representation only for denying the vote to male inhabitants, which would make little sense if the amendment gave women the vote.[^s1] And if citizenship already carried suffrage, the Fifteenth Amendment's ban on racial discrimination in voting would have been unnecessary.[^s1] The Court added that a government denying women the vote could still be republican in form.[^s1] Its answer was that the remedy lay with those who could change the law, not with the Court.[^s1][^s6]

In passing, the opinion said that children born in the country to citizen parents were natural-born citizens, while noting doubts about children of non-citizens that it said it did not need to resolve.[^s1]

## Legacy

The ruling closed the judicial path. The Constitution Annotated treats the case as superseded by the Nineteenth Amendment, which barred denying the vote on account of sex and was ratified by 36 states by August 1920.[^s4][^s3] In the meantime suffragists turned away from the courts and toward state-by-state campaigns and a federal amendment.[^s3][^s6] Senator Aaron Sargent of California introduced a resolution in 1878 with the language later ratified as the Nineteenth Amendment, but it lacked the support to pass.[^s4] Some territories and states enfranchised women first, among them Wyoming Territory in 1869 and Utah Territory in 1870.[^s4]

In Missouri, petitions for a state suffrage amendment came before the legislature 18 times between 1867 and 1901 without success; women won the right to vote for president in March 1919, and the state ratified the Nineteenth Amendment on July 3, 1919.[^s3] Virginia Minor remained active, testifying before a Senate committee in 1889, until her death in St. Louis in 1894.[^s2][^s5] The National Park Service, whose Gateway Arch National Park includes the Old Courthouse, presents the case alongside the Dred Scott case as one of the two landmark suits heard there that reached the Supreme Court.[^s2]

## Connections

The case **interprets** the Fourteenth Amendment, the background needed to understand it, holding that its Privileges or Immunities Clause added no right of suffrage.[^s1] The opinion was **authored by** Chief Justice Morrison Waite.[^s6] It **affected** the woman suffrage movement, which abandoned the courtroom strategy, and it **led to** the campaign for the federal amendment ratified as the Nineteenth Amendment.[^s3] It belongs to the history of voting rights in the United States, follows Susan B. Anthony's 1873 trial in *United States v. Anthony*, and turned on the Fifteenth Amendment's limits.[^s1][^s4] Its plaintiff, Virginia Minor, and the Missouri setting tie it to the Old Courthouse, where the Dred Scott case was also argued.[^s2]
