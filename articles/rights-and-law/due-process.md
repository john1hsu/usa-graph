# Due process

Due process is the American constitution's promise that government must follow fair, lawful procedures before it takes away a person's life, liberty, or property. The guarantee appears twice. The Fifth Amendment, ratified with the Bill of Rights on December 15, 1791, provides that no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without "due process of law."[^s2] The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified on July 9, 1868, places the same limit on the states.[^s6] Courts have read these clauses to require fair procedures, to apply most of the Bill of Rights to the states, and, more controversially, to protect certain rights the Constitution does not list.[^s4]

## Origins

In 1215 the barons who confronted King John of England won a promise in Magna Carta that free men would not be punished except by the judgment of their peers or "by the law of the land."[^s1] A reissue in 1225 became the standard text, and an English statute of 1354 restating the charter used the words "due process of law" for the first time.[^s1] In the seventeenth century the jurist Sir Edward Coke treated "law of the land" and "due process of law" as meaning the same thing, defined by the common law; the founders and the drafters of the American clauses drew heavily on his writings.[^s1]

Colonial charters and the first state declarations of rights generally used Magna Carta's older "law of the land" wording. Maryland's 1776 constitution did so, and Virginia placed the guarantee among its protections for people accused of crimes.[^s1] When the First Congress proposed constitutional amendments on September 25, 1789, the due process guarantee sat in the same article as the grand jury requirement, the bans on double jeopardy and compelled self-incrimination, and the promise of just compensation for property taken for public use.[^s2] That article became the Fifth Amendment. New York in 1821 became the first state to borrow the phrase "due process of law" for its own constitution.[^s1]

At first the federal guarantee did not reach the states. In Barron v. Baltimore (1833) the Supreme Court held that the Fifth Amendment and the rest of the Bill of Rights restrained only the national government; the Senate had earlier rejected James Madison's proposal to apply certain rights to the states as well.[^s5] The Civil War and emancipation changed that. The Fourteenth Amendment forbids any state to "deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law."[^s6]

## How it works

Courts recognize three broad kinds of protection under the Due Process Clauses.[^s4]

**Procedural due process.** At a minimum the government must give notice, an opportunity to be heard, and an impartial decision-maker, a standard the Supreme Court set out in Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank (1950).[^s4] Legislation and general agency rules usually need no individual hearing, but a decision aimed at identifiable people may.[^s3] For civil proceedings, Mathews v. Eldridge (1976) weighs the private interest at stake, the risk of a mistaken deprivation under the procedures used, and the government's interest.[^s3] The Court has treated some government benefits as protected property, as in Goldberg v. Kelly (1970).[^s4]

**Incorporation.** After the Slaughter-House Cases (1873) narrowed the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause, the Court turned to the Due Process Clause instead and has used it to apply most Bill of Rights protections to the states. The main exceptions are the Third Amendment, the Fifth Amendment's grand jury requirement and the Seventh Amendment's civil jury right.[^s4]

**Substantive due process.** The Court has also read "liberty" to protect some rights from government interference however fair the procedure. The first Supreme Court opinion to suggest this was Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), in which Chief Justice Roger Taney reasoned that freeing an enslaved man would deprive his enslaver of property without due process.[^s4] In Lochner v. New York (1905) and the decades that followed, the Court invoked "freedom of contract" to strike down laws regulating working conditions, an approach it repudiated in West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish (1937).[^s4] Later decisions used the clause to protect rights to use contraception (Griswold v. Connecticut, 1965), to marry across racial lines (1967) and to marry a person of the same sex (Obergefell v. Hodges, 2015). The Court recognized an abortion right in Roe v. Wade (1973) and later overruled it, focusing on the clause's original understanding; it declined to recognize a right to physician-assisted suicide in Washington v. Glucksberg (1997).[^s4]

## Debates and interpretations

Procedural due process and incorporation are widely accepted; substantive due process is not.[^s4] Law professor Nathan Chapman argues that the clause's text requires lawful procedure rather than limits on what laws may say, that the doctrine has little historical basis beyond Dred Scott, and that it moves decisions from voters to unelected judges.[^s4] Law professor Kenji Yoshino answers that the Ninth Amendment and more than a century of precedent support unenumerated rights, so the real question is how courts should identify them; he contrasts Glucksberg's demand that a right be deeply rooted in history with the more flexible reasoning of Obergefell.[^s4] A summary published by the Reagan Presidential Library takes the broader reading, describing the amendment as extending both the Bill of Rights and non-enumerated fundamental rights to every American citizen.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Fifth Amendment and Bill of Rights:** the original Due Process Clause was ratified in 1791 as part of the Fifth Amendment.[^s2]
- **Fourteenth Amendment:** the 1868 amendment applied the guarantee to the states.[^s6]
- **Magna Carta:** the clause descends from the charter's "law of the land" promise through Coke's commentaries.[^s1]
- **Barron v. Baltimore and incorporation:** the 1833 ruling confined the Bill of Rights to the federal government, the limit that incorporation through the Fourteenth Amendment later overcame.[^s5][^s4]
- **Equal protection:** the companion guarantee in the same sentence of the Fourteenth Amendment addresses unequal treatment rather than unfair procedure.[^s6]
