# Cold War

The Cold War was a decades-long contest between the United States and the Soviet Union for military, technological and ideological supremacy, waged with alliances, espionage, propaganda and an arms race rather than direct combat between the two countries' troops.[^s2] It ran from the collapse of the wartime alliance in 1945 to the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991, and it reshaped American foreign policy, government, the economy and everyday life.[^s1][^s7]

## Background

The United States and the Soviet Union fought as allies in World War II, but at Yalta and at Potsdam (July 17 to August 2, 1945) the Big Three failed to agree on a lasting settlement, and Truman resisted Stalin's demand for heavy German reparations.[^s1][^s2] In February 1946 the diplomat George F. Kennan sent his "Long Telegram" from Moscow, and in March Winston Churchill warned in a speech in Missouri that an iron curtain had divided Europe.[^s1] In a 1947 article signed "X," Kennan called for "long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment" of Soviet expansion.[^s4] Containment became the basic American strategy of the Cold War, adapted by every later administration.[^s4]

## Key developments

**Containment in Europe.** On March 12, 1947, Truman asked Congress for $400 million to aid Greece and Turkey, pledging to "support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities."[^s3] The Marshall Plan then sent about $13 billion to rebuild Western Europe, aid the Soviets refused and barred their satellites from taking.[^s2] When Stalin cut off West Berlin in June 1948, the Western Allies supplied the city by air until the blockade was lifted on May 12, 1949.[^s1][^s2] NATO followed in April 1949, and the National Security Act of 1947 created the National Security Council and the CIA.[^s2]

**A global and nuclear contest.** The Soviet atomic test of August 29, 1949, and the Communist victory in China that October deepened American alarm.[^s2] NSC-68 (April 1950) turned containment into a costly, worldwide military commitment that Kennan had opposed.[^s1][^s4] In Korea (1950–53) about five million people died, including about 36,500 American soldiers and a majority of Korean civilians.[^s2] Sputnik's launch on October 4, 1957, set off a race for missiles and space that led to NASA and ARPA in 1958.[^s1] As more than eighty nations won independence, mostly from European empires, the rivalry spilled into proxy wars across the decolonizing world, and in Vietnam the United States sent more than 500,000 troops and lost nearly 60,000.[^s1] In October 1962 the discovery of Soviet missiles in Cuba brought the superpowers to the brink; the crisis ended when Khrushchev agreed to remove them, and the United States quietly removed its Jupiter missiles from Turkey the next spring.[^s5] A period of détente followed, bringing the 1972 SALT and Anti-Ballistic Missile agreements and the 1975 Helsinki Final Act, until the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979 renewed competition.[^s6]

**At home.** Fear of subversion produced loyalty boards, blacklists and the career of Senator Joseph McCarthy; gay men and lesbians were also purged from government jobs.[^s2] Defense spending reshaped regional economies, and in 1961 Eisenhower warned of a "permanent armaments industry of vast proportions."[^s1] Fears also outran the facts: Democrats charged in the 1960 campaign that the Soviets had opened a "missile gap," but spy-plane photographs showed the Soviets had far fewer missiles than Americans believed — no gap existed.[^s8]

**The end.** Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms loosened Communist control; the Berlin Wall fell in November 1989, a hard-line coup failed in August 1991, and on December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned and the Soviet flag came down over the Kremlin.[^s7]

## Legacy

The Cold War left the United States with a permanent national-security establishment, including the National Security Council, the CIA and a unified Department of Defense, and with peacetime alliances such as NATO.[^s2] Its arms and space races produced NASA and ARPA, both created in 1958.[^s1] Abroad, American racism became a major theme of Soviet propaganda and an obstacle to U.S. goals in Africa, Asia and Latin America, which gave civil rights a foreign-policy dimension.[^s11] After 1991, Washington's priority became securing the former Soviet arsenal, and the Nunn-Lugar Act of November 1991 funded the dismantling of Soviet weapons.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

**Who started it?** Early "orthodox" historians such as Thomas Bailey (1950) blamed Soviet expansion and saw U.S. policy as a necessary response.[^s9] Revisionists, led by William Appleman Williams (1959) and Walter LaFeber, argued that America's drive for an "open door" for its trade made it largely responsible.[^s9] Post-revisionists such as John Lewis Gaddis (1972) found neither side solely to blame but held Stalin somewhat more accountable, while Melvyn Leffler stressed that American leaders sincerely feared for national security.[^s9]

**Who ended it?** Triumphalist writers credit Ronald Reagan's military buildup and ideological offensive with forcing Soviet collapse.[^s10] Leffler argues instead that Reagan sought to end the conflict through negotiation, that Gorbachev was the principal agent of change, and that Reagan's trust-building made him a "minor, yet indispensable partner."[^s10]

**Civil rights.** Mary Dudziak argues that because segregation hurt the nation's image under every administration from Truman to Johnson, the Cold War helped push desegregation forward, but that a focus on appearances limited how far reform went.[^s11]

## Connections

The Cold War **occurred in** the United States as well as abroad, and it **led to** NATO and the National Security Act of 1947.[^s2] It **led to** the anti-Communist crusade known as McCarthyism and to the space race, which produced NASA in 1958.[^s1] It **affected** the civil rights movement by turning segregation into a foreign-policy liability.[^s11] Understanding it **requires** World War II, whose end left the two superpowers facing each other across a divided Europe.[^s1]
