  myth: James Madison single-handedly wrote the U.S. Constitution.
  fact: Madison's Virginia Plan framed the convention's debates, but delegates rejected key parts of it, such as a congressional veto over state laws, and in 1834 Madison himself rejected the title of writer of the Constitution, calling it the work of many heads and many hands.

review:
  - question: Why did Madison, who had argued a bill of rights was unnecessary, introduce amendments in 1789?
    answer: He had argued that the federal government could exercise only the powers the Constitution listed, but he came to see that voters valued written protections and that adopting them could head off opponents seeking larger changes, so he introduced amendments on June 8, 1789, and pressed Congress to pass them.
  - question: How did the Virginia Resolutions of 1798 later come back to trouble Madison?
    answer: The resolutions said states could interpose against unconstitutional federal acts such as the Alien and Sedition Acts; in the late 1820s and 1830s nullifiers in South Carolina and elsewhere cited them, and Madison objected that his arguments about interposition were being used to justify nullification.
  - question: Why did Madison sign a national bank charter in 1816 after opposing Hamilton's bank in the 1790s?
    answer: After the first national bank's charter lapsed, financing the War of 1812 without its loans and credit proved hard, and Madison concluded that long acceptance by Congress and the public had settled the bank's constitutionality by precedent.
  - question: How did slavery shape Madison's life despite his statements against it?
    answer: Born into the largest slaveholding family in Orange County, he called slavery a violation of republican principles but offered no realistic plan to end it beyond colonization, defended the three-fifths formula, sold sixteen people in 1819, and freed no one in his will.

# James Madison

James Madison was a Virginia planter and political thinker who did more than almost anyone to design the federal Constitution and its first ten amendments, and who served as the fourth president of the United States from 1809 to 1817.[^s1][^s5] He led the nation through the War of 1812, and he spent his whole life as an enslaver, holding about 100 people in bondage when he died.[^s3][^s6]

## Life and work

Madison was born on March 16, 1751, at Port Conway, Virginia, and grew up at Montpelier, his family's plantation in Orange County, where his parents held more enslaved laborers than anyone else in the county.[^s2][^s6] He graduated from the College of New Jersey, now Princeton, in 1771.[^s5] At the Virginia Convention of 1776 he won changes to the Declaration of Rights that widened religious toleration into a right of conscience the government could not touch, and in the 1780s he helped pass Jefferson's statute for religious freedom in the House of Delegates.[^s2]

In 1780 he became the youngest member of the Continental Congress, at 29, and sought in vain to give Congress taxing and enforcement powers under the Articles of Confederation.[^s2][^s1] A 1783 revenue plan he helped shape counted an enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person, the formula the Constitution later adopted.[^s6] After the Annapolis meeting of September 1786 called for a national convention, he arrived in Philadelphia on May 5, 1787, with the Virginia Plan prepared; the delegates assembled on May 25.[^s2] The plan framed the debates, but Madison lost several fights, including his proposed congressional veto over state laws, and he left doubting the new government would succeed.[^s13] He then wrote about a third of the 85 essays of *The Federalist*, and in *Federalist* No. 54 he defended the three-fifths formula.[^s2][^s6] At Virginia's ratifying convention he faced Patrick Henry, and the state ratified on June 25, 1788, by 89 votes to 79.[^s2][^s6]

Elected to the House of Representatives in February 1789, Madison reversed his earlier view that a bill of rights was unnecessary. He introduced amendments on June 8, 1789; the House passed 17, the Senate cut them to 12, and on December 15, 1791, ten were ratified as the Bill of Rights.[^s2][^s8] In the 1790s he and Jefferson led the Republicans against Alexander Hamilton's Federalists, opposing Hamilton's national bank.[^s1][^s4] He married Dolley Payne Todd, later a defining First Lady, on September 15, 1794.[^s2][^s1] When Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts, he wrote resolutions that Virginia adopted on December 24, 1798, declaring the acts unconstitutional and saying the states, as parties to the constitutional compact, had a duty to "interpose."[^s9][^s2]

When his father died in 1801, Madison inherited more than 100 enslaved African Americans.[^s5] He served as Jefferson's secretary of state from May 2, 1801, to March 3, 1809, overseeing the Louisiana Purchase and the failed embargo of 1807.[^s12][^s1] As president, elected in 1808, he faced British impressment of American sailors and restrictions on neutral trade.[^s1][^s10] On June 1, 1812, he sent Congress a message listing British offenses; the House voted for war on June 4, the Senate on June 17, and he signed the declaration the next day.[^s3] Invasions of Canada failed, and in 1814 British troops burned the White House and most federal buildings, forcing Madison and his cabinet to flee.[^s3] Paul Jennings, a teenager enslaved by the Madisons, was in the White House that evening and described it in an 1865 memoir.[^s11] The Treaty of Ghent, signed on December 24, 1814, restored prewar boundaries; the Battle of New Orleans, an American victory on January 8, 1815, was fought after it had been signed.[^s10][^s3]

After the war Madison signed a charter for the Second Bank of the United States in 1816, accepting that long practice had settled the bank's constitutionality, yet just before leaving office in 1817 he vetoed a bill funding roads and canals because he believed such spending required a constitutional amendment.[^s4] In retirement he succeeded Jefferson as rector of the University of Virginia and led the American Colonization Society, which proposed sending freed Black Americans to Africa.[^s1][^s6] He called slavery a violation of republican principles, yet in 1819 he sold sixteen enslaved people to a cousin in Louisiana, and at his death on June 28, 1836, he freed none of the roughly 100 people he held.[^s6][^s5] Dolley Madison later sold people to pay debts; Jennings was sold in 1848 and soon bought and freed by Senator Daniel Webster.[^s6][^s11]

## Legacy

Madison is often called the Father of the Constitution, a title he refused. In 1834 he wrote that the Constitution was not the product of a single brain and should be seen as "the work of many heads & many hands."[^s7][^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians weigh how Madison's ideas fit together. The Virginia Resolutions seem to contradict the strong national government he sought in 1787, and many point to them as a starting point of the nullification controversy.[^s13] J. C. A. Stagg argues instead that nothing in them contradicted his *Federalist* essays, and notes that in the 1830s Madison objected when nullifiers used his idea of interposition.[^s13] Miller Center director William Antholis likewise reads his shifts, from opposing a bill of rights to championing one, as a steady search for a balance between central power and its limits.[^s14] On slavery, Jeff Broadwater finds that Madison condemned the institution in principle yet never offered a realistic plan to end it, defended its westward spread in 1819, and relied on colonization schemes.[^s6]

## Connections

Madison **held the office** of president from 1809 to 1817 and, earlier, **held the office** of secretary of state under Jefferson.[^s1][^s12] He **participated in** the Constitutional Convention, where his Virginia Plan set the agenda, and was a **member of** the House of Representatives, where he introduced the amendments that became the Bill of Rights.[^s2][^s8] As secretary of state he **participated in** the Louisiana Purchase, and in retirement he was a **member of**, and led, the American Colonization Society.[^s1] He also led the country into the War of 1812.[^s3]
