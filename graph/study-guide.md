## civil-war-and-reconstruction

### [Abolitionism](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/abolitionism.md) — Movement

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Abolitionism was a popular Northern cause, led by white reformers on behalf of enslaved people. | Abolitionists were a small minority whom most white Northerners disliked, and mobs attacked them in Boston, Philadelphia and Illinois; Black Americans, free and formerly enslaved, were central from the start, from the 1831 free Black convention and David Walker's Appeal to Frederick Douglass's lectures and writing. |

**Why it matters:** Picturing a broadly antislavery North led by white allies hides how unpopular the movement was and erases Black activists who shaped its demands, such as the rejection of colonization.

- **Q:** Why did the abolitionists of the 1830s reject colonization, which many earlier antislavery whites had supported?
  **A:** Free Black Americans opposed it, declaring at an 1831 convention that the United States was their home, and the writings of David Walker and James Forten persuaded William Lloyd Garrison that colonization was inherently racist, so the new movement demanded immediate emancipation and Black citizenship instead.
- **Q:** How did abolitionist women's work help launch the women's rights movement?
  **A:** Women such as the Grimké sisters lectured and petitioned against slavery and faced backlash for speaking in public; when the 1840 antislavery convention in London refused to seat women delegates, Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton returned determined to fight for women's rights and organized the Seneca Falls Convention in 1848.
- **Q:** How did abolitionists help bring about the Thirteenth Amendment?
  **A:** The Women's Loyal National League, led by Stanton and Susan B. Anthony, gathered nearly half a million signatures on emancipation petitions that Charles Sumner presented to the Senate from February 1864, and Sumner credited the league as the principal force behind the amendment, which the Senate passed in April 1864.
- **Q:** Why did the American Anti-Slavery Society split around 1840?
  **A:** Garrisonians held that the Constitution was proslavery and relied on moral suasion, while political abolitionists believed the Constitution could be used against slavery and formed the Liberty Party; the election of Abby Kelley to the society's business committee led Arthur Tappan and other conservatives to walk out.

### [American Civil War](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/american-civil-war.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Civil War was fought over states' rights or tariffs, not slavery. | The seceding states said otherwise at the time. Mississippi declared its position "thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery," South Carolina complained that Northern states would not enforce the Fugitive Slave Act, and Confederate Vice President Alexander Stephens rested the new government on Black inequality. The only state right at stake was the right to protect slavery. |

**Why it matters:** The postwar "Lost Cause" story recast secession as a defense of constitutional principle. Believing it hides what the war was about and why emancipation and the Reconstruction amendments followed from it.

- **Q:** Why did Deep South states secede after the 1860 election, before Lincoln had taken office?
  **A:** They saw the victory of a party opposed to slavery's expansion as a threat to slavery itself. Mississippi's declaration said its position was thoroughly identified with slavery, and South Carolina's centered on Northern refusal to return fugitives from slavery.
- **Q:** How did enslaved people help turn a war for the Union into a war against slavery?
  **A:** By fleeing to Union lines from 1861 on, they forced the army and Congress to decide their status, which led to contraband policy, the Confiscation Acts, abolition in Washington, D.C., the Emancipation Proclamation, and the enlistment of about 179,000 Black soldiers.
- **Q:** Why do historians now cite about 750,000 deaths instead of the long-used 620,000?
  **A:** J. David Hacker compared census survival rates for 1860–1870 with the decades before and after, and the shortfall pointed to about 750,000 deaths (range 650,000–850,000), since earlier counts relied on incomplete military records.

### [Freedmen's Bureau](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/freedmens-bureau.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Freedmen's Bureau gave freed families "forty acres and a mule." | The 1865 act let the bureau rent abandoned or confiscated land in plots of no more than forty acres, with an option to buy; after President Johnson pardoned former Confederates, the bureau was ordered to return that land to its former owners, a process nearly complete in Georgia by summer 1866, and only a small share of the land freedpeople had settled stayed in Black hands. |

**Why it matters:** Remembering a broken promise as a fulfilled one hides why most freedpeople started freedom without land and turned to wage labor and sharecropping on white-owned farms.

- **Q:** Why did most freedpeople fail to keep land that the bureau had been authorized to rent and sell to them?
  **A:** The land came from abandoned and confiscated Confederate property, and once President Johnson pardoned former Confederates and overturned Sherman's field order, the bureau was ordered to return it to its former owners; by summer 1866 restoration in Georgia was nearly complete, and only about one-sixth of the land settled by freed families stayed in their hands.
- **Q:** How did the bureau's fight with President Johnson help widen the split between him and Congress?
  **A:** Johnson vetoed the February 1866 extension bill as unnecessary, costly and an intrusion on states' rights; the override failed, but Congress passed a second bill in July 1866 over another veto, a clash that deepened the break between Johnson and congressional Republicans.
- **Q:** Why do historians and contemporaries such as W. E. B. Du Bois judge the bureau's record so differently?
  **A:** It achieved a great deal in relief, schools and legal recognition with fewer than 900 agents and little money, but it also pushed freedpeople into labor contracts with former enslavers and could not deliver land, so verdicts depend on whether one weighs its achievements against its resources or against freedpeople's hopes.

### [Reconstruction era](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/reconstruction-era.md) — Era

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Reconstruction failed because vindictive Radicals handed the South to corrupt carpetbaggers and to freedmen unfit to govern, until white "redeemers" restored honest rule. | This "tragic era" story, spread by William Dunning's Columbia school, Claude Bowers's The Tragic Era and films, has been rejected by historians; Southern corruption was no worse than national scandals, Black men held a small share of offices, and the Reconstruction governments built the South's first public school systems before being overthrown by violence. |

**Why it matters:** The legend served as intellectual justification for segregation and disfranchisement for much of the twentieth century, so correcting it changes how Americans understand Jim Crow and the civil rights movement.

- **Q:** Why did Congress replace Andrew Johnson's plan with Congressional (Radical) Reconstruction in 1867?
  **A:** Under Johnson's lenient plan, Southern states passed Black Codes that restricted freedpeople's movement and labor, and Johnson vetoed civil-rights measures, so Republican majorities overrode him, passed the Fourteenth Amendment and the Reconstruction Acts, and required new state constitutions with Black male suffrage before readmission.
- **Q:** How did violence and economic depression help end Reconstruction?
  **A:** The Klan and other paramilitaries terrorized Black voters and Republicans, as at Colfax in 1873, while the Panic of 1873 turned Northern attention to the economy and helped Democrats win the House in 1874; federal will to enforce Black rights faded, and the Compromise of 1877 withdrew the last troops.
- **Q:** Why do historians call Reconstruction a "splendid failure" rather than simply a failure?
  **A:** It failed to redistribute land or protect Black voting, and planters regained power, yet it wrote birthright citizenship, equal protection and Black male suffrage into the Constitution, and those amendments later became the legal basis of the civil rights movement.

### [Siege of Vicksburg](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/siege-of-vicksburg.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Lincoln's line that the Father of Waters again flowed "unvexed to the sea" was his spontaneous reaction to the news from Vicksburg. | The sentence appears in a public letter he wrote to James C. Conkling on August 26, 1863, nearly two months after the surrender, in a passage that also credits soldiers from many regions and Black troops. |

**Why it matters:** Detaching the line from the letter loses Lincoln's purpose, which was to defend emancipation and Black enlistment to Northern critics by pointing to victories like Vicksburg.

- **Q:** Why did both sides consider Vicksburg so important?
  **A:** Confederate guns on its bluffs blocked Union traffic on the Mississippi, on which Midwestern farmers depended, and the city linked the eastern Confederacy to the supplies and men of Louisiana, Arkansas and Texas; taking it would reopen the river and split the South.
- **Q:** How did Grant finally get his army into position to besiege Vicksburg after months of failure?
  **A:** He crossed the river south of the city on April 30–May 1, 1863, won a string of battles inland through May 17 that drove Pemberton back into the city, and after two failed assaults on May 19 and 22 settled into a siege.
- **Q:** Why did Pemberton surrender on July 4 rather than fight his way out?
  **A:** After about six weeks of siege, hunger and disease had worn down his garrison; his commanders judged a breakout impossible, and Grant's offer to parole the men rather than imprison them was the best term available.
- **Q:** How did the fall of Vicksburg lead to Union control of the entire Mississippi?
  **A:** The garrison at Port Hudson, Louisiana, the last Confederate stronghold on the river, surrendered on July 9, 1863, after learning Vicksburg had fallen, opening the river from its source to New Orleans.

### [Thirteenth Amendment](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/thirteenth-amendment.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Emancipation Proclamation ended slavery throughout the United States. | The 1863 proclamation applied only to areas in rebellion and left slavery legal in loyal border states; slavery ended nationwide only with the Thirteenth Amendment, which freed people still enslaved in Delaware and Kentucky in December 1865. |

**Why it matters:** Confusing a wartime executive order with a constitutional amendment hides why abolitionists, Congress and Lincoln fought for an amendment, and how close slavery came to surviving the war.

- **Q:** Why did Lincoln and Congress believe a constitutional amendment was needed even after the Emancipation Proclamation?
  **A:** The proclamation rested on Lincoln's war powers and applied only to areas in rebellion, not to loyal border states; its legal force after the war was doubtful, so an amendment was the only secure way to end slavery everywhere.
- **Q:** How did the 1864 election and the end of the war help the amendment pass and be ratified?
  **A:** After the House first failed to reach two-thirds, Lincoln's reelection campaign put the amendment on the Republican platform and a lame-duck session passed it in January 1865; later, President Andrew Johnson required Southern states seeking readmission to abolish slavery, and Georgia's ratification in December 1865 completed the three-fourths.
- **Q:** Why is the amendment's exception "as a punishment for crime" significant?
  **A:** Southern states used criminal convictions to lease mostly Black prisoners to private companies, as Georgia did from 1868 to 1908, and scholars still debate whether the framers meant the clause to permit that kind of forced labor.

### [Underground Railroad](../articles/civil-war-and-reconstruction/underground-railroad.md) — Movement

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Underground Railroad was a single, centrally run system of "stations" and "conductors" operated mainly by white abolitionists who carried most escapees to freedom. | Historians describe it as an interlocking series of local networks; most flight was started by enslaved people themselves, many escaped with no help at all, free and enslaved Black people were central helpers, and studies of runaway advertisements suggest only a small share of runaways used organized aid. |

**Why it matters:** Abolitionists and enslavers both had reasons to exaggerate its organization, and late-nineteenth-century reminiscences and Wilbur Siebert's 1898 route maps fixed that image in popular memory.

- **Q:** How did the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 change the Underground Railroad?
  **A:** By commanding bystanders to help catch freedom seekers, denying alleged fugitives the right to testify and fining or jailing anyone who helped them, it made even the free states unsafe, so aid became more deliberate and organized and more freedom seekers pushed on to Canada.
- **Q:** Why did Larry Gara's 1961 book change how historians understood the Underground Railroad?
  **A:** Gara argued that Siebert's account rested on embellished memories and that freedom seekers mostly crossed the South on their own, so he shifted the focus from white "conductors" to the courage of enslaved people and the free Black communities who helped them.
- **Q:** Why do some historians think most runaways never used the Underground Railroad?
  **A:** John Hope Franklin and Loren Schweninger's study of runaway advertisements found that most runaways hid in nearby woods or towns and returned within days or weeks, so only a small portion reached organized networks in the North.

## cold-war-and-civil-rights

### [Brown v. Board of Education](../articles/cold-war-and-civil-rights/brown-v-board-of-education.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The case began when Oliver Brown alone sued after his daughter Linda was turned away from a nearby white school and forced to walk far to a Black school. | The Topeka NAACP deliberately organized a class action with 13 parents on behalf of 20 children; Oliver Brown was recruited to join and named because he was the only man in the group, and the Supreme Court decided Kansas's case together with cases from South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware and the District of Columbia involving nearly 200 people. |

**Why it matters:** Reducing Brown to one family's story hides the years of organized legal strategy and the many students, parents, teachers and lawyers, in the South and beyond it, who took risks to bring the cases.

- **Q:** Why did the NAACP's lawyers value the Kansas case even though Topeka's Black and white schools were roughly comparable in buildings and resources?
  **A:** Because the facilities were substantially comparable, the Kansas case forced the Court to decide whether segregation itself was unconstitutional rather than merely ordering states to equalize unequal schools, and it showed that school segregation was a national issue and not only a Southern one.
- **Q:** Why was the District of Columbia case, Bolling v. Sharpe, decided separately from the four state cases?
  **A:** The Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause restrains the states, not the federal government, so segregation in the federal capital had to be challenged under the Fifth Amendment instead.
- **Q:** How did the Court's approach to remedies allow segregation to continue for years after 1954?
  **A:** The 1954 ruling did not say how or when schools must desegregate; Brown II in 1955 left it to federal district courts to proceed "with all deliberate speed," and opponents used that vague standard to delay, admit only token numbers of Black students, pass new segregation laws, or, as in Prince Edward County, close public schools from 1959 to 1964.

### [Civil rights movement](../articles/cold-war-and-civil-rights/civil-rights-movement.md) — Movement

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The civil rights movement was a Southern story, led from the top by Martin Luther King Jr., against a problem confined to the South. | The movement was built by many organizations and local people, especially students and Black women such as Ella Baker, Diane Nash, Clara Luper and Gloria Richardson, and it confronted segregation in housing, schools and jobs in Northern cities too, as the 1966 Chicago campaign showed. |

**Why it matters:** A story centered on one leader and one region makes the movement look finished and distant, hiding the grassroots organizing that powered it and the national scope of the discrimination it fought.

- **Q:** Why did violence against peaceful protesters in Birmingham and Selma help win federal civil rights laws?
  **A:** Televised attacks on marchers in Birmingham in May 1963 shocked the nation and were followed by Kennedy's civil rights bill, and the 1965 attack in Selma drew national attention that helped persuade Johnson and Congress to pass the Voting Rights Act.
- **Q:** How did the 1966 Chicago campaign challenge the idea that segregation was only a Southern problem?
  **A:** Chicago activists invited King and SCLC north to fight de facto segregation in housing, schools and employment; marchers met violent hostility in white neighborhoods, and city agencies later failed to carry out the open-housing agreement they had signed.
- **Q:** How do Jacquelyn Dowd Hall and Sundiata Cha-Jua and Clarence Lang disagree about the movement's time span?
  **A:** Hall argues for a "long" movement beginning in the late 1930s that linked racial and economic justice nationwide, while Cha-Jua and Lang argue that stretching it that far blurs chronology, place and the differences between civil rights and Black Power.

### [Cold War](../articles/cold-war-and-civil-rights/cold-war.md) — Era

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Cold War was "cold" — a tense standoff in which no real war was fought. | American and Soviet troops never fought each other directly, but the rivalry fed hot wars and proxy conflicts; the Korean War alone killed about five million people, including about 36,500 Americans, and nearly 60,000 Americans died in Vietnam. |

**Why it matters:** The name centers the nuclear standoff as Americans and Europeans experienced it at home and hides the war's heavy toll in Asia, Africa and Latin America.

- **Q:** What was containment, and how did the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan put it into practice?
  **A:** Containment, set out by George Kennan in 1946–47, meant blocking Soviet expansion through patient, long-term pressure; the Truman Doctrine (1947) sent $400 million in aid to Greece and Turkey, and the Marshall Plan gave about $13 billion to rebuild Western Europe so it would resist Communism.
- **Q:** How did the Cold War affect the civil rights movement?
  **A:** Soviet propaganda used American segregation to discredit U.S. democracy, especially in newly independent nations, so administrations from Truman to Johnson had foreign-policy reasons to support desegregation; Mary Dudziak argues this helped drive reform but also limited it to improving appearances.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about how the Cold War ended?
  **A:** Triumphalists credit Reagan's military buildup and ideological pressure, while historians such as Melvyn Leffler argue that Gorbachev was the principal agent of change and that Reagan mattered most by negotiating and building trust with him.

### [Desegregation of Little Rock Central High School](../articles/cold-war-and-civil-rights/desegregation-of-little-rock-central-high-school.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Once federal troops escorted the Little Rock Nine into Central High in September 1957, the school and the city's schools were desegregated and the crisis was over. | Troops stayed all year while the Nine faced daily harassment and one of them was expelled; in September 1958 Faubus closed all four Little Rock high schools under a new state law, voters backed closure three to one, and the schools did not reopen until August 1959. |

**Why it matters:** The famous images of paratroopers escorting the students compress a multi-year struggle into one moment of victory and hide how long and how deliberately segregationists resisted after the troops arrived.

- **Q:** Why did President Eisenhower send federal troops to Little Rock even though he was reluctant to act as a civil rights champion?
  **A:** He framed the deployment as enforcing the orders of federal courts against mob obstruction, arguing that if the executive did not uphold court rulings the rule of law would collapse; he also worried that the crisis was damaging America's standing abroad during the Cold War.
- **Q:** How did the Little Rock crisis lead to Cooper v. Aaron, and what did the Court decide?
  **A:** After a year of turmoil the school board sought to suspend its desegregation plan; in September 1958 the Supreme Court unanimously refused, holding under the Supremacy Clause that state officials cannot nullify federal court orders based on Brown or war against the Constitution they swore to support.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about Governor Orval Faubus's motives?
  **A:** Before 1957 he was seen as a racial moderate who had hired Black state employees and supported Black colleges, so his biographer argues his stand was a political calculation to win re-election in 1958, while some observers insisted catering to segregationists was out of character for him; many voters rewarded him for appearing to stand up to the federal government.

### [McCarthyism](../articles/cold-war-and-civil-rights/mccarthyism.md) — Era

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Joseph McCarthy started the Red Scare and ran the House Un-American Activities Committee that blacklisted Hollywood. | McCarthy was a senator who chaired a Senate subcommittee; HUAC was a House committee dating from 1938, it held its Hollywood hearings in 1947, and Truman's federal loyalty program began in March 1947, nearly three years before McCarthy's Wheeling speech. |

**Why it matters:** Naming a whole era after one senator hides how broad and bipartisan the anticommunist campaign was, and how much of it was carried out by agencies, employers, unions and state governments rather than by McCarthy himself.

- **Q:** Why do historians such as Ellen Schrecker argue that McCarthyism was much bigger than Joseph McCarthy?
  **A:** Federal loyalty reviews, HUAC investigations and the Hollywood blacklist were under way before McCarthy's 1950 Wheeling speech, and firings, loyalty oaths and screening programs continued after his 1954 censure; the repression worked through many public and private actors, from federal agencies to employers and unions, not one senator.
- **Q:** How did the televised Army-McCarthy hearings contribute to McCarthy's downfall?
  **A:** About 20 million people watched McCarthy's bullying tactics over 36 days in 1954, culminating in Joseph Welch's rebuke on June 9; his popularity collapsed, and in December the Senate voted 67 to 22 to censure him.
- **Q:** How did the Venona decrypts change the debate over the era?
  **A:** Released from 1995, they confirmed that some Americans, including Julius Rosenberg, really did spy for the Soviet Union; some historians argue this shows anticommunist fears had a real basis, while critics of McCarthyism reply that real espionage does not justify the false accusations and mass firings of people who were never spies.
- **Q:** Why was the Lavender Scare part of the same era?
  **A:** Anticommunists claimed that gay federal employees were morally weak and open to Soviet blackmail, so Eisenhower's 1953 Executive Order 10450 barred them from federal jobs, a ban not lifted in the civil service until 1975.

### [Montgomery bus boycott](../articles/cold-war-and-civil-rights/montgomery-bus-boycott.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Rosa Parks was a quiet seamstress who kept her seat only because she was tired after work, and the boycott arose spontaneously from her single act. | Parks was a longtime NAACP member and secretary of the local branch who had attended a 1955 activism workshop at the Highlander Folk School, and she later said the only tiredness she felt was being tired of giving in; Black women in Montgomery had pressed the city over bus treatment since 1954, Claudette Colvin and Mary Louise Smith had been arrested earlier in 1955, and the Women's Political Council had discussed a boycott for more than a year. |

**Why it matters:** The "tired seamstress" story turns an organized, community-wide campaign into an accident and hides the planning, risk and sacrifice of many people, especially Black women, that made it work.

- **Q:** Why did the boycott continue after the federal district court ruled against bus segregation in June 1956?
  **A:** The city appealed and kept enforcing segregation, so King and the MIA urged boycotters to stay off the buses until the ruling was actually implemented; that happened only after the Supreme Court affirmed it in November and its order reached Montgomery on December 20, 1956.
- **Q:** How did tens of thousands of people get to work for more than a year without the buses?
  **A:** Many walked, sometimes miles a day, and after the city penalized Black taxi drivers who offered cheap fares, the MIA organized a volunteer carpool of roughly 300 cars with pick-up stations, modeled on the 1953 Baton Rouge boycott and funded by collections at church mass meetings.
- **Q:** Why was Rosa Parks not a plaintiff in Browder v. Gayle?
  **A:** Her criminal case was still moving through the state courts, and Fred Gray wanted the federal court to face a single issue, the constitutionality of the bus segregation laws, so he filed on behalf of other women who had been mistreated on the buses.

### [Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944](../articles/cold-war-and-civil-rights/servicemens-readjustment-act-of-1944.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The GI Bill sent the returning World War II generation to college, and every veteran shared equally in its benefits. | Of about 8 million World War II veterans who used its education benefits, about 2.3 million attended colleges and universities, while millions more took school-based or on-the-job training; many also used its loans or unemployment pay. Benefits were administered through state agencies, local banks and segregated colleges, so Black veterans in the Jim Crow South, Native veterans on reservation land, and veterans given "blue" discharges, many of them gay, often received far less or nothing. |

**Why it matters:** Remembering the law only as universal free college overstates what it did for most veterans and hides how its design and administration widened some racial gaps in education and wealth even as it opened opportunity for millions.

- **Q:** Why did Congress let state agencies, banks and colleges administer much of the GI Bill, and what did that mean for Black veterans in the South?
  **A:** The law barred federal supervision of state educational agencies and schools and relied on private lenders for its guaranteed loans, a design Mississippi congressman John Rankin helped shape; in the Jim Crow South that let segregated colleges, local officials and banks limit Black veterans' benefits, as when Black veterans received two of 3,200 VA-backed loans in thirteen Mississippi cities.
- **Q:** How do Ira Katznelson and Suzanne Mettler differ in judging the GI Bill?
  **A:** Katznelson argues that its decentralized administration made it a form of "white affirmative action" that widened Black–white gaps, while Mettler, drawing on veterans' surveys and interviews, finds it a relatively inclusive program that expanded opportunity and made users more civically and politically active.
- **Q:** Why did planners in 1943 and 1944 want a large program for returning veterans?
  **A:** The Labor Department estimated that as many as 15 million service members could be unemployed after the war, and leaders wanted to avoid a repeat of the 1932 Bonus Army protests by World War I veterans.

## colonial-and-revolutionary

### [American Revolutionary War](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/american-revolutionary-war.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Revolutionary War ended when Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown in October 1781. | Britain still had about 26,000 troops in North America after Yorktown, fighting went on in the West and South into late 1782, the peace treaty was signed only on September 3, 1783, and the British did not leave New York City until November 25, 1783. |

**Why it matters:** Yorktown was the last great battle in the East and broke Britain's will to keep fighting, so later histories let the surrender stand for the whole victory and forgot the two years of frontier war, evacuation and diplomacy that followed.

- **Q:** Why did the American victory at Saratoga matter far beyond the battlefield?
  **A:** It was the first surrender of a British army to another nation, and the news persuaded France's foreign minister to offer a formal alliance; the treaties signed in February 1778 brought French money, troops and ships and turned the rebellion into a global war.
- **Q:** Why did most Native nations side with Britain, and how did the war turn out for them?
  **A:** Nations such as the Shawnee, Creek, Cherokee and most of the Six Nations hoped a British victory would hold back settlers west of the Appalachians; American victory opened those lands to expansion, and even the Oneida, who fought for the Americans, lost villages in the war and saw promises to protect their lands go unenforced.
- **Q:** How did the war open paths to freedom for enslaved people, and why were those paths limited?
  **A:** British offers of freedom such as Dunmore's 1775 proclamation led tens of thousands to flee their enslavers, and some Black men won freedom by serving in American units; but many who reached British lines were still held by loyalists, and the evacuations of 1782–1783 carried some to freedom in Nova Scotia and others onward in slavery.
- **Q:** Why did the war not end immediately after Yorktown?
  **A:** Britain still held New York, Charleston and other posts with about 26,000 troops, frontier warfare continued, and peace required a change of government in London, preliminary articles in November 1782 and a final treaty in September 1783.

### [Articles of Confederation](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/articles-of-confederation.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The government under the Articles of Confederation accomplished nothing and was simply a failure. | Despite having no power to tax, the Confederation Congress saw the Revolutionary War to its end with the 1783 Treaty of Paris, set up departments of Foreign Affairs, War, Marine and Treasury, and passed the Land Ordinances of 1784 and 1785 and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, which created a process for admitting new states on equal terms with the original thirteen. |

**Why it matters:** Because the Constitution replaced the Articles, the Confederation is often remembered only as the failed first draft, a view that overlooks lasting achievements and the real reasons people in the 1770s distrusted a strong central government.

- **Q:** Why did Congress under the Articles struggle to pay its bills, and what did that mean in practice?
  **A:** The Articles gave Congress no power to tax; it could only ask the state legislatures for money, and between 1781 and 1787 it received about $1.5 million of the $10 million it requested, leaving it unable to pay war debts or raise its own army, as Shays' Rebellion showed.
- **Q:** How did the dispute over western lands delay the Articles from taking effect?
  **A:** Ratification had to be unanimous, and states without western land claims, above all Maryland, refused to ratify until states such as Virginia gave up their claims; Maryland ratified only on March 1, 1781, after Virginia agreed to do so.
- **Q:** How did the Articles' clause on Indian affairs lead to conflict at Fort Stanwix in 1784?
  **A:** The Articles gave Congress power over affairs with Native peoples who were not members of any state, but New York claimed the Haudenosaunee as within its jurisdiction and held its own meeting, so Congress and the state competed for land cessions, and New York later sold land it had failed to obtain by treaty.
- **Q:** Why did the framers of the Articles deliberately create a weak central government?
  **A:** Many Americans feared losing power to a central body, as the earlier failure of the Albany Plan showed, so the Articles let each state keep its sovereignty and every power not expressly delegated, gave Congress no taxing power and no independent executive or courts, and required every state to agree to amendments.

### [Battle of the Virginia Capes](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/battle-of-the-capes.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The women of Havana gave up their diamonds and jewels to pay for the fleet that won at the Chesapeake and Yorktown. | Havana did supply the money, but as loans, not gifts of jewelry. Francisco de Saavedra and Spanish officials arranged it; about 500,000 pesos were pledged by Havana residents, promised 2 percent interest and repaid within six months, and reached de Grasse's fleet off Matanzas on August 18, 1781. |

**Why it matters:** The jewelry story traces to an anonymous pamphlet written after the war by someone who was not there, and it fits a romantic image; the real loan, arranged in a few hours by Spanish officials and merchants, is less colourful but shows that Spain's contribution to Yorktown, often overlooked, was concrete and organized.

- **Q:** If the Battle of the Virginia Capes was inconclusive as a fight, why is it counted among the war's decisive events?
  **A:** Because of what it decided strategically. Graves's damaged fleet withdrew to New York and the French, reinforced by de Barras, held the bay, so no British ships could reinforce or rescue Cornwallis at Yorktown, while Washington used the bay to move troops and supplies; Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, the same day the British relief fleet finally sailed.
- **Q:** Why did only part of the British fleet engage on September 5?
  **A:** Graves let the French come out and form a line instead of attacking them while they were disorganized, and then the wind and his unclear signals kept the rear of his line, under Hood, from closing with the enemy, so only the leading squadrons fought.
- **Q:** How did Spain help make the Chesapeake campaign possible even though it was not an ally of the United States?
  **A:** De Grasse could raise only a small sum at Cap-Français, so the Spanish official Francisco de Saavedra arranged loans in Havana; about 500,000 pesos were collected in a day and delivered to the fleet off Matanzas, and Spanish warships guarded Saint-Domingue so de Grasse could take his whole fleet north.
- **Q:** Why did Washington beg de Grasse not to leave the Chesapeake in late September 1781?
  **A:** He argued that with French command of the bay the capture of Cornwallis was nearly certain and would go far toward ending the war, while any absence would open a way to relieve Yorktown, starve the allied army of seaborne supplies and push peace further away.

### [Declaration of Independence](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/declaration-of-independence.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The delegates signed the Declaration of Independence on July 4, 1776. | Congress approved the text on July 4, but it ordered a parchment copy for signing only on July 19; most delegates signed on August 2, 1776, a few signed later that year, and Thomas McKean signed sometime after January 1777. |

**Why it matters:** Later recollections by Jefferson and Adams placed the signing on July 4, and the copies printed that week carried only the names of John Hancock and Charles Thomson, so the date of approval and the act of signing blurred together in popular memory.

- **Q:** Why did Congress cut Jefferson's passage blaming the king for the slave trade?
  **A:** Jefferson recorded that it was struck to accommodate South Carolina and Georgia, which wanted the slave trade to continue, and that some Northern delegates were uneasy because Northern merchants had carried enslaved people; the deletion kept those delegations behind independence.
- **Q:** How did declaring independence help the American war effort abroad?
  **A:** France would consider a formal alliance only once the colonies declared independence, so the Declaration opened the way to recognition and to the 1778 Treaty of Alliance with France, followed by Dutch recognition in 1782 and British acknowledgement in the 1783 Treaty of Paris.
- **Q:** Why is July 4 not the day the Declaration was signed?
  **A:** July 4 was the day Congress approved the text; the signing copy was only ordered on July 19, and most delegates signed it on August 2, 1776, with others signing later.
- **Q:** How did later reformers use the Declaration's language?
  **A:** The women who met at Seneca Falls in 1848 modelled their Declaration of Sentiments on it, declaring that all men and women are created equal, and Frederick Douglass in 1852 measured the nation's celebration of independence against the slavery it still allowed.

### [Jamestown Colony](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/jamestown.md) — City

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Jamestown was the first European settlement in what is now the United States. | Spain founded St. Augustine, Florida, in 1565, forty-two years before Jamestown, and it remains the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in the continental United States; Jamestown was the first permanent English settlement in North America. |

**Why it matters:** National memory has long traced the country's origins through the English colonies, which pushes the earlier Spanish presence in Florida and the Southwest, and the Native nations already there, out of the founding story.

- **Q:** Why did Jamestown's survival in its first years depend on the Powhatans?
  **A:** Disease, bad water, famine and war killed many colonists, and Powhatan sent food to the struggling fort; the National Park Service judges that without Powhatan help the settlement would most likely have failed, and when trade broke down in 1609 the Starving Time followed.
- **Q:** How did tobacco change Jamestown and Virginia?
  **A:** After John Rolfe introduced a new tobacco strain in 1612 it became the colony's cash crop, which drew more colonists, drove expansion onto Powhatan land that led to the 1622 and 1644 wars, and created the demand for labor that brought the first documented enslaved Africans in 1619.
- **Q:** Why is Jamestown not the first European settlement in the United States, and what is it the first of?
  **A:** Spain founded St. Augustine in 1565, forty-two years earlier; Jamestown was the first permanent English settlement in North America and the site of English America's first representative legislative assembly in 1619.

### [Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/lawes-divine-morall-and-martiall.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| "Dale's Laws" were written by Sir Thomas Dale and placed every Virginia colonist under martial law. | Thomas Gates issued the first orders in May 1610 and Lord De La Warr added more in June 1610; Dale enlarged them and added a military code in June 1611. Most of the printed text is that law martial for soldiers, and Brent Tarter argues Dale probably did not apply it to civilians, although the civil orders themselves carried death for many offenses. |

**Why it matters:** Dale governed longest under the code and enforced it most harshly, and Strachey's 1612 edition bound the civil orders and the soldiers' code together under one title.

- **Q:** Why did Thomas Gates issue strict orders as soon as he reached Jamestown in May 1610?
  **A:** He found only about sixty malnourished survivors of the Starving Time in a colony that had collapsed into disorder, so he imposed rules on work, food, livestock, trade and worship to restore discipline and keep the settlement alive.
- **Q:** How did the Lawes differ from the legal rights the Virginia Company's charters had promised colonists?
  **A:** The charters promised the rights of Englishmen, but the Lawes were company orders issued by military governors, not made by a legislature or applied by courts, and offenders were punished without English common-law procedures such as trial by jury.
- **Q:** Why did the Lawes forbid unauthorized trade with Native people and running away to live among them?
  **A:** During the war with the Powhatans, hungry colonists had fled to Native towns or traded on their own, and the company wanted to control the food trade and relations with Native nations, so it made unlicensed trade, robbing Native traders and desertion serious or capital crimes.
- **Q:** How did the Lawes come to an end?
  **A:** The company's reformed government replaced them; George Yeardley arrived as governor in April 1619 and the first General Assembly met that summer to make laws for a civilian government.

### [Second Continental Congress](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/second-continental-congress.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Second Continental Congress gathered in 1775 in order to declare independence. | When it met in May 1775 most delegates still hoped for reconciliation. In July 1775 Congress sent George III the Olive Branch Petition professing loyalty and adopted a declaration on taking up arms that denied any intention of separating from Britain. Support for independence grew only after the king refused the petition and declared the colonies in rebellion, and Congress voted for independence on July 2, 1776, fourteen months after it convened. |

**Why it matters:** The Declaration of Independence became Congress's best-remembered act, so its earlier year of petitions and professions of loyalty is easily forgotten.

- **Q:** Why did the Second Continental Congress spend more than a year fighting Britain before it declared independence?
  **A:** Most delegates in 1775 still hoped for reconciliation; Congress described its armed resistance as defence, denied any aim of separating, and sent the Olive Branch Petition to George III. Only after the king refused to receive it and declared the colonies in open rebellion did calls for independence grow, leading to the vote of July 2, 1776.
- **Q:** How could a body created only to coordinate colonial protest end up acting as a national government?
  **A:** Because war had already begun when it convened, Congress had to raise and command an army, issue bills of credit to pay for it, conduct diplomacy and negotiate with Native nations; in doing so it exceeded the authority the colonial governments had given it and governed de facto until the Articles of Confederation took effect in 1781.
- **Q:** Why was the question of how states would vote in Congress so contentious when the Articles of Confederation were debated in 1776?
  **A:** Congress had voted by colony, one vote each, and the Dickinson draft kept that rule; Benjamin Franklin argued that votes should follow population and contributions, but his amendment was defeated, so small and large states kept equal votes in the Confederation Congress.
- **Q:** What did Congress ask of the Six Nations in July 1775, and why?
  **A:** It asked the Haudenosaunee to stay at home and not join either side, calling the conflict a family quarrel with Britain, because in the war's first years Congress judged that keeping Native nations neutral cost less than seeking them as allies.

### [Starving Time of 1609–1610](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/starving-time.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Jamestown's colonists starved because they were lazy gentlemen who would not work. | Colony leaders such as John Smith, Ralph Hamor and William Strachey blamed idleness, but many historians now accept Karen Ordahl Kupperman's argument that malnutrition and disease produced an apathy that looked like laziness, while a severe drought and a Powhatan siege cut the fort off from food. |

**Why it matters:** The idleness story came from leaders and promoters who had their own reasons to blame the settlers rather than the Virginia Company or conditions in Virginia, and it was retold as a simple moral lesson about discipline and work.

- **Q:** Why did the Powhatan siege of 1609–1610 cause starvation rather than defeat in battle?
  **A:** Powhatan chose starvation over direct assault; his warriors killed the colony's hogs and anyone who left the fort to hunt, fish, trade or forage, so the colonists, already short of food in a drought, could not feed themselves until the siege lifted at spring planting.
- **Q:** How did the loss of the Sea Venture contribute to the Starving Time?
  **A:** The flagship carried much of the relief fleet's supplies and the colony's new leaders, so the ships that did arrive brought about 300 hungry colonists with little food, and Jamestown was left under weak leadership as winter came.
- **Q:** What evidence shows that survival cannibalism occurred at Jamestown, and why do some historians remain cautious?
  **A:** Accounts by George Percy and the 1624 Ancient Planters' report describe it, and in 2013 forensic study of a teenage girl's skull showed chop and knife marks consistent with survival cannibalism; but Rachel Herrmann notes that the written accounts had authors with motives, and no document describes that particular girl.
- **Q:** Why do historians now doubt that idleness explains the deaths?
  **A:** Karen Ordahl Kupperman argued that malnutrition and diseases such as scurvy and dysentery cause apathy and loss of appetite that contemporaries mistook for laziness, and tree-ring research shows the colony faced its worst drought in 770 years.

### [Treaty of Paris (1783)](../articles/colonial-and-revolutionary/treaty-of-paris-1783.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| In the Treaty of Paris, the Native nations east of the Mississippi gave up their lands to the United States. | The treaty was made between Britain and the United States alone, signed only by their commissioners, and contained no terms at all for Britain's Native allies; Britain signed away only its own claims, and each nation was left to make its own peace with the United States, which at Fort Stanwix in 1784 treated four of the Six Nations as defeated enemies. A Native confederacy north of the Ohio went on to defeat U.S. armies in 1790 and 1791. |

**Why it matters:** The treaty's boundary lines put the whole region between the Appalachians and the Mississippi inside the United States on paper, which makes it easy to read a European diplomatic agreement as if it settled who actually held and governed the land.

- **Q:** Why did the American commissioners sign preliminary terms with Britain in November 1782 that could not take effect at once?
  **A:** The United States was allied with France, so the preliminary articles were written to take effect only after Britain and France agreed their own peace terms; France and Britain signed their preliminaries on January 20, 1783, and the definitive treaty followed on September 3.
- **Q:** How did Article 7 of the treaty, which barred the British from carrying away enslaved people, end up being applied in New York?
  **A:** Sir Guy Carleton read it as not covering Black people who had already joined the British, so they could not be returned as property; about 3,000 in New York received passes recorded in the Book of Negroes, and upwards of 9,000 people of African descent left with the British from New York and other ports.
- **Q:** Why did the treaty leave the Haudenosaunee and other Native nations to negotiate separately with the United States?
  **A:** The treaty was between Britain and the United States only and contained no negotiated terms for Britain's Native allies, so each nation had to make its own peace, as the Six Nations did under pressure at Fort Stanwix in 1784.
- **Q:** How did disputes over the treaty's debt and western-post terms help make the case for a stronger national government?
  **A:** Virginia closed its courts to British creditors because Britain had broken the treaty, and British garrisons stayed at Detroit, Niagara and other posts inside U.S. borders; Federalists argued that only a Congress able to tax and raise an army could force Britain to leave.

## constitution-and-government

### [Constitutional Convention](../articles/constitution-and-government/constitutional-convention.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The delegates were sent to Philadelphia to write a new constitution. | Congress called the meeting only to revise the Articles of Confederation and report proposed alterations to Congress and the states; the delegates decided on their own, by mid-June 1787, to draft an entirely new frame of government, and their Constitution took effect once nine state conventions had ratified it. |

**Why it matters:** Because the convention's product replaced the Articles completely and is commemorated on September 17, it is easy to assume a new constitution was the assignment from the start, which hides how bold, and how contested, the delegates' decision was.

- **Q:** Why did the convention end up writing a whole new constitution when Congress had asked only for revisions?
  **A:** Many delegates believed the Articles left the central government too weak; Randolph's Virginia Plan on May 29 proposed a new national legislature, executive and judiciary, and by mid-June the delegates had accepted that they were drafting a new frame of government rather than amending the old one.
- **Q:** How did the Great Compromise settle the quarrel between large and small states?
  **A:** It gave the House of Representatives seats in proportion to each state's population and gave every state equal representation in the Senate; it passed on July 16, 1787, by a single state's margin.
- **Q:** How did the convention's decisions strengthen slaveholding states?
  **A:** Counting three-fifths of enslaved people added to slave states' seats in the House and, through the electoral college, their weight in presidential elections; the convention also barred Congress from ending the Atlantic slave trade before 1808 and added a fugitive slave clause.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about whether the Constitution was a proslavery document?
  **A:** Paul Finkelman argues its many protections for slavery made it a proslavery bargain, while Sean Wilentz argues that by refusing to recognise property in people the framers left an opening later antislavery politicians used; critics of Wilentz note that his key Madison quotation may have been added to Madison's notes later.

### [Federalism](../articles/constitution-and-government/federalism.md) — Principle

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Tenth Amendment limits the national government to the powers the Constitution "expressly" delegates to it. | The word "expressly" came from Article II of the Articles of Confederation; when the First Congress drafted the Tenth Amendment, the House voted 17 to 32 against adding it and the Senate also rejected it, so the amendment reserves only powers "not delegated." |

**Why it matters:** The omission leaves room for implied powers, the reading McCulloch v. Maryland adopted, so misquoting the amendment misstates how the Constitution actually divides power.

- **Q:** Why did Federalist No. 51 claim that federalism gives the American people a "double security" for their rights?
  **A:** Because the power the people surrendered was divided first between two distinct governments, national and state, and then among separate departments within each, so the governments would check each other while each was also checked internally.
- **Q:** How did the shift from dual federalism to cooperative federalism change the relationship between Washington and the states?
  **A:** Under dual federalism, which emerged in the late 1870s, each level kept to its own sphere and the Court blocked federal regulation such as that of manufacturing in E. C. Knight; from the Great Depression on, cooperative federalism blended the levels, with the national government taking on roles states had handled and funding grants that states administer, such as Medicaid.
- **Q:** Why do historians treat the antebellum "states' rights" argument as tied to slavery rather than as a neutral principle?
  **A:** South Carolina's 1860 secession declaration rested on northern hostility to slavery and complained that northern states had passed laws nullifying the federal fugitive slave acts, so it demanded strong federal enforcement for slaveholders while invoking state sovereignty for secession.
- **Q:** How does the position of federally recognized tribes differ from that of states within the federal system?
  **A:** The Constitution gives Congress plenary power over Indian affairs, so states have no authority over tribal governments unless Congress expressly grants it; tribes are generally not subordinate to states and deal with them government to government.

### [Judicial review](../articles/constitution-and-government/judicial-review.md) — Principle

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Marbury v. Madison invented judicial review, or the Constitution expressly gives the Supreme Court the power to strike down laws. | The Constitution does not expressly grant the power, but the idea was older than Marbury; state courts had voided statutes under state constitutions, Hamilton defended it in Federalist No. 78, and federal judges had weighed the constitutionality of acts of Congress in the 1790s. Marbury was the first time the Supreme Court actually held an act of Congress void. |

**Why it matters:** Treating Marbury as the origin hides both the founding-era debate that made the power widely expected and the real gap in the text that keeps its scope argued over today.

- **Q:** Why did Marbury v. Madison end with the Supreme Court refusing to order delivery of William Marbury's commission, even though it found he was entitled to it?
  **A:** Marshall held that the part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 letting the Court issue writs of mandamus in an original case added to the original jurisdiction set by Article III, so that provision was unconstitutional and void, leaving the Court without power to issue the writ.
- **Q:** How did Alexander Hamilton argue in Federalist No. 78 that judicial review would not make judges superior to Congress?
  **A:** He said the judiciary, with neither force nor will but only judgment, was the least dangerous branch, and that preferring the Constitution to a conflicting statute simply put the will of the people above the will of their agents, so it implied the people's power was superior to both.
- **Q:** How did the views of Jefferson and Lincoln differ from the position the Court took in Cooper v. Aaron?
  **A:** Jefferson called the people the ultimate arbiter and Lincoln said rulings bind the parties but must not irrevocably fix government policy, while in Cooper v. Aaron the Court said the federal judiciary is supreme in expounding the Constitution and that its reading in Brown bound every state official sworn to support the Constitution.

### [Presidency of the United States](../articles/constitution-and-government/presidency-of-the-united-states.md) — Office

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Constitution has always limited presidents to two terms. | The original Constitution set a four-year term with no limit on re-election. Two terms was a custom begun when George Washington retired after eight years; Franklin D. Roosevelt broke it by winning a third and a fourth term, and only the Twenty-second Amendment, ratified in 1951, made the limit law. |

**Why it matters:** Because every president since 1951 has been bound by the amendment, it is easy to forget that the limit is an amendment added after a century and a half of practice, not part of the framers' original design.

- **Q:** Why did the framers create a single president rather than an executive council?
  **A:** After a debate in which Edmund Randolph warned that a single executive could become a monarchy, the convention followed James Wilson's view that one person would be a better safeguard against tyranny; Hamilton later argued in Federalist No. 70 that unity gives the executive the energy good government needs.
- **Q:** How did the two-term limit go from custom to constitutional rule?
  **A:** Washington retired after two terms and later presidents followed his example until Franklin D. Roosevelt won a third and fourth term; Congress then proposed the Twenty-second Amendment in 1947, and its ratification in 1951 barred anyone from being elected president more than twice.
- **Q:** How do the president's powers depend on the other branches?
  **A:** The president alone commands the armed forces and grants pardons, but treaties and major appointments need the Senate's approval, Congress can override a veto by a two-thirds vote of both houses, and the House and Senate can remove a president through impeachment and conviction.
- **Q:** Why did the presidency's staff grow in the twentieth century?
  **A:** The Brownlow Committee reported in 1937 that the president's immediate staff was inadequate, and in 1939 Franklin D. Roosevelt created the Executive Office of the President to give the office its own advisers and agencies.

### [Supreme Court of the United States](../articles/constitution-and-government/supreme-court-of-the-united-states.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Constitution requires the Supreme Court to have nine justices. | Article III creates "one supreme Court" but sets no size; the Judiciary Act of 1789 gave it six justices, Congress changed the authorized number several times, and the figure of nine dates only from an 1869 statute. |

**Why it matters:** Because nine has stood for more than 150 years, an ordinary act of Congress is easily mistaken for a constitutional rule.

- **Q:** Why can Congress change the number of Supreme Court justices without amending the Constitution?
  **A:** Article III creates "one supreme Court" but says nothing about its size, so the number is set by statute; the Judiciary Act of 1789 set six, and later laws moved it between five (never in effect) and ten before an 1869 act fixed it at nine.
- **Q:** How did Marbury v. Madison expand the Court's role in the system of checks and balances?
  **A:** Chief Justice John Marshall held that part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 conflicted with the Constitution and that courts must follow the Constitution when a statute conflicts with it, the first time the Court declared an act of Congress unconstitutional and the basis of judicial review.
- **Q:** Why did Franklin D. Roosevelt's 1937 plan to enlarge the Court fail?
  **A:** Many saw it as a threat to judicial independence; a bipartisan Senate Judiciary Committee majority urged its emphatic rejection, the Court began upholding New Deal-era laws such as the minimum-wage law in West Coast Hotel v. Parrish, Justice Van Devanter retired, and the Senate defeated the plan on July 22, 1937.
- **Q:** How does most of the Court's work reach it, and how selective is it?
  **A:** Most cases arrive as petitions for a writ of certiorari asking the Court to review a lower-court decision; four justices must agree to take a case, and of up to about ten thousand requests a year the Court accepts fewer than 2 percent.

### [The Federalist Papers](../articles/constitution-and-government/federalist-papers.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Federalist Papers won over New York and carried the state into ratifying the Constitution. | Seventy-six essays had run in New York City newspapers by April 2, 1788, yet the state convention that met in June still had Antifederalists outnumbering Federalists by more than two to one. New York ratified by only 30 to 27, and only after news arrived that New Hampshire had become the ninth state to ratify and Virginia the tenth, which left New York facing a union that would go ahead without it. |

**Why it matters:** The essays' later fame as the classic commentary on the Constitution is read back into 1788, when contemporaries, including some Federalists, found them too long and learned for many readers and when the decisive pressures on New York were the other states' votes.

- **Q:** Why did Madison argue in Federalist No. 10 that a large republic would control faction better than a small one?
  **A:** Because the causes of faction are rooted in human nature and cannot be removed without destroying liberty, the effects must be controlled; a large republic takes in more parties and interests, so a majority is less likely to share a motive to oppress others and harder to organize if it does.
- **Q:** How did the timing of New York's ratification complicate claims that the essays decided the outcome?
  **A:** The convention met with Antifederalists outnumbering Federalists by more than two to one even after most essays had run, and it ratified narrowly, 30 to 27, only after New Hampshire and Virginia had ratified and the new government was going ahead.
- **Q:** Why do some legal scholars question relying on The Federalist to interpret the Constitution?
  **A:** Critics doubt that background texts should settle constitutional meaning at all, and they note that the essays were partisan newspaper advocacy written to win a ratification fight rather than neutral explanations.
- **Q:** How did Federalist No. 54 treat enslaved people in its defence of the apportionment rule?
  **A:** Speaking through an imagined Southern voice, it argued that the law treated enslaved people as both property and persons and accepted counting them as partial persons, three fifths, for representation and direct taxes.

### [United States Congress](../articles/constitution-and-government/united-states-congress.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Constitution fixes the House of Representatives at 435 members. | The Constitution set only the House's first size (65 members) and a minimum of one representative per state; Congress set the number by law, enlarging the House after most censuses, fixing it at 433 plus two in 1911 and capping it at 435 from 1929, apart from a temporary 437 after Alaska and Hawaii joined. |

**Why it matters:** Because 435 has not changed for about a century, it is easy to mistake an ordinary statute that Congress could amend for a constitutional rule.

- **Q:** Why does Congress have two chambers with different bases of representation?
  **A:** The framers created a bicameral legislature in July 1787 in which House seats are apportioned among the states by population while every state has two senators, balancing the interests of populous and small states.
- **Q:** How did the Seventeenth Amendment change the Senate, and why was it adopted?
  **A:** It replaced election of senators by state legislatures with election by the people; legislative selection had produced long deadlocks (45 in 20 states between 1891 and 1905), vacant seats and bribery cases, and many states had already moved to popular choice through primaries before the amendment was ratified in 1913.
- **Q:** How did the original Constitution's formula for apportioning House seats involve slavery?
  **A:** Article I counted the whole number of free persons, excluded "Indians not taxed," and added three-fifths of "all other Persons," meaning enslaved people, who could not vote, still added to the representation of the states that held them.
- **Q:** Why was the First Congress unable to start work on March 4, 1789?
  **A:** Too few members had arrived in New York; only 8 of 22 senators and 13 of 59 representatives were present, so neither chamber had a quorum until April 1 (House) and April 6 (Senate).

### [United States Constitution](../articles/constitution-and-government/united-states-constitution.md) — Document

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Constitution declares that "all men are created equal." | That phrase comes from the Declaration of Independence. The original Constitution contained no such promise and instead counted three-fifths of enslaved people for apportionment; an equality guarantee entered the text only with the Fourteenth Amendment after the Civil War. |

**Why it matters:** Running the two founding documents together hides how the 1787 text accommodated slavery and how much the Reconstruction amendments changed it.

- **Q:** How does the Constitution try to keep any one part of the government from becoming too powerful?
  **A:** It separates legislative, executive and judicial power among Congress, the president and the courts, gives each checks on the others such as the veto, veto override, Senate confirmation, impeachment and judicial review, and divides power between the national government and the states.
- **Q:** Why did several states ratify only after being promised amendments, and what came of that promise?
  **A:** Anti-Federalists feared a strong central government could threaten individual liberties and demanded a bill of rights; the First Congress proposed twelve amendments in September 1789, and ten were ratified on December 15, 1791, as the Bill of Rights.
- **Q:** How did the original Constitution protect slavery without using the word "slave"?
  **A:** It used euphemisms such as other persons and persons held to service or labor; it counted three-fifths of enslaved people for representation and taxes, barred Congress from banning their importation before 1808, and required the return of people who escaped slavery into another state.
- **Q:** How did Charles Beard's interpretation of the Constitution differ from that of later ideological historians?
  **A:** Beard argued in 1913 that the framers and ratifiers acted mainly to protect their economic interests, while historians such as Bernard Bailyn and Gordon Wood argued that political ideas, especially republicanism, better explain the framing; later scholarship has largely undermined Beard's account.

### [United States House of Representatives](../articles/constitution-and-government/house-of-representatives.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Constitution requires the Speaker of the House to be a member of the House. | Article I says only that the House "shall chuse their Speaker and other Officers"; it sets no membership requirement, although every Speaker so far has been a sitting representative. |

**Why it matters:** Because the House has always picked one of its own, a long custom is easily mistaken for a written constitutional rule.

- **Q:** Why did the framers make House members stand for election every two years?
  **A:** Short terms and direct election "by the People of the several States" were meant to keep the House the chamber closest to voters, which is why it is often called the People's House.
- **Q:** How does the House choose a president when no candidate wins an Electoral College majority, and when has this happened?
  **A:** Under the Twelfth Amendment the House picks from the top three candidates, with each state delegation casting one vote; it chose Thomas Jefferson in 1801 on the 36th ballot (under the original rules) and John Quincy Adams on February 9, 1825.
- **Q:** Why was the 1910 revolt against Speaker Joseph Cannon a turning point for the House?
  **A:** Cannon controlled the Rules Committee and all committee appointments; the Norris resolution, passed 191 to 156 with progressive Republicans joining Democrats, removed him from the Rules Committee, the House later took back the power to appoint committees, and no later Speaker regained such centralized control.
- **Q:** What did Joseph Rainey's and Jeannette Rankin's arrivals show about who could serve in the House?
  **A:** Rainey, born enslaved, took his seat in December 1870 during Reconstruction as the first Black representative, and Rankin was sworn in in April 1917 as the first woman elected to Congress, four years before the Nineteenth Amendment; both widened a body that had excluded Black Americans and women.

## contemporary-america

### [Affordable Care Act](../articles/contemporary-america/affordable-care-act.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Congress repealed the Affordable Care Act's individual mandate in 2017, and the Supreme Court later struck the law down. | The 2017 tax law reduced the penalty for going without coverage to $0 from 2019 but left the coverage requirement in the statute, and in California v. Texas (2021) the Supreme Court dismissed the challenge for lack of standing, leaving the rest of the law in force. |

**Why it matters:** Zeroing out the penalty was widely described as killing the mandate, and a lower court had ruled the whole law invalid before the Supreme Court reversed, so headlines from different stages of the fight blur together.

- **Q:** Why did the Supreme Court uphold the individual mandate in NFIB v. Sebelius even though it rejected the Commerce Clause argument for it?
  **A:** The Court held that the penalty for going without insurance could be sustained as an exercise of Congress's power to levy taxes, so the mandate was constitutional on that basis even though it was not valid under the Commerce Clause or the Necessary and Proper Clause.
- **Q:** How did NFIB v. Sebelius change the Medicaid expansion?
  **A:** The Court found that threatening states with the loss of their existing Medicaid funds was coercive, and limited the remedy so that states could decline the expansion to adults with incomes up to 133 percent of the poverty level without losing their other Medicaid money, making the expansion optional.
- **Q:** Why did California v. Texas end without a ruling on whether the law was constitutional?
  **A:** Because Congress had set the penalty for going without coverage at $0, the Court found the plaintiffs could not show an injury traceable to the government enforcing that provision, so they lacked standing and the case was dismissed.

### [COVID-19 pandemic in the United States](../articles/contemporary-america/covid-19-pandemic-in-the-united-states.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The 1918 influenza pandemic killed more Americans than any other pandemic, including COVID-19. | CDC estimates about 675,000 U.S. deaths in the 1918 pandemic, while recorded U.S. COVID-19 deaths reached 1 million by May 12, 2022, so in raw numbers COVID-19 was the deadlier outbreak in American history. |

**Why it matters:** The 1918 flu has long been remembered as the benchmark for a catastrophic pandemic, and the comparison is easy to miss because the two tolls were counted a century apart in a much smaller and a much larger country.

- **Q:** Why did the federal government declare emergencies in early 2020, and what did that make possible?
  **A:** After the first confirmed case on January 20, HHS declared a public health emergency on January 31 and the administration declared a nationwide emergency on March 13, which gave federal agencies emergency powers that later supported free testing, Medicaid and Medicare flexibilities, and emergency use authorizations for vaccines.
- **Q:** How did the pandemic affect the U.S. labor market in spring 2020?
  **A:** Efforts to contain the virus shut down much of the economy, so in April 2020 payroll employment fell by 20.5 million and unemployment reached 14.7 percent, the highest rate and largest one-month rise in the series dating to 1948, with leisure and hospitality hit hardest.
- **Q:** Why did U.S. life expectancy fall in both 2020 and 2021, and which group lost the most?
  **A:** COVID-19 deaths accounted for about three-quarters of the 2020 decline and half of the 2021 decline; American Indian and Alaska Native people lost the most, 6.6 years between 2019 and 2021.
- **Q:** What changed, and what did not, when the public health emergency ended on May 11, 2023?
  **A:** Requirements that Medicare and private insurers cover COVID-19 tests without cost sharing ended and some reporting authority lapsed, but vaccines stayed free, treatments stayed available, and existing emergency use authorizations were unaffected.

### [Deepwater Horizon oil spill](../articles/contemporary-america/deepwater-horizon-oil-spill.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| By August 2010 most of the spilled oil was gone, because the government's "oil budget" showed three-quarters of it had disappeared. | The oil budget was a rough operational tool for responders whose only directly measured category was oil recovered at the wellhead; it ignored biodegradation, and oil it classed as dispersed, dissolved or evaporated was not necessarily gone. The commission's staff found that the "75 percent gone" framing and the claim that the budget was peer-reviewed were inaccurate and produced misleading coverage. |

**Why it matters:** Officials announced the figures as good news after months of rising flow estimates, and headlines turned uncertain categories into a single reassuring number; much oil in fact sank to the seafloor.

- **Q:** Why did the presidential commission conclude the blowout was not simply an accident?
  **A:** It found the loss of the well could have been prevented, tracing it to identifiable mistakes by BP, Halliburton and Transocean that reflected systematic failures in risk management, and to a culture of complacency in industry and government, including a regulator whose leasing-revenue role conflicted with its safety role.
- **Q:** How did early underestimates of the flow rate affect the response?
  **A:** Officials first cited 1,000 and then 5,000 barrels a day, while later government estimates put the flow near 62,000 barrels a day at the outset; the commission found the understated estimates impeded planning to stop the oil at its source.
- **Q:** How did money from the BP settlements reach Gulf communities and ecosystems?
  **A:** Most of the $5.5 billion Clean Water Act penalty (80 percent) was directed to Gulf restoration under the RESTORE Act, $8.1 billion or more went to natural resource restoration, and the five Gulf states and hundreds of local governments received separate payments for economic losses; Louisiana planned to put $7 billion toward its Coastal Master Plan.

### [Department of Homeland Security](../articles/contemporary-america/department-of-homeland-security.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Department of Homeland Security took over the FBI and the CIA when it was created. | The FBI and CIA stayed outside the new department; the 2002 act transferred only the FBI's National Infrastructure Protection Center and allowed FBI and CIA analysts to be detailed to DHS, and President Bush considered folding in the FBI but decided against it. |

**Why it matters:** The department was sold as the answer to the intelligence-sharing failures before 9/11, so many people assume it absorbed the main intelligence agencies; the later Director of National Intelligence and counterterrorism center were separate reforms.

- **Q:** Why did the Bush administration move from a White House office to a full Cabinet department for homeland security?
  **A:** The Office of Homeland Security created in October 2001 coordinated agencies but many observers doubted it had the authority to overcome bureaucratic obstacles; with Congress already drafting a department bill, Bush reversed course in June 2002 and proposed a permanent department that would combine the agencies directly.
- **Q:** How did the creation of DHS change the federal government's handling of immigration?
  **A:** The act abolished the Justice Department's Immigration and Naturalization Service and split its work among three DHS agencies — Customs and Border Protection, Immigration and Customs Enforcement and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services — separating enforcement from the processing of visas and naturalization.
- **Q:** Why did Congress reorganize FEMA in 2006?
  **A:** The response to Hurricane Katrina in August 2005 exposed gaps in federal emergency management, so the Post-Katrina Emergency Management Reform Act gave FEMA substantial new authority and a stronger preparedness mission within the department.

### [Great Recession](../articles/contemporary-america/great-recession.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| When the recession officially ended in June 2009, the hard times were over. | The end date marks only the low point of activity. Unemployment kept rising until October 2009, when it peaked at 10 percent, and the NBER said its dating did not mean conditions afterward were favorable. |

**Why it matters:** Confusing a recession's official end with recovery hides why many families felt the downturn for years, through slow job growth, high long-term unemployment and lost home equity.

- **Q:** How did falling house prices turn into a nationwide recession?
  **A:** Riskier mortgages had been pooled into securities held across the financial system, so when prices fell from 2006–07 mortgage losses spread to banks and funding markets, leading to the failures and rescues of 2008; at the same time falling home values and stock prices cut household wealth sharply, and employment fell 5 percent.
- **Q:** Why did the Great Recession widen the wealth gap between white households and Black and Hispanic households?
  **A:** Home equity made up a larger share of Black and Hispanic wealth, and many Hispanic families lived in states where house prices fell most, so the housing collapse cut their median wealth by 53 and 66 percent from 2005 to 2009, compared with 16 percent for white households (Pew, 2011).
- **Q:** How did the majority of the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission and its dissenters disagree about the causes of the crisis?
  **A:** The majority blamed failures of regulation, corporate governance and risk management and called the crisis avoidable; Hennessey, Holtz-Eakin and Thomas stressed a global credit and housing bubble with several interacting causes; Wallison blamed federal housing policy and the affordable-housing goals of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.

### [Obergefell v. Hodges](../articles/contemporary-america/obergefell-v-hodges.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Same-sex marriage first became legal anywhere in the United States with the Obergefell decision in 2015. | Same-sex couples had been marrying in Massachusetts since May 17, 2004; by June 2015 legislators or voters in eleven states and the District of Columbia had legalized it, and federal and state courts had struck down bans in many more, so Obergefell extended marriage to the remaining states that still barred it. |

**Why it matters:** Remembering the decision as the starting point hides a decade of state court cases, legislative votes and ballot campaigns on both sides, and it overlooks that the Court's question was whether the states that still refused could keep doing so.

- **Q:** Why did the Court rely on both the Due Process Clause and the Equal Protection Clause rather than only one of them?
  **A:** The majority treated the right to marry as a fundamental liberty protected by due process and held that excluding same-sex couples from it also denied them equal treatment, reasoning that the two clauses are connected and each can inform the meaning of the other.
- **Q:** How did the dissenting justices' main objection differ from an argument about marriage policy itself?
  **A:** Chief Justice Roberts and the other dissenters argued chiefly about who should decide; they said the Constitution leaves the definition of marriage to voters and legislatures, so the Court had wrongly ended a democratic debate, whatever one thinks of same-sex marriage as policy.
- **Q:** Why did Congress pass the Respect for Marriage Act in 2022 even though Obergefell was still in force, and what does that law not do?
  **A:** The act replaced the Defense of Marriage Act's federal definition with one recognizing marriages valid where performed and barred states from denying recognition to other states' marriages because of the couple's sex or race; it does not require any state to issue marriage licenses itself, and it protects nonprofit religious organizations from having to provide services for weddings.

### [September 11 attacks](../articles/contemporary-america/september-11-attacks.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Twin Towers were brought down by a planned controlled demolition using explosives or thermite rather than by the hijacked planes. | The federal engineering investigation by NIST found that the impacts severed columns and stripped fireproofing, and that multi-floor fires weakened floors that pulled the perimeter columns inward until they failed; it found no corroborating evidence for explosives, and seismic signals came from falling debris after each collapse began. |

**Why it matters:** The towers' sudden, total collapse was unprecedented and hard to grasp, and demolition stories replace a documented chain of failures and warnings with a hidden plot that the engineering and investigative record does not support.

- **Q:** Why did the 9/11 Commission call imagination the most important failure before the attacks?
  **A:** Officials had warnings about al Qaeda and earlier attacks such as the 1998 embassy bombings and the 2000 USS Cole attack, but no one treated the use of hijacked airliners as weapons as a real threat; the Commission found that U.S. measures between 1998 and 2001 never disturbed or delayed the plot.
- **Q:** How did the actions of the people aboard United 93 change the outcome of the attacks?
  **A:** After learning by phone of the other hijackings, the passengers and crew voted to fight back and forced the plane down in a Pennsylvania field about 20 minutes' flying time from Washington, so it never reached its probable target, the U.S. Capitol or the White House.
- **Q:** How did the attacks reshape the federal government at home?
  **A:** Within weeks the president created a White House Office of Homeland Security, Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act and created the TSA in 2001, and the Homeland Security Act of 2002 merged 22 agencies into a new Cabinet department; the 9/11 Commission's 2004 report then led to proposals such as a National Counterterrorism Center and a national intelligence director.

## culture-and-arts

### [Blues](../articles/culture-and-arts/blues.md) — Tradition

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| W. C. Handy, the "Father of the Blues," invented the blues. | Handy was a trained bandleader who encountered the blues already being played by Black musicians in the Mississippi Delta around 1903 — a slide guitarist at the Tutwiler station and a string band in Cleveland, Mississippi — and only then worked it into his repertoire. He wrote down and combined fragments of melodies and lyrics he heard from itinerant performers, arranged them as published songs, and made the music a national craze; the "Father of the Blues" title, which he used for his 1941 autobiography, honored him as its popularizer. |

**Why it matters:** Published sheet music, copyrights and an autobiography left a paper trail with Handy's name on it, while the unnamed rural musicians who created the music left almost no written record.

- **Q:** Why is it misleading to say that W. C. Handy invented the blues?
  **A:** Handy himself described hearing the blues from Black musicians in the Delta around 1903, before he played it; he wrote down and arranged fragments he heard from itinerant performers and published them, which made him the music's great popularizer rather than its creator.
- **Q:** How did the blues change when Mississippi musicians moved to Chicago?
  **A:** In the city they electrically amplified their instruments and added drums and horns, so the solo singer with a guitar became the blues band heard on labels such as Chess.
- **Q:** Why did Mamie Smith's 1920 recording of "Crazy Blues" matter to the music business?
  **A:** It was the first commercial vaudeville blues record by a Black singer, and in the following years "race records" — recordings by Black artists marketed mainly to Black buyers — grew into a business that sent scouts across the South and into northern cities to find blues talent.

### [Chinese detention-barracks poetry](../articles/culture-and-arts/chinese-detention-barracks-poetry.md) — Work

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The poems lay forgotten until a park ranger stumbled on them by chance in 1970, and they are the only writing on the walls. | Officials knew of the poems and had them repeatedly puttied and painted over, and two detainees copied nearly 200 of them in 1931–32; the 1970 find was a rediscovery. The walls also hold inscriptions in Japanese, Korean, Russian and other languages. |

**Why it matters:** The 1970 find launched the campaign that saved the barracks, so it became the story's starting point, and the Chinese poems' fame has overshadowed the other inscriptions.

- **Q:** Why did detainees at Angel Island write poems on the barracks walls, and why are most of them unsigned?
  **A:** Chinese men held for weeks, months or sometimes years while officials enforced the exclusion laws used the walls to voice homesickness, anger and despair; most did not sign or date their work, likely for fear of punishment by the authorities.
- **Q:** How did the efforts of officials to erase the poems end up helping preserve them?
  **A:** After brushed poems were painted over, detainees carved the characters into the wood; crews filled the carvings with putty and paint, which sealed the wood, and as the putty shrank and the paint cracked the poems reappeared.
- **Q:** Why are no poems by women known, and how were some damaged poems recovered?
  **A:** Women were held in the administration building, which burned in 1940, so any writing there was lost; poems copied by detainees Smiley Jann in 1931 and Tet Yee in 1932 were given to researchers in 1976 and helped them restore damaged or missing poems.
- **Q:** How do scholars differ in reading the poems' form?
  **A:** Him Mark Lai described them as largely in the classical Tang style, while R. John Williams argues their irregular rhythms and loose constructions sit closer to modern free verse than to the classics.

### [Hip hop](../articles/culture-and-arts/hip-hop.md) — Tradition

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Sugarhill Gang's "Rapper's Delight" (1979) was the first rap record, and hip hop began when it was released. | The Fatback Band's "King Tim III (Personality Jock)" was released about two months earlier in 1979, and DJs, MCs and dancers had been building hip hop at Bronx parties since the early-to-mid 1970s; "Rapper's Delight" was the first hip hop song to reach the Billboard Hot 100. |

**Why it matters:** Treating the first hit record as the beginning erases the years of unrecorded neighborhood creativity — the DJs, MCs, b-boys, b-girls and graffiti writers — that made the culture before the record industry noticed it.

- **Q:** Why did DJ Kool Herc's way of playing records matter so much to early hip hop?
  **A:** By playing two copies of the same record on two turntables and switching between them, he extended the "break," the drum-heavy passage dancers loved, which gave b-boys and b-girls room to dance and MCs a beat to rhyme over.
- **Q:** How did conditions in the Bronx in the late 1960s and 1970s shape hip hop?
  **A:** Economic decline brought cuts to schools, job training and the arts, rising unemployment and abandoned buildings; young Black and Latino people, some of them former gang members, turned to DJing, dancing and rhyming at parties as do-it-yourself expression and an alternative to gang conflict.
- **Q:** Why is it misleading to say hip hop began with "Rapper's Delight"?
  **A:** Hip hop had been developing at Bronx parties since 1973, and another rap record, "King Tim III," came out about two months earlier; "Rapper's Delight" was the first to reach the Hot 100, not the start of the culture.

### [Jazz](../articles/culture-and-arts/jazz.md) — Tradition

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Jazz was born in the brothels of Storyville, and its musicians scattered north when the district was closed in 1917. | Jazz grew out of New Orleans neighborhood life — brass bands, parades, dance halls, social clubs and street music — and Storyville was only one of many places musicians worked. Research by Tulane's Hogan Jazz Archive curator Bruce Raeburn on musicians who left the city between 1902 and 1922 found that they went for better pay, working conditions, racial equality and safety, not because the district closed; several had left years before 1917. |

**Why it matters:** The red-light story is colorful and easy to tell, but it hides the African American and Creole communities, institutions and traditions that actually produced the music, and the segregation and police harassment that drove many musicians away.

- **Q:** How did the segregation laws of the 1890s help shape early jazz in New Orleans?
  **A:** They stripped Creoles of color of their separate social status and pushed them together with uptown Black musicians, combining the Creoles' formal training and discipline with the improvised, blues-based playing of uptown bands.
- **Q:** Why is the story that jazz musicians left New Orleans because Storyville closed misleading?
  **A:** Many musicians, such as Jelly Roll Morton, the Original Creole Orchestra and Tom Brown's band, had left before the district closed in November 1917, and research on musicians who left between 1902 and 1922 shows they sought better pay, working conditions, racial equality and safety; King Oliver, for example, had been repeatedly arrested while playing.
- **Q:** How did bebop change the role of jazz compared with the swing era?
  **A:** Swing big bands played largely for dancing, while bebop, developed in Harlem after-hours sessions in the early 1940s, used small groups, fast tempos and improvised solos over chord changes, and was meant mainly for listening.

### [Thanksgiving](../articles/culture-and-arts/thanksgiving.md) — Tradition

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Americans have celebrated Thanksgiving every year since the Pilgrims and their friendly Indian neighbors held the "first Thanksgiving" in 1621. | The 1621 event was a three-day harvest gathering that also reinforced a new diplomatic alliance between the Wampanoag and the English; neither surviving English account calls it a thanksgiving. The national holiday descends from later official proclamations, above all Lincoln's in 1863, and the familiar story of a friendly shared meal took hold only in the mid-1800s. |

**Why it matters:** The storybook version leaves out the Wampanoag's own reasons for the alliance, the epidemics that had devastated their communities, and the colonization and violence that followed — which is why many Native people mark the day as one of mourning.

- **Q:** Why did the Wampanoag ally with the Plymouth colonists in 1621, according to the Smithsonian's account?
  **A:** Epidemics between 1616 and 1619 had killed up to 90 percent of New England's Native population, and the weakened Wampanoag also needed help against the rival Narragansett, so they agreed to a mutual-defense treaty; the alliance was driven by diplomacy more than friendship.
- **Q:** How did Thanksgiving become a fixed annual national holiday rather than an occasional proclamation?
  **A:** Sarah Josepha Hale campaigned for decades for a national day; Lincoln's 1863 proclamation set the last Thursday of November, and presidents kept proclaiming the day until Roosevelt's 1939 change caused confusion, after which Congress in 1941 fixed it by law on the fourth Thursday of November.
- **Q:** Why did Congress choose the "fourth" rather than the "last" Thursday of November in 1941?
  **A:** The Senate amended the House resolution so that the date would stay consistent in years when November has five Thursdays, the problem that had prompted the 1939 change.

### [Winged Figures of the Republic](../articles/culture-and-arts/winged-figures-of-the-republic.md) — Work

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Thomas Jefferson coined the phrase "eternal vigilance is the price of liberty," which Hansen invoked to explain his statues. | Monticello's research staff have found no evidence that Jefferson said or wrote it; the idea traces to John Philpot Curran, and the modern wording circulated in American newspapers without his name, first credited to Jefferson in print in 1834. |

**Why it matters:** Patriotic maxims tend to migrate to the most famous founder, and a phrase carved into civic memory, as at Hoover Dam, rarely carries its real source with it.

- **Q:** How does the star map let a future viewer work out when Hoover Dam was dedicated?
  **A:** Hansen laid out the positions of celestial bodies as they stood at the dam's dedication on September 30, 1935, and set them within a Circle of Precession showing the slow wobble of Earth's axis known as the Platonic Year, so the sky's arrangement fixes the moment even without a written date.
- **Q:** Why do some catalogues date the figures to 1935 when the plaza was finished in 1938?
  **A:** The Smithsonian's sculpture inventory ties the work to the dam's 1935 dedication, which the star map commemorates, but Reclamation photographs show Hansen still overseeing fabrication of the star map in April 1938, and the plaza was completed that year.
- **Q:** How did the builders set the heavy bronze figures on their polished bases without scratching them?
  **A:** The diorite bases were placed on blocks of ice and guided into position as the ice melted, avoiding scars to the polished stone and floor.
- **Q:** Why did Hansen describe the figures in terms of vigilance, and what is wrong with crediting that idea to Jefferson?
  **A:** Hansen said the figures stood for the eternal vigilance that is the price of liberty, casting them as sentries of the Republic; Monticello finds no evidence Jefferson ever said this, tracing it instead to John Philpot Curran.

## democracy-and-elections

### [Census and apportionment](../articles/democracy-and-elections/census-and-apportionment.md) — Principle

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Census Bureau decides how many House seats each state gets and draws the new congressional districts. | The Bureau counts the population and applies a formula that Congress wrote into law (equal proportions, adopted in 1941); the states then redraw their own district lines using census redistricting data. |

**Why it matters:** Because seat gains and losses are announced by the Census Bureau, the count, the formula and the separate state-run redistricting process are easily blurred together.

- **Q:** Why did the original apportionment formula give slaveholding states extra representation, and how did that change?
  **A:** Article I counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for apportionment though they could not vote, adding seats for slaveholding states; after emancipation the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) counted the whole number of persons, which would have fully counted freed people and so increased Southern power, which is why it added a never-enforced penalty for denying men the vote.
- **Q:** Why did Washington veto the first apportionment bill in 1792?
  **A:** He judged it unconstitutional because no single ratio produced its allotment and it gave eight states more than one representative per 30,000 people, the Constitution's limit.
- **Q:** How did the failure to reapportion after the 1920 census change the process?
  **A:** Rural members, fearing a loss of power to fast-growing cities, blocked reapportionment for a decade; the 1929 act responded by making apportionment automatic after each census with the House kept at 435 seats.
- **Q:** What is the difference between apportionment and redistricting?
  **A:** Apportionment divides the 435 House seats among the states by population; redistricting is each state's redrawing of district boundaries within its borders, using small-area counts the Census Bureau supplies.

### [Electoral College](../articles/democracy-and-elections/electoral-college.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Constitution creates a body called the "Electoral College" whose members gather in one place to elect the president. | The phrase "electoral college" does not appear in the Constitution, and the electors never meet as one body; each state's electors meet in their own state in December, and Congress counts their certified votes in January. |

**Why it matters:** The name suggests a single deliberative assembly, which hides how much of the process is run by the states and how little room individual electors now have.

- **Q:** Why did the framers choose electors rather than election by Congress or by a national popular vote?
  **A:** It was a compromise; selection by Congress risked making the president dependent on the legislature, while most delegates distrusted direct popular election, and Madison noted that a popular vote would disadvantage Southern states whose enslaved people could not vote.
- **Q:** How did the election of 1800 lead to the Twelfth Amendment?
  **A:** Each elector cast two undifferentiated votes, so Jefferson and his running mate Burr tied at 73 and the House needed 36 ballots to choose; the amendment, ratified in 1804, gave electors separate votes for president and vice president.
- **Q:** How can a candidate win the national popular vote and still lose the presidency?
  **A:** The president is chosen by state-allotted electors, and 48 states and the District of Columbia give all their electors to the statewide winner, so narrow wins in some states can outweigh large margins elsewhere, as in 1876, 1888, 2000 and 2016.
- **Q:** What did the Supreme Court decide in Chiafalo v. Washington, and why?
  **A:** It held unanimously in judgment in 2020 that states may enforce electors' pledges, reasoning from long practice that electors are not free agents but vote for the candidate their state's voters chose.

### [Fifteenth Amendment](../articles/democracy-and-elections/fifteenth-amendment.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Fifteenth Amendment gave all Black Americans the right to vote in 1870. | The amendment only forbade denying the vote because of race, color or previous servitude; it conferred no right of suffrage on anyone, did not cover sex, so Black women were still excluded, and left states free to impose poll taxes, literacy tests and other qualifications that Southern states used to disfranchise most Black men until federal enforcement under the Voting Rights Act of 1965. |

**Why it matters:** Treating 1870 as the moment Black Americans won the vote hides nearly a century of disfranchisement and explains poorly why the civil rights movement still had to fight for the ballot in the 1960s.

- **Q:** Why could Southern states keep most Black men from voting for decades even though the Fifteenth Amendment was in force?
  **A:** The amendment only barred denial of the vote on account of race, color or previous servitude and left states free to set other qualifications, so states used poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses and discriminatory administration, while 1876 Supreme Court rulings such as United States v. Reese weakened federal enforcement.
- **Q:** How did the amendment divide the women's rights movement?
  **A:** Because it protected against racial but not sex discrimination, Lucy Stone and Frederick Douglass supported it as a first step while Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony opposed any amendment that excluded women; the American Equal Rights Association broke up in 1869 and two rival suffrage organizations formed.
- **Q:** How is the Voting Rights Act of 1965 connected to the Fifteenth Amendment?
  **A:** The Act used the enforcement power in the amendment's Section 2; President Johnson asked Congress for a law that would make it impossible to thwart the amendment, and the Act abolished remaining barriers and authorized federal supervision of registration.

### [Reynolds v. Sims](../articles/democracy-and-elections/reynolds-v-sims.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Reynolds v. Sims coined the phrase "one person, one vote." | The phrase entered the Supreme Court's case law a year earlier, in Gray v. Sanders (1963), which struck down Georgia's county-unit system for statewide elections; Reynolds applied that equality standard to the seats of both houses of state legislatures. |

**Why it matters:** Because Reynolds reshaped every state legislature, it became the case most associated with the slogan, which blurs the sequence by which the Court built the doctrine from Baker to Gray, Wesberry and Reynolds.

- **Q:** How had rural Alabamians come to control the state legislature by the early 1960s?
  **A:** Although the 1901 constitution required reapportionment after each census, the legislature had not reapportioned since the 1900 census while nearly all of the state's growth came in urban areas; by the 1960 census about a quarter of the population lived in districts that could elect a majority of each house.
- **Q:** Why did the Court reject the argument that a state senate could be modeled on the U.S. Senate?
  **A:** It held that the federal arrangement grew out of a unique historical compromise among sovereign states, whereas counties and other political subdivisions are subordinate instruments of the state, so the federal analogy did not apply to state legislatures.
- **Q:** How did Reynolds build on Baker v. Carr?
  **A:** Baker (1962) held that apportionment challenges could be heard by federal courts; Reynolds supplied the substantive standard, requiring both houses to be apportioned so that districts were as nearly equal in population as practicable.
- **Q:** How did opponents of the ruling try to reverse it, and why did they fail?
  **A:** They proposed a constitutional amendment allowing one house of a state legislature to be apportioned using geography and political subdivisions as well as population; it won a 57 to 39 majority in the Senate on August 4, 1965, but fell short of the two-thirds a constitutional amendment requires.

### [Shelby County v. Holder](../articles/democracy-and-elections/shelby-county-v-holder.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| In Shelby County v. Holder the Supreme Court struck down the Voting Rights Act. | The Court invalidated only the Section 4(b) coverage formula. It issued no holding on Section 5, which remains in the statute, and said the nationwide ban on racial discrimination in Section 2 was unaffected; preclearance stopped operating because no jurisdiction is covered unless Congress writes a new formula or a court orders coverage under Section 3. |

**Why it matters:** Because preclearance stopped working overnight, the ruling is often summarized as ending the Act, which hides both the remaining protections and the choice the Court left to Congress.

- **Q:** Why did preclearance stop operating even though the Court did not strike down Section 5?
  **A:** Section 5 applies only to jurisdictions identified by the Section 4(b) coverage formula; once the Court held that formula unconstitutional, no jurisdiction was covered, so the preclearance requirement had nothing to attach to unless Congress enacts a new formula or a court orders coverage under Section 3.
- **Q:** How did the majority and the dissent differ in judging the 2006 reauthorization?
  **A:** Chief Justice Roberts held that the formula rested on data from the 1960s and early 1970s and could not justify treating some states differently under the principle of equal sovereignty, pointing to near-equal Black and white registration by 2004; Justice Ginsburg argued that Congress's large record showed continuing discrimination and that preclearance itself explained the progress.
- **Q:** Why did the U.S. Commission on Civil Rights and its critics disagree about the decision's effects?
  **A:** The Commission found that new voting restrictions followed in formerly covered places and that case-by-case Section 2 lawsuits were slow and costly, while witnesses such as Hans von Spakovsky argued that Black registration and turnout data and Section 3's court-ordered preclearance made a new Section 5 unnecessary.

### [Voting Rights Act of 1965](../articles/democracy-and-elections/voting-rights-act-of-1965.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Black Americans' right to vote rests on the Voting Rights Act and will expire unless Congress renews the Act. | The bans on racial discrimination in voting in the Fifteenth Amendment and in the Act's core provisions, such as Section 2, are permanent; only the "special provisions" for certain jurisdictions, such as Section 5 preclearance and federal examiners and observers, were written with expiration dates and periodically renewed. |

**Why it matters:** The rumor, which the Justice Department publicly called false, confuses temporary remedies with the underlying constitutional right, and it can distract from the real debates over how the Act's remedies should work.

- **Q:** Why did Congress single out certain states and counties for preclearance instead of relying only on lawsuits?
  **A:** Case-by-case litigation was slow and easily evaded, so Section 4 used a formula (a voting test or device in 1964 plus registration or turnout under 50 percent) to identify places with a record of disenfranchisement, and Section 5 required them to get federal approval before changing voting rules; the Supreme Court said this shifted the "advantage of time and inertia" from those discriminating to their victims.
- **Q:** How did the Selma campaign help produce the Act?
  **A:** Violence against peaceful marchers, especially on Bloody Sunday in March 1965, drew national outrage; Johnson sent voting rights proposals to Congress within days and later credited the events at Selma with prompting federal action, though he had already been considering reform after the 1964 election.
- **Q:** How did Shelby County v. Holder change the Act without striking down Section 5 itself?
  **A:** The Court held that the Section 4(b) coverage formula relied on decades-old data, so no jurisdiction could be required to seek preclearance until Congress wrote a new formula, which it has not done.
- **Q:** What did the 1982 amendment to Section 2 change, and why?
  **A:** After Mobile v. Bolden (1980) required proof of discriminatory purpose in vote-dilution cases, Congress amended Section 2 so a violation could be shown without proving intent.

## depression-and-world-war

### [1943 Detroit race riot](../articles/depression-and-world-war/detroit-race-riot-of-1943.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The 1943 riot was a Black uprising stirred up by "agitators" such as the NAACP and the Black press. | The violence grew from a fight on Belle Isle and rumors that inflamed both Black and white crowds; white mobs beat Black people while police looked on, police killed 17 Black people and no white people, and about 85 percent of those arrested were Black. Contemporary analysts traced the riot to segregated, overcrowded housing, hate strikes against Black promotions and discriminatory policing. |

**Why it matters:** Detroit's prosecutor privately blamed the NAACP and the Black weekly Michigan Chronicle, and the governor's committee report put the onus on Black residents while sparing local authorities, so the official story shaped early memory of the event.

- **Q:** How did wartime housing and jobs set the stage for the 1943 riot?
  **A:** War work drew hundreds of thousands of migrants to Detroit, but Black residents were confined to crowded districts such as Paradise Valley and Black Bottom; white mobs fought Black families moving into the Sojourner Truth Homes in 1942, and white workers staged hate strikes against Black promotions, including a walkout of some 25,000 at Packard in June 1943.
- **Q:** Why did Black Detroiters see the official response to the riot as unjust?
  **A:** Police killed 17 Black people and no white people, about 85 percent of those arrested were Black, police had watched white mobs beat Black men, and the governor's committee and the county prosecutor blamed Black residents and the NAACP rather than the conditions and police conduct behind the violence.
- **Q:** How did federal troops come to be used, and why did it take so long?
  **A:** Black leaders pressed Mayor Jeffries to seek troops; Governor Kelly asked the President, who under the Constitution's domestic-violence guarantee first had to issue Proclamation 2588 ordering rioters to disperse, and the roughly 13-hour delay meant most of the killing and burning had already happened.

### [Attack on Pearl Harbor](../articles/depression-and-world-war/attack-on-pearl-harbor.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| President Roosevelt knew in advance that Japan would strike Pearl Harbor and let it happen to bring the United States into the war. | U.S. codebreakers were reading Japan's diplomatic messages (MAGIC), which tracked Tokyo's policy but did not reveal military plans; those travelled in Japanese Navy codes that largely resisted American cryptologists. Eight wartime and postwar investigations divided blame among the Hawaiʻi commanders and officials in Washington, but none established foreknowledge. |

**Why it matters:** Responsibility was never settled to everyone's satisfaction, and for decades revisionist accounts defending the local commanders claimed that intelligence had been suppressed, keeping the story alive.

- **Q:** Why did Japan decide to attack the U.S. fleet at Pearl Harbor?
  **A:** After Japan moved into French Indochina in 1941, the United States and Britain cut off its oil and Washington froze its assets. Japan's main aim was the oil of the Dutch East Indies, which meant fighting Britain and the United States; planners struck the Pacific Fleet to keep it from interfering while Japan seized Southeast Asia, hoping for a quick victory because they doubted they could win a long war.
- **Q:** How did the attack bring the United States into the war in Europe as well as the Pacific?
  **A:** Congress declared war only on Japan on December 8, 1941, but Japan's ally Germany then declared war on the United States on December 11, putting the country at war in both Europe and Asia.
- **Q:** Why did the attack fall short of crippling the United States in the long run?
  **A:** All three Pacific Fleet aircraft carriers were at sea, fuel storage and repair facilities survived, and many sunk or damaged ships were raised, repaired and returned to service; six months later U.S. carriers sank four Japanese carriers at Midway.
- **Q:** How did the attack affect Japanese Americans?
  **A:** The FBI immediately arrested people on prewar watch lists, and on February 19, 1942, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, which led to the mass removal and incarceration of Japanese Americans on the West Coast, about two-thirds of them U.S. citizens by birth.

### [Dust Bowl](../articles/depression-and-world-war/dust-bowl.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The "Okies" who poured into California in the 1930s were mostly farm families blown out of the Dust Bowl. | Dust Bowl refugees were only part of a migration of roughly half a million people from several Southern Plains states; many came from Arkansas, Missouri, eastern Oklahoma and East Texas, where tenant farmers were pushed off the land by falling prices, foreclosures and landlords using federal crop payments to mechanize and evict them. |

**Why it matters:** John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath and Depression-era photographs fused the dust storms and the westward migration into one story, and Californians labelled all the newcomers "Okies".

- **Q:** Why did drought in the 1930s produce such severe dust storms on the Southern Plains?
  **A:** Between about 1909 and 1929 farmers had broken some 32 million acres of native sod, much of it for wheat; when drought killed the crops from 1931, bare plowed fields lost the grass roots that had held the soil, and high winds lifted it away.
- **Q:** How did the Dust Bowl change federal policy?
  **A:** Dust clouds reaching Washington in 1934 and 1935 helped Hugh Hammond Bennett persuade Congress to pass the Soil Conservation Act of 1935, which created the permanent Soil Conservation Service; it promoted terracing, strip cropping and grass seeding and led to locally run soil conservation districts.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about the causes of the Dust Bowl?
  **A:** Donald Worster argued that a market-driven plow-up of marginal grassland made it a human-made ecological disaster, while later researchers using county data and climate models stress that dust storms are natural in long Plains droughts, that the heaviest wheat areas were not always the worst hit, and that ocean temperatures drove the drought.
- **Q:** Were most migrants who went to California from the Dust Bowl itself?
  **A:** No. Of roughly half a million Southern Plains migrants, many were tenant farmers from Arkansas, Missouri, eastern Oklahoma and East Texas displaced by economic forces rather than drought.

### [Great Depression](../articles/depression-and-world-war/great-depression.md) — Era

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The stock market crash of October 1929 caused the Great Depression. | The downturn had already begun in August 1929, and production, prices and incomes were falling before the crash; the crash was one of a series of financial shocks, and the waves of bank failures and the shrinking money supply of 1930–33 did most to turn a recession into a depression. |

**Why it matters:** Black Tuesday is a single, dramatic date, while bank panics, tight monetary policy and the gold standard worked over years and are harder to picture.

- **Q:** Why do most economists argue the 1929 crash alone does not explain the Great Depression?
  **A:** The economy was already contracting from August 1929, and the deepest damage came later, when regional and national banking panics from 1930 to 1933 wiped out deposits, the money supply fell by nearly 30 percent, and the Federal Reserve failed to act as a lender of last resort.
- **Q:** How did the gold standard help spread and deepen the Depression?
  **A:** Because gold linked interest rates and prices across countries, U.S. tightening forced other gold-standard countries to deflate too; countries that left gold early, like Britain, began recovering sooner, while those that stayed on longest, like France, suffered longer.
- **Q:** How did Hoover's response differ from Roosevelt's?
  **A:** Hoover relied mainly on voluntary pledges from business and labor and private relief, adding the Reconstruction Finance Corporation's loans to banks and railroads in 1932; Roosevelt's New Deal used direct federal relief, jobs programs such as the WPA, banking reform and Social Security.
- **Q:** Why did the Depression and the New Deal affect Black, Mexican American and Native communities differently from others?
  **A:** Black workers faced unemployment near 50 percent by 1932 and were often left out of New Deal programs, such as Social Security's exclusion of domestic and farm workers; people of Mexican ancestry, many of them U.S. citizens, were pushed or forced to leave for Mexico; and the 1934 Indian Reorganization Act reversed allotment and encouraged tribal self-government.

### [Japanese American incarceration during World War II](../articles/depression-and-world-war/japanese-american-incarceration.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Japanese Americans were removed and imprisoned because they were caught spying or committing sabotage, so the camps were a military necessity. | No Japanese American was convicted of serious espionage or sabotage during the war; the rumours cited by the Army were unverified, and Congress later found the policy was driven by racial prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership. |

**Why it matters:** The government justified the removal as military necessity at the time, the Supreme Court accepted that claim in 1944, and the word "evacuation" made forced removal sound like protection.

- **Q:** Why do the National Park Service and many historians prefer "incarceration" and "forced removal" to "internment" and "evacuation"?
  **A:** Internment is a law-of-war term for detaining enemy nationals, which the Justice Department and Army did separately to non-citizens; most people in the ten WRA camps were U.S. citizens held without charges, and "evacuation" wrongly suggests being moved out of danger.
- **Q:** How did the government justify the removal, and what does the evidence show?
  **A:** General DeWitt and President Roosevelt claimed military necessity, citing reports of signals and sabotage; those reports were unverified, the FBI's director mocked the Army's intelligence as hysterical, and no Japanese American was convicted of serious espionage or sabotage.
- **Q:** How did the Supreme Court's two December 1944 decisions differ?
  **A:** In Korematsu it upheld the exclusion order 6–3 as a military necessity, while in Ex parte Endo it ruled that the government could not keep detaining citizens it conceded were loyal; the exclusion orders were lifted effective January 2, 1945.
- **Q:** What did the redress movement achieve, and how?
  **A:** Japanese American organizations pressed Congress, which created a commission in 1980; its findings and recommendations led to the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, which apologized, paid $20,000 to each surviving incarcerated person (82,219 people in all) and funded public education.

### [New Deal](../articles/depression-and-world-war/new-deal.md) — Policy

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The New Deal ended the Great Depression. | The New Deal brought relief and growth after 1933, but unemployment was still above 19 percent in 1939 and around 15 percent in 1940; full recovery came with wartime mobilization for World War II. |

**Why it matters:** The New Deal's most visible achievements, such as Social Security and public works, outlasted the Depression, so the program is often remembered as the cure rather than as a partial recovery cut short by the 1937 recession.

- **Q:** Why did the Supreme Court's 1935–36 rulings threaten the New Deal, and how did that change after 1937?
  **A:** In Schechter (1935) the Court struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act for delegating too much power and reaching intrastate commerce, and in Butler (1936) it struck down the Agricultural Adjustment Act's tax; after Roosevelt's failed 1937 plan to enlarge the Court, the justices began upholding laws such as the Wagner Act and Social Security and did not strike down another major New Deal law.
- **Q:** How could a farm program meant to help farmers end up hurting many of the poorest people in agriculture?
  **A:** The AAA paid landowners to take land out of production, so its payments mostly went to large farmers rather than to the Black and white tenant farmers, sharecroppers and farm workers who lost work when acreage was cut.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about why farm and domestic workers were left out of Social Security in 1935?
  **A:** Some scholars argue Southern Democrats engineered the exclusion to keep Black workers out, since 65 percent of Black workers fell in excluded jobs; SSA historian Larry DeWitt argues the record shows it came from Treasury concerns about collecting payroll taxes from scattered workers, and notes most of those excluded were white and coverage was extended in 1950 and 1954.
- **Q:** Why do economists such as Price Fishback argue New Deal spending was too small to end the Depression on its own?
  **A:** Federal outlays by 1939 were only about 8 percent of 1929 GDP above their 1929 level, tax increases kept deficits small, and public works and relief raised incomes by roughly a dollar per dollar spent while adding little private employment.

### [World War II and the United States](../articles/depression-and-world-war/world-war-ii-and-the-united-states.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The United States stayed strictly neutral in World War II until the attack on Pearl Harbor. | The country was neutral in law but not in practice. From 1939 it sold arms to Britain and France on a cash-and-carry basis, in September 1940 it traded destroyers to Britain for bases, and from March 1941 the Lend-Lease Act let it supply Britain and other nations without immediate payment. |

**Why it matters:** Pearl Harbor was such a sudden shock, and isolationist sentiment before it so loud, that the long, deliberate drift toward aiding the Allies is easily forgotten.

- **Q:** How was the United States involved in the war before Pearl Harbor even though it was officially neutral?
  **A:** It sold war materiel to Britain on a cash-and-carry basis from 1939, traded more than 50 destroyers to Britain for base leases in 1940, and under the March 1941 Lend-Lease Act supplied Britain and eventually more than 30 countries with about $50 billion in aid without demanding immediate payment.
- **Q:** Why did the United States end up fighting Germany as well as Japan after Pearl Harbor?
  **A:** Congress declared war only on Japan on December 8, 1941. On December 11 Germany and Italy declared war on the United States, and Congress then passed resolutions recognizing a state of war with both.
- **Q:** Why did African American newspapers promote a "Double V" campaign during the war?
  **A:** About a million Black Americans served, mostly in segregated units led by white officers, while discrimination persisted at home; the Pittsburgh Courier's campaign urged victory over fascism abroad and over racism at home.
- **Q:** How did the war change the American economy and workforce?
  **A:** Federal spending soared, unemployment virtually disappeared as some 20 million workers entered the military, women took over a million administrative jobs and became a majority of aircraft-industry workers by 1943, and the bracero program brought Mexican laborers to fill farm jobs.

## early-republic-and-expansion

### [California Gold Rush](../articles/early-republic-and-expansion/california-gold-rush.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The forty-niners struck it rich; the Gold Rush made ordinary miners wealthy. | A few miners found fortunes, but most did not; many made modest profits at best, while others went bankrupt or died, and the surest fortunes went to merchants and speculators such as Samuel Brannan and Levi Strauss who sold goods to miners. Even the discoverer, James Marshall, was at one point stranded penniless. |

**Why it matters:** Stories of lucky strikes travelled faster than accounts of hardship, and the "forty-niner" became a romantic symbol of quick wealth in the West.

- **Q:** Why did the Gold Rush bring California into the Union so quickly, and why was its admission controversial?
  **A:** The rush raised California's non-Native population from about 14,000 in 1848 to more than 200,000 by 1852, enough to write a constitution in 1849 and seek statehood; because California would be a free state and part of it lay south of the Missouri Compromise line, its admission as a free state reopened the fight over slavery's expansion and was settled only as part of the Compromise of 1850.
- **Q:** How did the Gold Rush affect Native Californians?
  **A:** Miners took their land, water and food sources, mining ruined salmon runs, epidemics spread, and the 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians let Native people, including children, be held as indentured laborers; the Native population fell from about 150,000 in the 1840s to under 30,000 by 1870, and in 2019 the state formally apologized for this violence and dispossession.
- **Q:** Who profited most from the Gold Rush, and why?
  **A:** Merchants and suppliers such as Samuel Brannan and Levi Strauss, because thousands of miners in remote camps had to buy tools, food and clothing at inflated prices, while most miners found little gold and some went bankrupt.
- **Q:** How did California's foreign miners' taxes work, and whom did they target?
  **A:** A 1850 law charged non-citizen miners $20 a month, aimed mainly at Latin American miners and used by white miners to drive them from rich claims; after its repeal a $4 monthly tax followed, and an 1852 law aimed at Chinese miners, who paid more than $5 million, about a quarter of state revenue, until it was voided in 1870.

### [Louisiana Purchase](../articles/early-republic-and-expansion/louisiana-purchase.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| In 1803 the United States bought the land of the Louisiana Territory from France for a few cents an acre. | France sold what it had, a European claim to sovereignty that gave the United States the exclusive right to acquire land from the Native nations who held and lived on it; the United States then acquired that land over the following decades through hundreds of treaties, agreements and seizures, eventually paying Native nations about $2.6 billion in disbursements by one historian's count. |

**Why it matters:** The famous price makes the purchase look like a single real-estate bargain and erases the Native nations whose lands were the real subject of later negotiation, pressure and war.

- **Q:** Why did Jefferson's envoys end up buying far more land than they were sent to get?
  **A:** They were authorized to pay up to $10 million for New Orleans and the Floridas, but Napoleon, whose army had been devastated in Saint-Domingue and who faced renewed war with Britain, offered all of Louisiana, so Livingston and Monroe exceeded their instructions and agreed to $15 million for the whole territory.
- **Q:** How did the purchase test Jefferson's own view of the Constitution, and how did he resolve it?
  **A:** Jefferson believed the Constitution made no provision for acquiring foreign territory and drafted an amendment, but his Cabinet argued it was unnecessary and Napoleon's deadline loomed, so he let the Senate ratify the treaty without one, comparing himself to a guardian investing a ward's money in adjacent land.
- **Q:** According to Robert Lee, what did the United States actually acquire from France, and why does that change the meaning of the $15 million price?
  **A:** It acquired France's claim of preemption — the exclusive right to obtain land from Native nations — rather than the land itself, so the real cost of the territory was paid later through many treaties and cessions backed by force, adding up to far more than $15 million.

### [Mexican–American War](../articles/early-republic-and-expansion/mexican-american-war.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Abraham Lincoln opposed the Mexican War so strongly that he refused to vote money and supplies for the soldiers fighting it. | Lincoln voted for a House amendment declaring the war unnecessarily and unconstitutionally begun, but he wrote in February 1848 that he had always intended to vote supplies for the army. |

**Why it matters:** Merging his attack on Polk's justification with a refusal to support the troops erases a distinction Lincoln drew deliberately in explaining his votes.

- **Q:** Why did the land between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande matter so much in starting the war?
  **A:** The United States, inheriting Texas's claim, said the border was the Rio Grande, while Mexico and many Americans held that the strip had never been part of Texas. When Polk sent Taylor's army into it and Mexican troops attacked a U.S. patrol there, Polk could tell Congress that American blood had been shed on American soil, while Mexico saw an invasion.
- **Q:** How did victory over Mexico make the conflict over slavery worse rather than settling it?
  **A:** The huge Mexican Cession reopened the question of whether slavery would spread into new territories. The Wilmot Proviso to ban slavery there passed the House but failed in the Senate, deepening sectional division that the Compromise of 1850 only temporarily eased and that fed into the Civil War.
- **Q:** What did Lincoln's 'Spot' Resolutions challenge, and did he refuse to support the army?
  **A:** They demanded that Polk identify the exact spot where American blood was first shed and show it was U.S. soil; the House never acted on them. Lincoln also voted that the war had been unconstitutionally begun, but he said he always intended to vote supplies for the troops.
- **Q:** How, according to historian Brian DeLay, did Native raids shape the U.S. invasion of northern Mexico?
  **A:** Some fifteen years of raiding and counter-raiding across ten northern Mexican states had killed thousands and depopulated the countryside, strengthening U.S. arguments for taking the territory and leaving northern Mexicans too divided and exhausted to resist the American invasion effectively.

### [Missouri Compromise](../articles/early-republic-and-expansion/missouri-compromise.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Henry Clay drew up the Missouri Compromise and proposed its famous 36°30′ line. | Senator Jesse B. Thomas of Illinois proposed the 36°30′ line; Speaker Henry Clay, working with President Monroe and Senate leaders, steered the package through the House and later brokered the 1821 second compromise over Missouri's constitution. |

**Why it matters:** The Missouri fight launched Clay's lasting reputation as "the Great Compromiser," so later memory folded the work of Thomas, Monroe and others into his name.

- **Q:** Why did Congress link Maine's statehood to Missouri's?
  **A:** Admitting Missouri as a slave state would have tipped the evenly divided Senate toward the slave states, so Senate leaders would advance Maine (a free state carved from Massachusetts) only if Missouri entered without the Tallmadge restriction; admitting both kept the balance at twelve and twelve.
- **Q:** How did the 36°30′ line work, and how was it undone?
  **A:** It barred slavery in the remaining Louisiana Purchase territory north of Missouri's southern border while leaving Missouri itself open to slavery; the Kansas-Nebraska Act repealed it in 1854, and in 1857 the Supreme Court's Dred Scott decision declared the compromise unconstitutional.
- **Q:** Why did a second compromise become necessary in 1821?
  **A:** Missouri's 1820 constitution directed the legislature to keep free Black people from entering the state, which some members of Congress said violated the rights of free Black people recognized as citizens in northern states; Clay's resolution admitted Missouri only after its legislature pledged in a public act that the disputed clauses would not be enforced.
- **Q:** Why did Jefferson call the settlement "a reprieve only"?
  **A:** He believed that a geographic line coinciding with a moral and political principle would never be erased and would deepen with each new dispute, threatening the Union.

### [Monroe Doctrine](../articles/early-republic-and-expansion/monroe-doctrine.md) — Policy

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Monroe Doctrine was a law or treaty that the United States enforced with its own military power from 1823 onward. | It was a few paragraphs in a presidential message to Congress, not a statute or treaty. European powers paid it little attention, most governments doubted the United States could enforce it, and in practice Britain's Royal Navy kept other powers out; only in the late nineteenth century did U.S. power make enforcement realistic. |

**Why it matters:** Later presidents from Polk to Theodore Roosevelt and Kennedy invoked the doctrine so forcefully that its twentieth-century weight is easily projected back onto 1823.

- **Q:** Why did John Quincy Adams oppose issuing the declaration jointly with Britain?
  **A:** He argued a joint statement would make the United States look like it was following British policy and could limit future U.S. expansion, and he doubted Britain's commitment to the new Latin American republics, so the proposal became a unilateral U.S. declaration.
- **Q:** How could a weak United States make such a sweeping claim in 1823?
  **A:** Britain favored the policy, so the United States could in effect rely on the Royal Navy, and London secured a French promise not to help Spain recover its colonies.
- **Q:** How did the Roosevelt Corollary change the doctrine's meaning?
  **A:** The 1823 message warned Europeans against intervening in the Americas; Roosevelt's 1904 corollary claimed a right for the United States itself to intervene, leading to Marine deployments in Santo Domingo, Nicaragua and Haiti.

### [War of 1812](../articles/early-republic-and-expansion/war-of-1812.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Battle of New Orleans was fought after the War of 1812 was already over, so it was pointless. | The Treaty of Ghent was signed on December 24, 1814, but hostilities were to stop only after both governments ratified it, and the United States did not do so until February 1815; the war was still legally on when the battle was fought on January 8, 1815. |

**Why it matters:** Treating the signing date as the end of the war makes the war's best-remembered American victory look like an accident of slow mail and hides how ratification actually worked.

- **Q:** Why did the United States declare war on Britain in 1812, and why do historians argue about it?
  **A:** Madison and Congress cited British impressment of American sailors and British limits on neutral trade, alongside British support for Native resistance on the frontier. Most historians treat these maritime grievances as the core cause, but some have argued that the real aim was to seize Canada, while others stress national honor and British hostility to the young republic.
- **Q:** How could the war end in a treaty that changed no borders and yet still have large consequences?
  **A:** The Treaty of Ghent restored prewar territory and did not mention impressment, but the war shattered Tecumseh's confederacy and the Creek Red Sticks, cost the Creeks millions of acres at Fort Jackson, left Native nations without British support, made Andrew Jackson a national hero and helped finish off the Federalist Party.
- **Q:** What choices did the war present to enslaved African Americans?
  **A:** Some Black men joined U.S. forces hoping to earn freedom, while about 4,000 enslaved people in the Chesapeake escaped to British ships after Cochrane's 1814 proclamation; about 200 served as Colonial Marines, and Britain later resettled most in Canada's Maritime provinces or Trinidad.
- **Q:** Was the Battle of New Orleans fought after the war ended?
  **A:** No. It came after the treaty was signed but before the United States ratified it in February 1815, and the treaty required fighting to continue until ratification.

## economy-and-work

### [Great Railroad Strike of 1877](../articles/economy-and-work/great-railroad-strike-of-1877.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The 1877 strike was a planned national uprising, organized and directed by labor unions or radical leaders. | The walkout began on the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad and spread largely on its own along the rail lines; almost none of the strikers belonged to a union and no leaders or organization controlled the strike as a whole, although local groups such as a railroad firemen's organization in West Virginia and socialists in Chicago and St. Louis played real roles in particular places. |

**Why it matters:** The strike's sudden national reach and the visible part socialists played in a few cities made it look coordinated, and treating it as a conspiracy obscures the wage cuts and hard times that drove ordinary workers and their neighbours into the streets.

- **Q:** Why did a wage cut on one railroad turn into a crisis that stretched from Baltimore to St. Louis?
  **A:** Railroad workers had already suffered years of depression and repeated wage cuts since 1873, so when the Baltimore and Ohio announced another ten percent cut, the walkout at Martinsburg spread along the connected trunk lines, drew in unemployed people and workers in other trades, and local police and militias could not or would not stop it.
- **Q:** How did the federal government become involved, and what limits did President Hayes set on that role?
  **A:** Governors who said they could not keep order formally asked for federal help, and Hayes issued proclamations and sent Army troops; he refused the Pennsylvania Railroad's request to act directly for the companies and did not use soldiers to run trains, describing his aim as restoring order, though the deployment set a precedent for later presidents.
- **Q:** What lessons did workers and employers each draw from the strike?
  **A:** Many workers concluded they needed lasting, organized unions, while businesses concluded they needed more political influence and government help, including better-prepared state forces to deal with future labor unrest.

### [Labor movement](../articles/economy-and-work/labor-movement.md) — Movement

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| May Day, the international workers' holiday on May 1, is a foreign or Soviet tradition with no American roots. | May Day grew out of an American campaign; unions chose May 1, 1886, for a national strike for the eight-hour day, and an international socialist congress in 1889 adopted the date for worldwide demonstrations beginning in 1890. |

**Why it matters:** Because the Haymarket bombing tied May 1 to anarchists and later Cold War images tied it to Moscow, more conservative American unions dropped it for a September Labor Day, and its U.S. origin was largely forgotten.

- **Q:** How did the Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor differ in whom they organized?
  **A:** The Knights aimed at "One Big Union" open to nearly all wage workers, including women, African Americans and immigrants of every skill level, while the AFL federated autonomous craft unions of skilled workers, concentrated on wages and conditions, and left most factory workers unorganized.
- **Q:** Why did union membership grow so fast in the 1930s?
  **A:** The Wagner Act of 1935 guaranteed workers the right to form unions, required employers to bargain with a majority union and created the NLRB to enforce it, while the CIO organized whole mass-production industries and won breakthroughs such as the 1936–37 Flint sit-down strike against General Motors.
- **Q:** What explanations do scholars give for the decline of union membership after the mid-twentieth century?
  **A:** Some stress structural change, such as international competition, unemployment and industry moving to the nonunion South; others stress employer opposition, including a sharp rise in unfair labor practice violations in the 1970s, and weak legal protection under the Wagner Act as narrowed by Taft-Hartley.

### [Lattimer massacre](../articles/economy-and-work/lattimer-massacre.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The "Lattimer riots" were a violent uprising by armed foreign miners that deputies had to put down. | The marchers had agreed not to carry firearms or clubs, the state's historical marker and a National Park Service study describe them as unarmed, and over a dozen marchers were shot in the back while fleeing; exactly how the first shots came is still debated. |

**Why it matters:** Early newspaper accounts came largely from the sheriff and coal-company sympathizers, the trial records have been lost, and nativist hostility toward Slavic immigrants made the "riot" label easy to accept, so some newspapers still used it in the 1930s.

- **Q:** Why were the Lattimer marchers on strike, and why were immigrant miners especially vulnerable in 1897?
  **A:** They were protesting low wages, high prices for supplies the company sold them and abuses by company-town staff. As recent Polish, Slovak and Lithuanian immigrants, none of them citizens, they faced nativist hostility, a new state tax on employers of non-citizens, and a sheriff's posse drawn from Anglo-American townspeople and company agents.
- **Q:** How did a massacre that ended in acquittals end up strengthening the miners' union?
  **A:** The killings united the region's immigrant communities and disproved the idea that Slavic and Italian workers were docile; within four months more than 15,000 anthracite workers joined the United Mine Workers, which under John Mitchell led the 1900 and 1902 strikes that brought presidential intervention.
- **Q:** Why did the Lattimer massacre fade from national memory, and how was it revived?
  **A:** Scholars point to the victims' ethnicity, nativist attitudes, the site's rural remoteness and coal operators' interest in forgetting. Interest revived in the civil rights era, leading to a 1972 memorial dedicated with Cesar Chavez, centennial markers in 1997 and an archaeological survey in 2010.

### [Minimum wage](../articles/economy-and-work/minimum-wage.md) — Policy

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The minimum wage began with Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal and the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938. | The states came first. Massachusetts passed the first minimum wage law in 1912, and by the end of 1938 twenty-five states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico had minimum wage legislation. The 1938 federal law built on that state experiment, and at first it covered industries employing only about a fifth of the labor force. |

**Why it matters:** The federal act was the first national floor and its 25-cent rate is a famous milestone, so the decades of earlier state laws, most of them limited to women and minors, drop out of the popular story.

- **Q:** Why were the first American minimum wage laws limited to women and minors?
  **A:** Organizing women into unions was seen as difficult, so legislated standards were thought justified for them, while the AFL under Samuel Gompers opposed legal wage floors for men and wanted men's pay won through collective bargaining.
- **Q:** How did the Supreme Court's 1937 decision in West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish clear the way for a federal minimum wage?
  **A:** It upheld Washington's minimum wage law and overruled Adkins v. Children's Hospital, rejecting the idea that a constitutional freedom of contract barred wage laws; that turnaround improved the prospects of the federal bill Roosevelt sent to Congress two months later.
- **Q:** Why does the federal minimum wage not settle what most American workers must be paid?
  **A:** Employers must pay the highest applicable federal, state or local rate, and as of July 2026 thirty states and the District of Columbia set minimums above $7.25, so the federal rate binds mainly in states with no law or one at or below it.
- **Q:** How did the 1938 law's exemptions shape who benefited from it?
  **A:** It left out farmworkers, domestic workers, government employees, intrastate retail and much transportation; farmworkers were covered only after the 1966 amendments and domestic workers in 1974, and historian Phyllis Palmer links the exclusions to racial and gender inequalities.

### [Panic of 1873](../articles/economy-and-work/panic-of-1873.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Panic of 1873 was a purely American crash that began on Wall Street when Jay Cooke's bank failed. | Cooke's failure turned the strain into a U.S. panic, but stock market crashes in Vienna in May and September 1873 had already led European investors to dump American railroad bonds, cutting off the credit that overbuilt U.S. railroads needed. |

**Why it matters:** Seeing the transatlantic origins shows how closely American railroads and banks already depended on European capital, and why a crash abroad could ruin firms, workers and depositors across the United States.

- **Q:** How did a stock market crash in Europe help cause a banking panic in the United States?
  **A:** After the Vienna crashes of 1873, European investors sold American securities, especially railroad bonds; that cut off the borrowing that overbuilt railroads depended on, many defaulted on their bank loans, and when Jay Cooke and Company, deeply invested in the Northern Pacific Railway, failed on September 18, depositors rushed to withdraw their money from banks across the country.
- **Q:** Why did the panic fall so hard on Black depositors of the Freedman's Savings Bank?
  **A:** After an 1870 charter change, the bank's trustees had put depositors' savings into railroads, real estate and risky loans; the panic made most of those investments worthless, runs drained its cash, and when it closed in June 1874 its deposits had no federal protection, so only about half of the depositors ever received anything, and they recovered roughly three-fifths of their balances.
- **Q:** Why do historians link the depression that followed the panic to the end of Reconstruction?
  **A:** The downturn pushed economic issues ahead of Reconstruction on the national agenda, eroded Northern willingness to keep intervening in the South, and helped Democrats win the House in 1874, while white Southern Democrats were regaining power state by state; historians differ on how much weight to give the economy versus Southern political violence.

### [Stock market crash of 1929](../articles/economy-and-work/stock-market-crash-of-1929.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Most Americans had their savings in the stock market, so the crash ruined ordinary families across the country at a stroke. | Only about 3 million of roughly 120 million Americans, under 2.5 percent, owned any stock in 1929, most of them in small holdings; the widespread ruin came later, as banks failed and jobs disappeared during the Depression. |

**Why it matters:** The crash is remembered through the Depression that followed, so the hardship of the 1930s is often projected back onto a few days of trading that directly touched a small minority.

- **Q:** How did buying on margin make the crash worse once prices began to fall?
  **A:** Investors had paid only part of a share's price and borrowed the rest against the shares, an arrangement that worked only while prices kept rising; once prices fell, the borrowed positions could not be sustained, and panic selling drove prices down further.
- **Q:** Why do many economists say the crash did not by itself cause the Great Depression?
  **A:** The economy had already begun contracting in the summer of 1929, the crash's effects on spending faded within months and recovery looked likely by fall 1930, and it was later problems in the financial system, above all bank failures, that turned a recession into a depression; some, like Allan Meltzer, call the crash a symptom of the downturn rather than a cause.
- **Q:** How did the crash change federal regulation of Wall Street?
  **A:** It led the Senate to investigate stock exchange practices; the Pecora hearings exposed questionable dealings such as Charles Mitchell's tax-avoiding stock sale and built support for the Banking Act of 1933, which separated commercial from investment banking and created deposit insurance, and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934.

### [Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire](../articles/economy-and-work/triangle-shirtwaist-factory-fire.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Triangle owners were convicted and punished for the deaths. | Max Blanck and Isaac Harris were indicted for manslaughter but acquitted on December 27, 1911, after the jury was asked whether they knew the ninth-floor door was locked. Civil suits were settled in 1914 for $75 per life lost, and in 1913 Blanck was fined just $20 for again locking a factory door during working hours. |

**Why it matters:** The fire is remembered for the sweeping reforms that followed, so it is easy to assume the law also held the owners accountable; in fact the reforms came largely because existing law had failed to.

- **Q:** Why did so many workers on the ninth floor die when most on the eighth and tenth floors escaped?
  **A:** The ninth-floor workers were trapped by a locked stairwell door, blocked exits and a single overwhelmed elevator; the fire escape collapsed and firefighters' ladders could not reach that high, so many died inside or jumped.
- **Q:** How did the fire lead to new labor laws in New York?
  **A:** Public outrage and lobbying led the legislature to create the Factory Investigating Commission in June 1911; its hearings and inspections of thousands of workplaces produced dozens of state laws on fire safety, sanitation, machine guarding and limits on working hours for women and children.
- **Q:** How did the fire's influence reach the national level two decades later?
  **A:** Frances Perkins witnessed the fire and worked for the commission; as Franklin Roosevelt's Secretary of Labor she applied lessons from New York's reforms nationally, and New Deal laws of the 1930s adopted similar standards.
- **Q:** Why were the owners acquitted despite testimony that the doors would not open?
  **A:** The jury had to decide whether Blanck and Harris knew the door was locked at the time of the fire, and defense lawyer Max Steuer raised enough doubt on that point to win a not-guilty verdict.

## foreign-relations-and-military

### [Good Neighbor Policy](../articles/foreign-relations-and-military/good-neighbor-policy.md) — Policy

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy is what brought the Marines home from Nicaragua. | The last Marines left Nicaragua on January 2, 1933, two months before Roosevelt's inauguration; the retreat from intervention began under Herbert Hoover, and Roosevelt continued it, ending the occupation of Haiti in 1934. |

**Why it matters:** The policy is named for a phrase in Roosevelt's 1933 inaugural address, so changes that began under his predecessors are often credited to him.

- **Q:** Why did abrogating the 1903 treaty with Cuba matter to the Good Neighbor policy?
  **A:** That treaty, based on the Platt Amendment, had given the United States a legal right to intervene in Cuba, so giving it up in 1934 turned the pledge against armed intervention into a concrete change.
- **Q:** How did the U.S. position on non-intervention change between Montevideo in 1933 and Buenos Aires in 1936?
  **A:** At Montevideo Hull voted for the article forbidding intervention but attached a reservation keeping the U.S. free to follow its own policies and the law of nations; at Buenos Aires the United States accepted the non-intervention protocol without reservation, as Latin American states had wanted.
- **Q:** How did Roosevelt's response to Mexico's 1938 oil expropriation test the policy?
  **A:** Instead of intervening on behalf of the American companies, his administration accepted Mexico's right to expropriate with compensation and negotiated a settlement, partly to keep Mexico from aligning with the Axis.
- **Q:** Why do some historians see a contradiction in the Good Neighbor policy?
  **A:** While renouncing military intervention, Washington backed dictators such as Somoza, Trujillo and Batista and kept influence through advisers, loans, trade treaties and military ties, and the Cold War brought renewed interventions.

### [North Atlantic Treaty Organization](../articles/foreign-relations-and-military/nato.md) — Organization

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Article 5 automatically obliges every NATO member to go to war when an ally is attacked. | Article 5 treats an attack on one as an attack on all, but each member takes "such action as it deems necessary," which may or may not include armed force; U.S. negotiators insisted on that wording because the Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war. |

**Why it matters:** The slogan "an attack on one is an attack on all" is remembered without the clause that leaves each government to decide its own response.

- **Q:** Why did the United States refuse to write an automatic military commitment into the North Atlantic Treaty?
  **A:** European negotiators wanted a guarantee of automatic U.S. intervention, but under the Constitution only Congress can declare war, so Article 5 lets each member take the action it deems necessary, including but not limited to armed force.
- **Q:** How did the Vandenberg Resolution prepare the way for NATO?
  **A:** Adopted by the Senate in June 1948, it endorsed U.S. association with regional collective-defense arrangements under the UN Charter and through constitutional processes, giving the administration bipartisan Senate backing to negotiate the treaty.
- **Q:** Why was joining NATO a break with earlier American foreign policy?
  **A:** It was the first peacetime military alliance the United States entered outside the Western Hemisphere, ending a long tradition of avoiding alliances with European powers.
- **Q:** What did the only invocation of Article 5 lead to?
  **A:** After the September 11, 2001, attacks the allies invoked Article 5 on September 12; NATO then agreed eight support measures and sent AWACS radar planes to patrol U.S. skies in Operation Eagle Assist, its first Article 5 operation.

### [Panama Canal](../articles/foreign-relations-and-military/panama-canal.md) — Landmark

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Ships crossing the Panama Canal from the Atlantic to the Pacific sail westward. | From its Atlantic end the canal runs south for about 10 miles and then east to the Pacific, so a ship heading for the Pacific finishes its transit to the east of where it started. |

**Why it matters:** World maps fix the Atlantic on the east and the Pacific on the west, but the Isthmus of Panama bends in an S-shape, so the general picture of the Americas misleads about this crossing.

- **Q:** Why did Panama, rather than Colombia, sign the 1903 canal treaty with the United States?
  **A:** Colombia's congress rejected the Hay-Herrán Treaty over its financial terms; Panama then declared independence on November 3, 1903, with U.S. warships nearby, the United States recognized it within three days, and Panama's envoy Philippe Bunau-Varilla signed the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty on November 18.
- **Q:** How did the gold and silver rolls shape life for the canal's workers?
  **A:** Skilled white workers, mostly from the United States, were paid in gold on the gold roll and got better housing, dining and clubs; the mostly Black West Indian laborers on the silver roll were paid in local silver at about half the wage, lived in crowded camps, did the most dangerous work such as dynamiting, and died at far higher rates.
- **Q:** Why did the United States succeed in building a canal where the French effort had failed?
  **A:** The French were defeated by disease and bankruptcy after about 20,000 deaths; the Americans first ran a large sanitation campaign under William Gorgas that ended yellow fever in Panama City by 1905 and cut malaria deaths sharply, and they switched to a lock design, which Stevens argued could be finished about a decade sooner than a sea-level cut.
- **Q:** Why did the United States agree in 1977 to hand the canal to Panama?
  **A:** Many Panamanians had long questioned the 1903 treaty, which Bunau-Varilla negotiated without formal approval from Panama's government; after the 1964 flag riot both countries saw the need for a new agreement, and U.S. officials feared isolation abroad if talks failed, so the Torrijos-Carter Treaties scheduled the transfer for December 31, 1999, while a separate treaty kept the canal permanently neutral.

### [Roosevelt Corollary](../articles/foreign-relations-and-military/roosevelt-corollary.md) — Policy

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Roosevelt Corollary was simply part of the Monroe Doctrine as announced in 1823. | It was a separate statement made more than eighty years later, in Theodore Roosevelt's December 1904 message to Congress. The 1823 doctrine asked European powers to stay out of the hemisphere; the corollary claimed a right for the United States itself to intervene. |

**Why it matters:** Roosevelt presented his policy as an extension of Monroe's, and the two are usually taught together, so the later claim of a U.S. police power is easily read back into 1823.

- **Q:** Why did a crisis over Venezuela's debts prompt Roosevelt to rethink the Monroe Doctrine?
  **A:** The 1902 European blockade of Venezuela showed that European powers would use force to collect debts in the Americas; Roosevelt feared such actions could become invasions, so he claimed a U.S. right to step in first and make sure creditors were paid.
- **Q:** How did the corollary turn the Monroe Doctrine's meaning around?
  **A:** Monroe's 1823 doctrine was a largely passive warning to Europeans not to intervene in the hemisphere, while the corollary justified U.S. intervention, including later occupations of Haiti and the Dominican Republic.
- **Q:** How did the Dominican customs receivership show the corollary in practice?
  **A:** A U.S.-appointed receiver collected Dominican customs duties and applied them to receivership costs and a refinanced debt before passing the rest to the Dominican government, which could not raise its debt or change tariffs without U.S. agreement.
- **Q:** Why did Roosevelt use executive agreements in the Dominican case?
  **A:** Critics said his January 1905 agreement bypassed the Senate, and when Democratic opposition blocked a treaty, he put the receivership into effect by a modus vivendi until the Senate approved a convention in 1907.

### [Third Locks Project](../articles/foreign-relations-and-military/third-locks-project.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The third set of Panama Canal locks that opened in 2016 was an idea first pursued in the 2000s. | The United States authorized a third set of larger locks in 1939 and dug for nearly two years before halting the work in 1942; Panama's expansion later built its Atlantic-side locks on that original excavation. |

**Why it matters:** The 1940s work stopped before any lock was built, so it left only dormant channels rather than a landmark, and it is easily overlooked in accounts of the modern expansion.

- **Q:** Why did Congress authorize a third set of locks for the Panama Canal in 1939?
  **A:** Ships, especially warships, were growing too wide for the 110-foot lock chambers, and officials feared one act of sabotage could cripple the existing locks, so with war looming they backed larger, separately sited locks to improve both defense and commercial capacity.
- **Q:** Why did President Roosevelt sign the 1939 law while objecting to part of it?
  **A:** He wanted the third locks for the security of the canal and the hemisphere, but the law required new skilled and supervisory construction hires to be U.S. citizens, which he said conflicted with the 1936 treaty pledge of equal job opportunity for Panamanians, so he promised to seek an amendment.
- **Q:** Why was the project stopped in 1942?
  **A:** After Pearl Harbor naval priorities shifted from battleships to aircraft carriers, the Montana-class battleships the locks were sized for were suspended, wartime money and materials were needed elsewhere, and Army commanders argued new locks would add another air-attack target; Secretary of War Stimson halted the work on May 23, 1942.
- **Q:** How did the abandoned 1940s work affect the canal's 21st-century expansion?
  **A:** On the Atlantic side, Panama built its new, larger lock chambers on the original third-locks excavation, which sped construction; on the Pacific side the locks were placed elsewhere and the old cut was filled in.

### [War in Afghanistan (2001–2021)](../articles/foreign-relations-and-military/war-in-afghanistan.md) — Conflict

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Afghanistan's army collapsed in 2021 simply because its soldiers would not fight. | At least 66,000 Afghan troops were killed in the war (SIGAR, 2021), and Congressional researchers traced the collapse to structural causes — corruption that left soldiers unpaid or unfed, dependence on departing U.S. contractors for maintenance, intelligence and air support, overstretched rural outposts, and demoralization after the 2020 U.S.-Taliban agreement. |

**Why it matters:** The speed of the Taliban's August 2021 advance made a single explanation tempting, but it hides two decades of heavy Afghan losses and the design choices that left the force unable to sustain itself once foreign support ended.

- **Q:** Why did the United States go to war in Afghanistan in 2001?
  **A:** After the September 11 attacks Congress authorized force against those who planned them and anyone who harbored them; al Qaeda was based in Afghanistan under Taliban protection, so U.S. strikes began on October 7, 2001, to destroy al Qaeda's base and drive the Taliban from power.
- **Q:** How did the war in Iraq affect the war in Afghanistan?
  **A:** From 2003 U.S. attention and resources shifted to Iraq, where about 150,000–170,000 U.S. troops served against about 20,000 in Afghanistan; officials later said Afghanistan was under-managed in those years as the Taliban regrouped and violence rose from about 2005–2006.
- **Q:** How did the 2020 Doha agreement contribute to the events of August 2021?
  **A:** It committed the United States to withdraw all forces within 14 months without conditioning that on reduced Taliban violence; Congressional researchers reported it demoralized Afghan forces, and the later departure of contractors who maintained aircraft and supplied air support helped the Afghan military collapse as the Taliban took Kabul on August 15, 2021.

## geography-and-environment

### [1964 Alaska earthquake](../articles/geography-and-environment/1964-alaska-earthquake.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Most of those killed in the 1964 Alaska earthquake died in collapsing buildings during the shaking. | The USGS attributes the large majority of deaths—about 119 to 122 of some 131—to tsunamis, many of them local waves raised by underwater landslides at places such as Valdez, Chenega and Seward rather than by the main ocean-crossing wave. |

**Why it matters:** Photographs of Anchorage's wrecked downtown and the Turnagain Heights slide dominate memory of the disaster, while the deadliest waves struck small coastal towns and Native villages within minutes, far from cameras.

- **Q:** Why did the 1964 earthquake have no large fault visible at the surface, and why did that matter for science?
  **A:** It ruptured a subduction zone, where oceanic crust sinks beneath continental crust, so the slip happened deep below Prince William Sound. George Plafker's mapping of coastlines that rose and sank showed that pattern and gave some of the first real-world proof of tectonic plate movement.
- **Q:** How did the earthquake cause deaths as far away as California and Oregon?
  **A:** Uplift of the sea floor generated a tsunami that crossed the Pacific in hours, killing 12 people at Crescent City, California, and four children at Beverly Beach, Oregon.
- **Q:** Why was the town of Valdez moved after the earthquake?
  **A:** Old Valdez sat on water-saturated silt and sand; liquefaction triggered an underwater landslide that destroyed the waterfront and killed about 30 people, and geologists judged the site unstable, so the town was rebuilt on bedrock a few miles west near Mineral Creek.
- **Q:** What lasting warning and monitoring systems resulted from the earthquake?
  **A:** A tsunami warning center was founded at Palmer, Alaska, in 1967 (now the National Tsunami Warning Center), and the USGS credits the event with spurring its Earthquake Hazards Program, the Alaska Earthquake Center and other monitoring networks.

### [Bureau of Ocean Energy Management](../articles/geography-and-environment/bureau-of-ocean-energy-management.md) — Institution

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| After the Deepwater Horizon spill, Congress passed a law abolishing the Minerals Management Service and creating BOEM. | The breakup was carried out administratively, by the Interior Secretary's orders of 2010. The House passed a bill to restructure the agency in July 2010, but Congress did not enact the split into law; the 2026 merger into the Marine Minerals Administration was likewise made by secretarial order. |

**Why it matters:** Other large post-crisis reorganizations, such as the creation of the Department of Homeland Security, were done by statute, and news coverage often described the 2010–2011 changes as a sweeping reform without noting how they were made.

- **Q:** Why did the Interior Department split the Minerals Management Service into separate bodies after 2010?
  **A:** One agency had been both selling offshore leases and collecting about $13 billion a year in revenue while also policing drilling safety; after the Deepwater Horizon blowout and earlier inspector-general findings of ethical lapses and conflicts of interest, Secretary Salazar's order separated leasing and planning (BOEM), safety enforcement (BSEE) and revenue collection (ONRR) so that their missions would not pull against one another.
- **Q:** How does BOEM's job differ from that of the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement?
  **A:** BOEM decides where and when offshore resources are offered and manages them — the five-year oil and gas leasing program, renewable-energy leases, sand and gravel for coastal restoration, resource evaluation and environmental studies — while BSEE permits and inspects operations and enforces safety and environmental rules.
- **Q:** Why is the 2026 merger into the Marine Minerals Administration disputed?
  **A:** Interior and industry supporters say two overlapping bureaus administering one law cause delays and that a single agency can coordinate better without weakening safety standards; critics, including environmental groups and congressional Democrats, argue that recombining leasing and safety oversight removes the separation created after Deepwater Horizon.

### [Chesapeake Bay](../articles/geography-and-environment/chesapeake-bay.md) — NaturalFeature

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Chesapeake Bay was created by a meteor or comet impact. | A bolide did strike about 35 million years ago, leaving a crater about 85 km across now buried 300–500 m beneath the southern bay, but the bay itself formed only about 10,000 years ago, when rising seas flooded river valleys; the old crater's depression helped steer those rivers, and so the bay's location. |

**Why it matters:** The impact story is dramatic and true in part, but treating it as the bay's origin skips the Ice Age sea-level rise that actually made the estuary, and hides why the crater still matters today through land subsidence and salty groundwater.

- **Q:** If the Chesapeake Bay impact crater did not create the bay, why does it matter to the bay's geography?
  **A:** The 35-million-year-old crater left a long-lasting depression that changed river courses, so when rising seas flooded the Susquehanna and other valleys about 10,000 years ago the bay formed where those valleys converged over it; the crater's breccia also compacts and lowers the land, and its very salty pore water limits fresh groundwater.
- **Q:** How did control of the Chesapeake Bay shape the outcome at Yorktown in 1781?
  **A:** After the Battle of the Capes on September 5, 1781, the damaged British fleet withdrew to New York and the French controlled the bay, so no British reinforcements could reach Cornwallis while Washington's forces used the bay to move troops and supplies; the relief fleet sailed only on October 19, the day Cornwallis surrendered.
- **Q:** Why did the Chesapeake oyster fishery collapse after its 1880 peak?
  **A:** Heavy, often illegal dredging and tonging stripped and broke up the reefs, reef habitat shrank, and the introduced MSX parasite (from 1959) and a worsening native Dermo parasite in the 1980s killed oysters, until both public and private fisheries collapsed in the mid-1980s.
- **Q:** What does the Chesapeake Bay TMDL limit, and why?
  **A:** It caps the nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment entering the bay from all seven watershed jurisdictions, because those pollutants cloud the water and feed algae blooms that shade underwater grasses and lower the dissolved oxygen fish, crabs and oysters need.

### [Colorado River](../articles/geography-and-environment/colorado-river.md) — NaturalFeature

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The 1922 Colorado River Compact divided a river whose long-term average flow was well known. | Negotiators relied on only about 20 years of gauge records; a 1976 tree-ring reconstruction put the 1564–1961 average at about 13.5 million acre-feet a year and found 1905–1930 the wettest such period in 400 years, while the Compact and the 1944 treaty together assigned up to 17.5 million. |

**Why it matters:** The Compact's fixed numbers look like measurements, but they rest on an unusually wet stretch of record, which is a root of today's shortages.

- **Q:** Why did the 1922 Compact divide the river at Lee Ferry rather than state by state, and what did it promise each side?
  **A:** Herbert Hoover proposed splitting the basin into an Upper and a Lower half; the Compact gave each 7.5 million acre-feet a year (the Lower Basin could add 1 million more) and required the Upper Division not to deplete the flow at Lee Ferry below 75 million acre-feet over any ten years. The Lower Basin's share was divided among Arizona, California and Nevada later, by the 1928 Boulder Canyon Project Act.
- **Q:** How do tree-ring studies help explain the Colorado River's modern water shortages?
  **A:** They show the Compact was based on an unusually wet period (1905–1930), so the long-term flow (about 13.5 million acre-feet a year in one reconstruction) is far below the 16.5 million acre-feet promised to the two basins plus 1.5 million to Mexico.
- **Q:** How were Native nations' water rights treated in the Compact and later recognized?
  **A:** No tribe took part in the 1922 negotiations, and the Compact only said it did not affect U.S. obligations to tribes; in Arizona v. California (1963) the Supreme Court applied the 1908 Winters doctrine and held that five lower-river reservations had reserved enough water to irrigate their practicably irrigable land.

### [Hayden Geological Survey of 1871](../articles/geography-and-environment/hayden-geological-survey-of-1871.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| William Henry Jackson's photographs, laid on the desk of every member of Congress, persuaded Congress to create Yellowstone National Park. | The Congressional Globe mentions the photographs only once, when Senator Pomeroy told colleagues on January 23, 1872, that they could see them; the House debate never mentions them, Hayden's report carried drawings rather than photographs, and the desk story first appears in a 1947 book by Jackson's son with no contemporary record to support it. The photographs were one part of a larger body of evidence alongside reports, specimens, art, lectures and personal lobbying. |

**Why it matters:** Histories of photography from the late 1930s onward repeated the story, often without sources, because a single striking cause is easier to tell than a ten-week campaign by many advocates.

- **Q:** Why did Hayden's survey carry a painter and a photographer as well as scientists?
  **A:** Hayden's instructions called for gathering as full material as possible to illustrate his report, so alongside botanists, a mineralogist, a topographer and others he brought photographer William Henry Jackson and artists Thomas Moran and Henry W. Elliott to give visual proof of features earlier explorers had only described.
- **Q:** How did the survey contribute to the creation of Yellowstone National Park?
  **A:** It corroborated the 1869 and 1870 accounts with scientific measurements, maps and specimens; Hayden then went to Washington to report, displayed specimens and images for members of Congress and published articles urging a park law, joining Nathaniel Langford and others in the lobbying that preceded the March 1, 1872 act.
- **Q:** Why do some historians doubt that Jackson's photographs were decisive?
  **A:** Howard Bossen found that the congressional record mentions the photographs only once, the House never discussed them, and the famous story of prints on every desk comes from a 1947 book with no contemporary support, so the images look like one strand of evidence among many rather than the deciding factor.
- **Q:** Why is the survey's legacy now viewed more critically?
  **A:** Hayden made demeaning statements about Native peoples, urging that they be made into farmers or destroyed if they resisted federal demands, and the park his survey helped create removed Indigenous people from lands they had used for at least 11,000 years.

### [Mississippi River](../articles/geography-and-environment/mississippi-river.md) — NaturalFeature

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Mississippi is the longest river in the United States. | The U.S. Geological Survey lists the Missouri River, the Mississippi's largest tributary, at 2,540 miles against the Mississippi's 2,340, and the National Park Service also describes the Missouri as about 100 miles longer. |

**Why it matters:** The Mississippi carries the larger flow and the larger name, and the two rivers are often added together as one system, so its length record gets assumed rather than checked.

- **Q:** Why is it inaccurate to call the Mississippi the longest river in the United States, and why do published lengths for it differ?
  **A:** The Missouri, which flows into it, is longer (2,540 miles against 2,340 in the USGS list, or about 100 miles longer by the Park Service's count); figures for the Mississippi itself range from about 2,300 to 2,350 miles because agencies measure a shifting channel differently.
- **Q:** Why did Union leaders consider capturing Vicksburg essential?
  **A:** The Confederacy had closed the Mississippi to Northern commerce; taking Vicksburg would reopen the river to Midwestern farm exports, split the South in two and cut a vital Confederate supply line.
- **Q:** How did the Great Flood of 1927 change federal flood-control policy, and who bore its heaviest costs?
  **A:** It showed that levees alone could not hold the river, so the 1928 Flood Control Act added spillways, floodways and diversion works; the heaviest costs fell on displaced residents, including many Black tenant farmers who were segregated in relief shelters and later left for northern cities.

### [Yellowstone National Park](../articles/geography-and-environment/yellowstone-national-park.md) — Landmark

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Native Americans were afraid of Yellowstone's geysers and hot springs and avoided the area. | More than 1,900 archeological sites have been found in the park even though only about 3 percent has been surveyed; people hunted, gathered, quarried obsidian and used thermal waters there for more than 11,000 years, and Kiowa, Crow and Shoshone traditions describe its thermal features as places of benevolent spirits and origin stories. |

**Why it matters:** Early park administrators promoted the story to market a tourist destination emptied of its original inhabitants and to justify keeping Native people out; a secondhand line in William Clark's journals and later scholarship repeated it.

- **Q:** Why did Congress create Yellowstone as a park rather than let it be settled like other western public land?
  **A:** Expeditions in 1869–1871, especially Hayden's survey with Moran's paintings and Jackson's photographs, publicized its wonders, and promoters including Nathaniel Langford and the railroad financier Jay Cooke, who expected tourist traffic, lobbied for a law withdrawing it from settlement, occupancy or sale and placing it under the Secretary of the Interior.
- **Q:** How did the U.S. Army come to run the park, and what changed after the 1894 Ed Howell arrest?
  **A:** Congress refused to fund civilian administration, so the Army took charge in August 1886; when soldiers caught Howell poaching bison in 1894, the publicity led Congress within about two months to pass the National Park Protection Act, which banned hunting in the park and gave its rules teeth.
- **Q:** How did the park's creation affect the Native peoples who used the area?
  **A:** The military was already moving tribes onto reservations, and after 1872 early superintendents discouraged Native people from hunting or gathering in the park; the Tukudika (Sheep Eaters) were dispersed to the Wind River and Fort Hall reservations, and the myth that Indians feared the geysers helped justify their exclusion.
- **Q:** Why do historians now treat the 1870 Madison Junction campfire story as legend?
  **A:** The account of Cornelius Hedges proposing a national park around the campfire was published by Langford only in 1905, expedition diaries recorded nothing of the kind, and the park grew out of many social, scientific, economic and aesthetic forces rather than one evening's conversation.

## gilded-age-and-progressive-era

### [Chinese Exclusion Act](../articles/gilded-age-and-progressive-era/chinese-exclusion-act.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Chinese Exclusion Act shut out every Chinese person, and its repeal in 1943 reopened immigration from China. | The 1882 act suspended the entry of Chinese laborers, skilled and unskilled, while merchants, students, teachers, diplomats and travelers with certificates could still enter; the 1943 repeal replaced exclusion with a quota of about 105 people a year, and racial quotas lasted until 1965. |

**Why it matters:** Seeing exclusion as a class-and-race filter enforced through papers, interrogations and quotas explains why Chinese families relied on certificates and paper son identities, and why its effects outlasted the repeal.

- **Q:** Why did President Arthur veto the first exclusion bill in April 1882, and how did the law he signed in May differ?
  **A:** He argued that a twenty-year ban broke the 1880 treaty, which let the United States limit but not prohibit Chinese immigration, and objected to making Chinese residents register; the act he approved cut the suspension to ten years.
- **Q:** How did the 1882 act lead to paper sons and daughters?
  **A:** Because laborers were barred while merchants and their families could still enter with papers, many Chinese immigrants entered as paper sons and daughters, claiming family ties on paper to qualify for admission.
- **Q:** Why did Congress repeal the exclusion laws in 1943, and why did the repeal not open immigration widely?
  **A:** China was a wartime ally and Japanese propaganda used exclusion against the alliance; the repeal still set an ethnic quota of about 105 Chinese immigrants a year.

### [Gilded Age](../articles/gilded-age-and-progressive-era/gilded-age.md) — Era

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Gilded Age politics was issueless and corrupt, and ordinary Americans tuned it out. | Corruption and patronage were real, but voter participation in the 1880s and 1890s was the highest in American history, presidential elections were razor-close, and voters fought hard over tariffs, currency, Prohibition and schooling. |

**Why it matters:** Taking the era's satirical label at face value hides how intensely Americans contested the direction of an industrializing country, and how those fights set up Populist and Progressive reform.

- **Q:** Why did Twain and Warner's title become the name of a whole era?
  **A:** Their 1873 novel satirized postwar corruption, and "gilded" captured the contrast historians still see between dazzling new wealth and progress on the surface and deep inequality and corruption underneath.
- **Q:** How did rapid industrialization lead to the labor conflicts of the period?
  **A:** Railroads and large corporations grew fast while wealth concentrated at the top, wages stayed low and work was often seasonal; fewer workers could expect to become independent, so many joined unions such as the Knights of Labor, and more than 20,000 strikes and lockouts followed, often broken by private guards, militia or federal troops.
- **Q:** Why do some historians, such as Rebecca Edwards, argue that "Gilded Age" is a poor label?
  **A:** The name was meant as an indictment of the elite, so it focuses attention on robber barons and corrupt politicians and misses the grassroots ferment of the period, from farmers' alliances and unions to women's organizations and Black activism.

### [Jim Crow laws](../articles/gilded-age-and-progressive-era/jim-crow-laws.md) — Policy

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Jim Crow laws existed only in the former Confederate states. | Most Jim Crow laws were passed in the South, but border states adopted them too, Oklahoma used a grandfather clause to bar Black voters until 1915, and Black Northerners met segregation in schools and public accommodations. |

**Why it matters:** Treating segregation as a purely regional custom hides how widely racial discrimination operated and why the civil rights movement sought national laws to end it.

- **Q:** Why did historians argue for decades about when Jim Crow began?
  **A:** C. Vann Woodward held that legal segregation was not inevitable and arrived mainly in the 1890s after a more fluid period, while critics such as Joel Williamson and Howard Rabinowitz found segregation by custom, and in some places by law, already in place during Reconstruction; the dispute shapes whether Jim Crow is seen as a legacy of slavery or a new system built by later choices.
- **Q:** How did southern states keep Black citizens from voting despite the Fifteenth Amendment?
  **A:** They used poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, white primaries and new state constitutions such as Louisiana's of 1898 and Alabama's of 1901, enforced alongside economic intimidation and violence including lynching.
- **Q:** Which federal actions dismantled the Jim Crow system?
  **A:** Court rulings such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954), Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections (1966) and Loving v. Virginia (1967), together with the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and the Civil Rights Act of 1968.

### [Muckrakers](../articles/gilded-age-and-progressive-era/muckrakers.md) — Movement

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle to campaign for food-safety laws, and Roosevelt coined "muckraker" as a compliment. | Sinclair, a socialist, wrote to expose the exploitation of immigrant packinghouse workers and complained that readers fixed on the tainted meat instead; Roosevelt borrowed the muck-rake image from Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress as a rebuke to journalists who saw only filth. |

**Why it matters:** The reforms that followed (food and meat laws) and the later pride journalists took in the label have reshaped memory of both, hiding Sinclair's labor message and the political tension between Roosevelt and the press.

- **Q:** Why did magazines rather than newspapers or Congress become the main vehicle for muckraking after 1900?
  **A:** Cheap, nationally circulated magazines such as McClure's, sold at fifteen cents and reaching hundreds of thousands of readers, could publish long, researched series on a national audience quickly, and exposés sold copies—Cosmopolitan's circulation doubled within two months of The Treason of the Senate.
- **Q:** How did The Jungle lead to federal law, and why was Sinclair disappointed by the result?
  **A:** Sinclair sent Roosevelt the novel, Roosevelt had the packinghouses investigated, inspectors found the unsanitary conditions it described, and Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Food and Drugs Act in 1906; Sinclair had aimed to expose the exploitation of immigrant workers and capitalism, so laws protecting consumers rather than workers missed his main point.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about whether Roosevelt's 1906 speech ended the muckraking era?
  **A:** Steffens told Roosevelt the speech had put an end to the investigations and it was vague enough to tar all reform writers, but the McClure's staff split had already come to a head for internal reasons, including McClure's risky expansion plans, and the magazine's later decline owed much to debt and the 1907 depression.

### [Plessy v. Ferguson](../articles/gilded-age-and-progressive-era/plessy-v-ferguson.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Supreme Court's majority opinion in Plessy announced the phrase "separate but equal." | The majority opinion speaks of the statute's "equal but separate" accommodations; the words "separate but equal" appear in Justice Harlan's dissent, and the label for the doctrine became common afterward. |

**Why it matters:** Knowing the exact wording shows that the Court was approving a state statute's own formula, and that the promise of equality was a legislative claim the majority accepted rather than tested.

- **Q:** Why did Homer Plessy board a whites-only car on a train that stayed inside Louisiana rather than an interstate train?
  **A:** Weeks earlier, in Abbott v. Hicks, the Louisiana Supreme Court had ruled that the Separate Car Act did not apply to interstate passengers, so the Comité des Citoyens needed an intrastate trip to test the law's constitutionality directly.
- **Q:** How did Justice Brown's majority answer the argument that forced separation stamped Black citizens as inferior?
  **A:** He said any such stigma did not come from the law itself but from how Black Americans chose to interpret it, and he treated segregation as a reasonable use of a state's police power that could take established customs into account.
- **Q:** Why is Plessy considered so consequential even though it concerned railroad cars?
  **A:** By holding that state-mandated separation did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment if accommodations were nominally equal, it gave legal cover to segregation in schools, transportation and public life for more than half a century, until Brown v. Board of Education rejected the doctrine in public education in 1954.

### [Women's suffrage movement](../articles/gilded-age-and-progressive-era/womens-suffrage-movement.md) — Movement

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Nineteenth Amendment gave all American women the right to vote in 1920. | The amendment barred denying the vote on account of sex, but most Native American women were not citizens until 1924, Chinese immigrant women were barred from citizenship by exclusion laws, and Black women in the Jim Crow South faced the same disfranchisement and violence that kept Black men from the polls. |

**Why it matters:** Treating 1920 as the finish line erases decades of further struggle by women of color and hides how race, citizenship and state law continued to decide who could vote.

- **Q:** Why did the women's suffrage movement split in 1869, and how did the two wings differ?
  **A:** The proposed Fifteenth Amendment barred racial discrimination in voting but not sex discrimination; Lucy Stone backed it and formed the American Woman Suffrage Association to win the vote state by state, while Stanton and Anthony opposed it, used racist arguments against Black men voting, and formed the National Woman Suffrage Association to seek a federal amendment.
- **Q:** How did NAWSA's and the National Woman's Party's different strategies combine to win the Nineteenth Amendment?
  **A:** Catt's 1916 Winning Plan concentrated NAWSA's resources on promising state campaigns, lobbying Congress and preparing for ratification, while Alice Paul's National Woman's Party kept pressure on President Wilson through parades, White House picketing, arrests and hunger strikes; together they built the pressure that brought Wilson's support in 1918 and passage by Congress in 1919.
- **Q:** Why did many women remain unable to vote after 1920?
  **A:** The amendment only banned sex discrimination, so other barriers still applied; Native American women were mostly not citizens until 1924, Chinese immigrant women could not become citizens under exclusion laws, and Southern states used the same discriminatory laws, intimidation and violence against Black women that they used against Black men.

## native-nations

### [Cherokee Nation v. Georgia](../articles/native-nations/cherokee-nation-v-georgia.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia the Supreme Court upheld Georgia's laws and ruled that the Cherokee Nation had no sovereignty. | The Court never reached the merits; it dismissed the suit because it lacked jurisdiction, while describing the Cherokee Nation as a distinct political society capable of governing itself. A year later Worcester v. Georgia held Georgia's laws void inside the Nation. |

**Why it matters:** Because the Cherokees lost the case and were later forcibly removed, the jurisdictional dismissal is easily remembered as a ruling on Georgia's power or a denial of Cherokee nationhood.

- **Q:** Why did the Supreme Court refuse to hear the Cherokee Nation's suit against Georgia?
  **A:** The Constitution lets the Court hear suits between a state and foreign states, and the majority held that the Cherokee Nation, though a distinct political society, was not a foreign state but a domestic dependent nation, partly because the commerce clause lists foreign nations, states and Indian tribes as separate categories; without jurisdiction it had to dismiss the case.
- **Q:** How did the 1831 dismissal help set up Worcester v. Georgia?
  **A:** Marshall suggested the land question might be decided in a proper case with proper parties, and when Georgia convicted missionaries such as Samuel Worcester under its license law, their appeal from a state court gave the Supreme Court jurisdiction to rule that Georgia's laws had no force in the Cherokee Nation.
- **Q:** Why is the case's guardian-and-ward language still important in federal Indian law?
  **A:** Marshall's description of the tribes' relation to the United States as resembling that of a ward to its guardian became a root of the federal trust responsibility, the duty the federal government owes tribes to protect their remaining lands and provide services.

### [Dawes Act](../articles/native-nations/dawes-act.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Dawes Act divided the lands of every Native nation, including the Cherokee, and the Dawes Rolls were drawn up under it. | Section 8 of the 1887 act exempted the Cherokee, Creek (Muscogee), Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole nations, the Osage and several other nations in Indian Territory, as well as the Seneca reservations in New York. The Five Tribes were allotted later through the Dawes Commission of 1893, the Curtis Act of 1898 and negotiated agreements, and the Dawes Rolls, closed in 1907, were that commission's enrollment lists. |

**Why it matters:** Because Henry Dawes chaired the later commission and the rolls carry his name, the 1887 law, the commission and the rolls are often treated as one thing, hiding how the Five Tribes resisted allotment and how it was imposed on them separately.

- **Q:** How did the Dawes Act reduce Native landholding even though it gave land to individual Native people?
  **A:** After each tribal member received an allotment, the reservation land left over was declared "surplus" and could be bought by the government and sold to non-Indians; allottees later lost more land through tax foreclosures, distressed sales and leases once trust protections were weakened, so Native-held land fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million by 1934.
- **Q:** Why did reformers and western developers both support allotment?
  **A:** Reformers believed private property and farming would assimilate Native people into American society, while western interests wanted the "surplus" land opened for settlement, mining, ranching and forestry; historians call their partnership an uneasy alliance that got the bill through Congress.
- **Q:** Why does allotment still cause problems for tribes and landowners today?
  **A:** When an allottee died, the land was not divided but passed to heirs as undivided shares, so over generations single tracts came to have hundreds of owners; this fractionation, together with checkerboarded trust and non-Indian land, makes the land hard to use, govern or develop, which the Interior Department's Land Buy-Back Program tried to address.

### [Haudenosaunee](../articles/native-nations/haudenosaunee.md) — TribalNation

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The framers copied the United States Constitution from the Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace. | Some framers knew and admired the Six Nations' union, and Congress formally acknowledged the confederacy's influence in 1988, but historians disagree about how much it shaped the Constitution; the two systems also differ sharply, since Haudenosaunee clan mothers select chiefs and the Grand Council decides by consensus rather than by elections and majority votes. |

**Why it matters:** The story is a corrective to histories that left Native nations out of the founding, and real documents such as Franklin's 1751 letter seem to confirm it, so an argument about influence is easily remembered as a claim of direct copying.

- **Q:** How does the Haudenosaunee Grand Council reach a decision?
  **A:** The 50 chiefs sit on three benches — the Mohawk and Seneca as Older Brothers, the Oneida and Cayuga as Younger Brothers, and the Onondaga as Firekeepers — and each bench must agree before passing a matter on; a decision is made only when all three agree, which the Onondaga Nation calls reaching "One Mind."
- **Q:** Why did the American Revolution devastate the Haudenosaunee even though one of its nations fought on the winning side?
  **A:** The confederacy split, with the Oneida and Tuscarora supporting the Americans and most of the other nations joining Britain; American forces burned many Seneca, Onondaga and Cayuga towns in 1779, an Oneida village was destroyed in 1780, and after the war New York's land purchases shrank even the Oneida homeland despite the 1784 Fort Stanwix guarantee.
- **Q:** How did the Kinzua Dam conflict with the Treaty of Canandaigua?
  **A:** In 1794 the United States promised never to claim or disturb the Seneca lands it recognized, yet the federal government took Allegany Reservation land by eminent domain for the dam, flooding about 10,000 acres and moving about 700 people, after courts declined to stop it.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about whether the Haudenosaunee influenced the Constitution?
  **A:** Scholars such as Donald Grinde and Bruce Johansen point to Canasatego's 1744 call for colonial union and Franklin's 1751 letter as evidence of influence, while Elisabeth Tooker argued the evidence is thin, that European theory supplied the framers' models, and that the League's matrilineal, consensus-based structure differs greatly from the federal system.

### [Indian Removal Act](../articles/native-nations/indian-removal-act.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Indian Removal Act itself ordered the army to drive Native nations out of the East. | The act authorized the president to offer western lands in exchange for eastern homelands to nations that chose to make the exchange, and appropriated $500,000; the force came afterwards, through treaties obtained under heavy pressure — the Cherokee treaty was signed by a faction without the authority of the nation's government — and through troops sent to enforce them. |

**Why it matters:** Knowing that the law was framed as voluntary exchange shows how coercion operated through treaties, state laws and enforcement decisions, and why the Cherokee and others fought removal in the courts and in Congress rather than simply in the field.

- **Q:** If the Indian Removal Act was written as a voluntary exchange of lands, how did it lead to the forced removal of the Cherokee Nation?
  **A:** The act let the president negotiate exchange treaties. When the Cherokee government under John Ross refused, federal agents concluded the Treaty of New Echota with a minority Treaty Party that had no authority to sign it; the Senate ratified it by a single vote, and in 1838 President Van Buren sent the army to round Cherokees into stockades and march them west.
- **Q:** Why did the Supreme Court's ruling in Worcester v. Georgia fail to stop removal?
  **A:** The Court held that Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory, but neither Georgia nor President Jackson enforced the decision, so state pressure on Cherokee land continued.
- **Q:** Why do estimates of how many people were removed range from about 50,000 to about 88,000?
  **A:** The figures count different things — about 50,000 eastern Native people removed under Jackson's treaties, about 70,000 from the five southeastern nations over several decades, and about 88,000 including northern nations removed in the 1830s and 1840s.

### [Native Americans](../articles/native-nations/native-americans.md) — Community

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Native Americans are a vanishing people who belong to the American past. | The 2020 census counted 9.7 million people who identify as American Indian or Alaska Native alone or in combination, up from 5.2 million in 2010; the United States recognizes 575 tribal nations, and more than 70 percent of Native people live in urban or suburban areas. |

**Why it matters:** Costumes, tipis and other stock images suggest that Native cultures exist only in the past, hiding the contemporary nations and communities that survived and continue to thrive.

- **Q:** Why do scholars' estimates of the Native population before 1492 differ so widely?
  **A:** Early counters like James Mooney and Alfred Kroeber used numbers recorded by Europeans after contact, which already reflected deaths from epidemics; later scholars such as Henry Dobyns scaled those numbers up on the assumption that disease had killed most people before counting began, so the result depends on how large one judges the epidemic losses to have been.
- **Q:** How did federal policy toward Native nations change between the 1950s and the 1970s?
  **A:** In the 1950s the government pursued termination of federal supervision over tribes and relocated Native people to cities; by 1970 President Nixon called forced termination unacceptable, and the 1975 Self-Determination Act let tribes run federal programs themselves, shifting policy toward tribal self-government.
- **Q:** Why did gaining citizenship in 1924 not guarantee Native Americans the vote?
  **A:** Voting remained governed by state law, and some states continued to bar Native Americans from voting until 1957.
- **Q:** Why does the census report two different counts of the American Indian and Alaska Native population?
  **A:** The "alone" figure (3.7 million in 2020) counts only people who chose that single race, while the "alone or in combination" figure (9.7 million) also includes people who reported it together with another race.

### [Tribal sovereignty](../articles/native-nations/tribal-sovereignty.md) — Principle

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Tribal sovereignty is a privilege the federal government granted to Native Americans as a racial group. | Federal law treats tribal self-government as inherent, held before the United States existed and acknowledged in treaties and the Constitution rather than created by them; tribal status is political, attaching to nations and their citizens, not to race. |

**Why it matters:** Because Congress can limit tribal powers and federal agencies administer many tribal programs, sovereignty is easy to mistake for a federal gift, and because tribal citizens are Native people, a political status is easy to mistake for a racial one.

- **Q:** Why did the Supreme Court in Worcester v. Georgia hold that Georgia's laws could not reach the Cherokee Nation?
  **A:** Chief Justice John Marshall reasoned that Indian nations had always been distinct, independent political communities whose relations with the United States ran through treaties and federal law, so the Cherokee Nation was a distinct community occupying its own territory where state law had no force, and the Georgia statute used to convict Samuel Worcester was void.
- **Q:** How is tribal sovereignty both inherent and limited under federal law?
  **A:** The Court treats tribal powers as inherent, existing before and apart from the United States, yet it has also held that Congress has broad (plenary but not absolute) power over Indian affairs and can restrict tribal authority, as when courts held tribes lack criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians without congressional authorization.
- **Q:** How did termination policy in the 1950s and 1960s run against the principle of tribal sovereignty?
  **A:** Termination sought to end federal recognition and trust protection of tribes and shifted reservation jurisdiction to states; Congress began 60 termination proceedings and more than three million acres of tribal land were lost before the policy was repudiated in 1970 in favor of self-determination.

## people-who-shaped-america

### [Frederick Douglass](../articles/people-who-shaped-america/frederick-douglass.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Douglass was born on February 14, 1818, a birthday recorded at the time. | No record gives the day. Douglass believed he was born about 1817; a ledger kept by his enslaver Aaron Anthony notes February 1818, possibly added later, and Douglass chose February 14 to celebrate. |

**Why it matters:** Enslaved people were routinely denied knowledge of their own birthdays, and the uncertainty is itself evidence of what slavery took from them.

- **Q:** Why did publishing his Narrative in 1845 put Douglass in danger, and how was that danger resolved?
  **A:** The book named the people and places of his enslavement, exposing him as a fugitive who could be seized; he toured Britain and Ireland, where supporters raised $711.66 to buy his freedom from Hugh Auld, so he returned to the United States legally free in 1847.
- **Q:** How did Douglass's view of the Constitution change, and why does it matter?
  **A:** He first held William Lloyd Garrison's view that the Constitution was a proslavery compact, but between about 1848 and 1852 came to read it as an antislavery document; that let him work through politics and the law against slavery and broke his alliance with Garrison.
- **Q:** Why did Douglass support the Fifteenth Amendment even though it did not enfranchise women?
  **A:** He backed women's suffrage, but argued in 1869 that for Black Americans in the former slave states the vote was a matter of life and death, so Black men's voting rights could not wait for a separate women's amendment.

### [Harriet Tubman](../articles/people-who-shaped-america/harriet-tubman.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Tubman made 19 trips into the South and rescued more than 300 people, with a $40,000 bounty on her head. | Records and her own accounts show about 13 trips, all to Maryland's Eastern Shore, freeing about 70 people, while she gave directions to about 70 more who escaped on their own. The only documented reward was $100 each for her and two brothers in 1849, offered by slaveholders who did not know she was Tubman. |

**Why it matters:** The larger numbers come from Sarah Bradford's 1868–69 biography and an 1867 letter supporting her pension claim; they stuck because they suited a legend, but they obscure how carefully she chose people she knew and trusted.

- **Q:** Why did Tubman return only to Maryland's Eastern Shore rather than travel all over the South?
  **A:** Her missions were to bring away family members and friends she knew and trusted; she knew that landscape from her years of labor in its marshes and woods, and unfamiliar places were far more dangerous.
- **Q:** How did the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 change where Tubman took the people she rescued?
  **A:** The 1850 law put any freedom seeker in the United States, even in the North, at risk of recapture, so she guided people beyond Philadelphia to St. Catharines, Ontario, in Canada.
- **Q:** Why did Tubman have to petition Congress for a pension, and what did she get?
  **A:** She had served the Union as a nurse, cook, scout and spy without formal enlistment or regular pay, and at first received only an $8-a-month widow's pension as Nelson Davis's widow; in 1899 Congress raised it to $20 a month for her service as a nurse.

### [John Marshall](../articles/people-who-shaped-america/john-marshall.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| John Marshall held only a handful of enslaved household servants, so slavery was marginal to his life. | Marshall bought hundreds of enslaved people over his lifetime; by 1830 he held about 150, and with the people he gave his sons the family's plantations held roughly 250. |

**Why it matters:** Many biographers repeated a figure of about a dozen servants; Marshall's 1827 will and the 1830 census show far more, and the smaller number hides how deeply slavery shaped his household and fortune.

- **Q:** How did Marbury v. Madison let Marshall strengthen the Supreme Court while denying William Marbury his remedy?
  **A:** Marshall held that Marbury had a right to his commission but that the provision of the Judiciary Act of 1789 letting the Court issue the writ exceeded the Constitution; by refusing to apply an unconstitutional act of Congress, the Court established judicial review.
- **Q:** Why did Marshall's ruling in Worcester v. Georgia fail to protect the Cherokee Nation from removal?
  **A:** Although the Court held that Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory, Georgia ignored the ruling and President Jackson did not enforce it; a dissident Cherokee faction signed the Treaty of New Echota in 1835 and the U.S. Army forced most Cherokees west in 1838.
- **Q:** How does Paul Finkelman connect Marshall's slaveholding to his decisions?
  **A:** He argues that Marshall, who bought hundreds of enslaved people and supported removing free Black people from Virginia, never wrote an opinion supporting Black freedom, and that enslaved plaintiffs lost every freedom case in which he wrote the majority opinion.
- **Q:** Why did Marshall's broad view of national power put him at odds with Presidents Jefferson, Madison and Monroe?
  **A:** Marshall favored a strong national government with the federal courts as final interpreters of the Constitution, while those Virginia presidents held more state-centered views, so he faced political headwinds from them for about 24 years.

### [Martin Luther King Jr.](../articles/people-who-shaped-america/martin-luther-king-jr.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| King was a widely admired, broadly popular figure in his own lifetime. | In an August 1966 Gallup survey, 63% of Americans viewed King unfavorably, 44% highly so; by August 2011 Gallup found 94% favorable. |

**Why it matters:** Remembering King only as a unifying icon hides how controversial his demands for desegregation, voting rights, economic justice and an end to the Vietnam War were when he made them.

- **Q:** Why did King's 1963 Birmingham campaign matter for national civil rights law?
  **A:** Televised images of police violence against demonstrators in May 1963 pressured the Kennedy administration, which then proposed major civil rights legislation; President Johnson signed a civil rights law the following year.
- **Q:** How did King's standing with the public and the federal government change after 1965, and why?
  **A:** His Chicago campaign against housing discrimination brought no significant gains, and his 1967 Riverside Church speech against the Vietnam War cost him influence on federal racial policy and drew more FBI scrutiny; by August 1966 a Gallup poll found 63% of Americans viewed him unfavorably.
- **Q:** Why did the Justice Department in 2000 reject the conspiracy finding of the 1999 King v. Jowers civil verdict?
  **A:** It found the allegations of Loyd Jowers and Donald Wilson not credible because both men contradicted themselves and nothing reliable corroborated them, and it judged that the civil trial rested heavily on hearsay, so it saw no reason to disturb the 1969 finding that James Earl Ray killed King.

### [Sojourner Truth](../articles/people-who-shaped-america/sojourner-truth.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| At Akron in 1851 Sojourner Truth gave a speech in Southern dialect, repeating "Ain't I a woman?" and telling how she had borne thirteen children, most sold into slavery. | The refrain, the dialect and the thirteen children come from Frances Dana Gage's version published in 1863, twelve years later. Marius Robinson's account, printed within weeks of the convention, has no such refrain; Truth, raised in New York speaking Dutch, never lived in the South, and she had five children. |

**Why it matters:** Gage's dramatic version was reprinted widely, entered later editions of Truth's own Narrative and became the text schoolbooks quote, so the more reliable 1851 record was long overlooked.

- **Q:** Why do historians treat Marius Robinson's 1851 account of the Akron speech as more reliable than Frances Gage's 1863 version?
  **A:** Robinson published his within weeks of the convention and is believed to have reviewed it with Truth, while Gage wrote twelve years later without Truth's review and put a Northern-born, Dutch-speaking woman's words into Southern dialect.
- **Q:** How did New York law help Truth recover her son Peter in 1828?
  **A:** New York's gradual-emancipation laws forbade selling an enslaved New Yorker out of state in a way that denied the person the benefits of those laws, so his sale to Alabama was illegal and she could sue for his return, which she won.
- **Q:** How did Truth fund her work during the Civil War years?
  **A:** She sold copies of her Narrative and cartes de visite of herself at lectures and by mail, registering a copyright in the photographs in 1864 so that the income came to her.

### [Susan B. Anthony](../articles/people-who-shaped-america/susan-b-anthony.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Susan B. Anthony was the first woman to vote in the United States, casting a lone ballot in Rochester in 1872. | Some New Jersey women voted from 1776 until an 1807 law limited the vote to free white men, and women in Wyoming Territory (1869) and Utah Territory (1870) were enfranchised before her vote. In Rochester she registered with her three sisters and roughly ten other women, and warrants were issued for all of them, though only she was tried. |

**Why it matters:** Her arrest, trial and refusal to pay the fine made her the public face of the 1872 protest, so the other Rochester voters and the earlier history of women voting dropped out of the popular story.

- **Q:** Why could Anthony not take her 1873 conviction to the Supreme Court?
  **A:** The law gave her no right of appeal, and because Justice Hunt fined her but did not jail her, she could not seek a writ of habeas corpus either; she refused to pay the $100 fine, and it was never collected.
- **Q:** How did the Fifteenth Amendment divide Anthony from allies such as Frederick Douglass?
  **A:** It barred racial but not sex discrimination in voting; Douglass supported it, while Anthony and Stanton opposed an amendment that left women out and formed the National Woman Suffrage Association to seek a federal woman suffrage amendment, at times using racist arguments that historians criticize.
- **Q:** Why is the Nineteenth Amendment often called the Susan B. Anthony Amendment?
  **A:** It grew out of the woman suffrage amendment that Senator Aaron Sargent introduced in her name in 1878, after her National Woman Suffrage Association returned to petitioning Congress, and which she campaigned for until her death; it was ratified in 1920, fourteen years after she died.

## presidents-and-founders

### [Abraham Lincoln](../articles/presidents-and-founders/abraham-lincoln.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Lincoln dashed off the Gettysburg Address on the back of an envelope on the train to Gettysburg. | Lincoln worked on the speech in the weeks before November 19, 1863; a Library of Congress copy begins in ink on Executive Mansion stationery written in Washington, and the drafts are in steady handwriting unlike a moving train's. |

**Why it matters:** The envelope story turns a carefully revised speech into a lucky improvisation and hides how deliberately Lincoln shaped his public words.

- **Q:** Why did Lincoln win the presidency in 1860 with less than 40 percent of the popular vote?
  **A:** Four major candidates split the vote; Lincoln carried every free state, which gave him 180 of 303 electoral votes, a clear Electoral College majority, even though he was not on the ballot in much of the South.
- **Q:** How did the actions of enslaved people shape Lincoln's emancipation policy, according to historians such as Ira Berlin?
  **A:** By fleeing to Union lines and offering labor and military service, enslaved people forced Union officers, Congress and the president to decide their status; Lincoln, who had first favored gradual, compensated emancipation and colonization, moved toward the Emancipation Proclamation as that pressure grew.
- **Q:** Why do historians debate Lincoln's title of "Great Emancipator"?
  **A:** Some see his leadership as the decisive force that ended slavery, while others argue he was a reluctant emancipator and that enslaved people were the prime movers; the debate is about how to weigh presidential leadership against the agency of the enslaved.

### [Andrew Jackson](../articles/presidents-and-founders/andrew-jackson.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| When the Supreme Court ruled for the Cherokee missionaries in Worcester v. Georgia, Jackson retorted that John Marshall had made his decision and could enforce it himself. | The line first appears in Horace Greeley's 1864 book The American Conflict, more than thirty years later and secondhand; historians judge it most likely apocryphal. Jackson's own letter of April 7, 1832, said only that the decision had fallen "still born", and because the Court adjourned without ever ordering federal enforcement, he was never formally called on to act. |

**Why it matters:** The invented retort neatly sums up Jackson's real refusal to protect Cherokee treaty rights, so it has been repeated as if it were his words.

- **Q:** Why did Jackson and his supporters call the 1824 election a "corrupt bargain"?
  **A:** Jackson led the popular and electoral vote but no candidate had an electoral majority, so the House chose John Quincy Adams with Henry Clay's support, and Adams then made Clay secretary of state.
- **Q:** How did Jackson's Bank veto message challenge the Supreme Court's role in interpreting the Constitution?
  **A:** He argued that although McCulloch v. Maryland had upheld a national bank, Congress and the president were not bound by the Court's view when acting in their own roles; each branch had to judge constitutionality for itself.
- **Q:** Why did the Senate censure Jackson in 1834, and what happened to the censure?
  **A:** After vetoing the Bank's recharter he fired a treasury secretary who would not remove federal deposits and had Roger Taney move them to state banks; the Senate censured him 26 to 20 for assuming powers not given by law, and a Democratic majority expunged the censure in January 1837.
- **Q:** How did Jacksonian democracy expand political participation for some Americans while narrowing it for others?
  **A:** States dropped property requirements so more poor white men could vote, but by 1839 almost all states limited Black voting, women remained excluded, and Jackson's removal policy drove nearly 50,000 Native people from their homelands.

### [Benjamin Franklin](../articles/presidents-and-founders/benjamin-franklin.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Benjamin Franklin discovered electricity when lightning struck his kite. | Electrical forces had been recognized for more than a thousand years, and Franklin's kite was not hit by lightning; it gathered the storm's ambient charge, showing that lightning is electrical. |

**Why it matters:** The myth turns a careful (and dangerous) test of an idea into a lucky accident, and hides that Dalibard in France had demonstrated the same point about a month earlier.

- **Q:** How did Franklin's diplomacy in France help the United States win independence?
  **A:** As a commissioner from 1776 he worked to gain French support; in 1778 France recognized American independence and allied with the United States, and in 1779 he became the first American minister received by a foreign government.
- **Q:** Why is Franklin's record on slavery described as a change over his lifetime rather than a single position?
  **A:** For about fifty years his household included enslaved people, including Peter, Jemima, King and Othello, but late in life he became president of the Pennsylvania abolition society and his last public act was its 1790 petition asking Congress to act against slavery.
- **Q:** What did the 1752 kite experiment actually show, and why was it not a "discovery of electricity"?
  **A:** It showed that lightning is electrical by collecting a storm's ambient charge; electricity itself had been known for centuries, and the kite was not struck by lightning.
- **Q:** Did Franklin formally propose the turkey as the national bird?
  **A:** No. In an unsent 1784 letter to his daughter mocking the eagle on the Society of the Cincinnati badge, he disparaged the eagle and praised the turkey, but he made no formal proposal.

### [Franklin D. Roosevelt](../articles/presidents-and-founders/franklin-d-roosevelt.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Americans never knew that Roosevelt had polio, because he hid it from the public. | His illness was public knowledge; he founded the Georgia Warm Springs Foundation in 1927 and launched the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis in 1938. What was minimized was its extent. Few Americans understood that he could not walk unaided, because he took care to be seen standing or walking with support and press photographers generally lowered their cameras. |

**Why it matters:** The "secret" version erases both the public polio work he led and how he shaped buildings, cars and wheelchairs around his disability, which the National Park Service now presents as part of disability history.

- **Q:** Why does the Miller Center argue that the New Deal did not by itself end the Great Depression?
  **A:** Unemployment was still above 19 percent in 1939 and in double digits in 1940, and a sharp recession in 1937–38 followed spending cuts aimed at balancing the budget; in Leuchtenburg's view it was wartime mobilization for World War II that finally ended the slump.
- **Q:** How did the New Deal both expand and limit economic security for different Americans?
  **A:** It created old-age and unemployment insurance, union bargaining rights and a minimum wage, but Social Security left out agricultural and domestic workers, many of them African American or Mexican American, and Black Americans and women received far fewer benefits than white men.
- **Q:** How did A. Philip Randolph's threatened march lead to Executive Order 8802?
  **A:** Randolph and other Black leaders demanded an order against job discrimination in defense work and threatened to bring tens of thousands of marchers to Washington; Roosevelt issued the order on June 25, 1941, creating the Committee on Fair Employment Practice, and Randolph called off the march.
- **Q:** What is the core disagreement among historians about Roosevelt's response to the Holocaust?
  **A:** Critics such as David Wyman argue that political expediency led him to abandon European Jews until it was too late, while Richard Breitman and Allan Lichtman acknowledge early failures but argue that he was the first president to intervene, at least part of the time, for Jews abroad, notably through the War Refugee Board created in January 1944, which is credited with saving at least 200,000 Jews.

### [George Washington](../articles/presidents-and-founders/george-washington.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| George Washington wore wooden false teeth. | Mount Vernon finds no wood in any of his dentures; they were made of human teeth, ivory, metal and probably cow and horse teeth, and staining could make them look like wood. |

**Why it matters:** The myth hides a harder story, since some of the human teeth available to him may have come from enslaved people at Mount Vernon.

- **Q:** Why did Washington's will free only 123 of the 317 enslaved people living at Mount Vernon in 1799?
  **A:** Only 123 belonged to him; 153 were "dower" slaves from the estate of Martha Washington's first husband, which neither spouse could legally free and which passed to the Custis grandchildren, and 40 more were rented from a neighbor.
- **Q:** How did the Western Confederacy's victory over Arthur St. Clair in 1791 change Washington's frontier policy?
  **A:** After more than 630 of St. Clair's roughly 930 soldiers were killed, Washington and Congress built a larger, professionally trained Legion of the United States under Anthony Wayne, which won at Fallen Timbers in 1794 and forced the 1795 Treaty of Greenville cessions.
- **Q:** Why do historians disagree about whether Washington stood above party politics?
  **A:** He warned in his Farewell Address against the spirit of party and never joined a party, yet he sided with Hamilton against Jefferson and, in Encyclopedia Virginia's account, led a Federalist faction that voters rejected in 1800.

### [Herbert Hoover](../articles/presidents-and-founders/herbert-hoover.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Herbert Hoover did nothing to fight the Great Depression. | Hoover called business and labor conferences in November 1929, pressed for public works, created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation in 1932 and signed a law providing $2 billion for public works and $300 million for state relief; what he refused was large-scale direct federal relief to the unemployed. |

**Why it matters:** Unemployment kept rising throughout his term, and shantytowns nicknamed "Hoovervilles," the rout of the Bonus Army and the contrast with Roosevelt's New Deal fixed an image of an indifferent president that historians have since revised.

- **Q:** How did Hoover's World War I relief work prepare the way for his presidency?
  **A:** Organizing the Commission for Relief in Belgium, which fed more than nine million Belgian and French civilians, and then the U.S. Food Administration and the American Relief Administration made him famous as a humanitarian and organizer, which led to his appointment as commerce secretary and, after he ran the 1927 flood relief as a "master of emergencies," to the 1928 Republican nomination.
- **Q:** Why is it inaccurate to say Hoover did nothing about the Depression, and what do historians still criticize?
  **A:** He organized wage pledges, pushed public works, created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation and approved $2 billion for public works and $300 million for state relief. Historians still fault him for refusing large-scale direct relief to the unemployed, for limited federal spending and for misjudging the depth of the crisis.
- **Q:** Why do economists such as Lee Ohanian argue that one of Hoover's interventions made the Depression worse?
  **A:** Hoover persuaded big industrial firms to keep wages up after the 1929 crash; as prices fell, real wages rose, and Ohanian argues firms cut employment instead, a labor-market failure he estimates reduced output and hours worked by about 20 percent.
- **Q:** What did the 1927 flood reveal about the treatment of Black Mississippians, and how did Hoover respond?
  **A:** In Greenville, Black families were held in levee camps under National Guard watch and about 500 Black men were made to repair the levee without pay under threat of losing Red Cross food. Hoover set up a Colored Advisory Committee under Robert Moton, but when it delivered a harsh report he took no action.

### [James Madison](../articles/presidents-and-founders/james-madison.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| James Madison single-handedly wrote the U.S. Constitution. | Madison's Virginia Plan framed the convention's debates, but delegates rejected key parts of it, such as a congressional veto over state laws, and in 1834 Madison himself rejected the title of writer of the Constitution, calling it the work of many heads and many hands. |

**Why it matters:** The nickname "Father of the Constitution," his detailed notes of the convention and his long life as its last surviving framer made one man stand in for a collective, negotiated document.

- **Q:** Why did Madison, who had argued a bill of rights was unnecessary, introduce amendments in 1789?
  **A:** He had argued that the federal government could exercise only the powers the Constitution listed, but he came to see that voters valued written protections and that adopting them could head off opponents seeking larger changes, so he introduced amendments on June 8, 1789, and pressed Congress to pass them.
- **Q:** How did the Virginia Resolutions of 1798 later come back to trouble Madison?
  **A:** The resolutions said states could interpose against unconstitutional federal acts such as the Alien and Sedition Acts; in the late 1820s and 1830s nullifiers in South Carolina and elsewhere cited them, and Madison objected that his arguments about interposition were being used to justify nullification.
- **Q:** Why did Madison sign a national bank charter in 1816 after opposing Hamilton's bank in the 1790s?
  **A:** After the first national bank's charter lapsed, financing the War of 1812 without its loans and credit proved hard, and Madison concluded that long acceptance by Congress and the public had settled the bank's constitutionality by precedent.
- **Q:** How did slavery shape Madison's life despite his statements against it?
  **A:** Born into the largest slaveholding family in Orange County, he called slavery a violation of republican principles but offered no realistic plan to end it beyond colonization, defended the three-fifths formula, sold sixteen people in 1819, and freed no one in his will.

### [Theodore Roosevelt](../articles/presidents-and-founders/theodore-roosevelt.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Theodore Roosevelt created the National Park Service. | The National Park Service was established in 1916, seven years after Roosevelt left office. What he did do was help create 23 of the 35 sites the new agency inherited, proclaim 18 national monuments under the Antiquities Act, and create the U.S. Forest Service in 1905. |

**Why it matters:** Crediting him with the agency blurs the difference between the parks and monuments he set aside and the bureau that later managed them, and hides the separate national-forest system he built.

- **Q:** How did Roosevelt end the 1902 anthracite coal strike, and how did that episode give his domestic program its name?
  **A:** When the mine owners refused to negotiate, he threatened to have troops seize and run the mines as a federal operation; both sides then agreed to accept the recommendations of a commission. He called the settlement a "square deal," and the phrase became the name of his whole domestic program.
- **Q:** Why is it inaccurate to say that Roosevelt founded the National Park Service, even though he shaped the park system?
  **A:** The National Park Service was created in 1916, seven years after he left office. He shaped the system by helping create 23 of the 35 sites the agency inherited and by proclaiming 18 national monuments under the 1906 Antiquities Act.
- **Q:** Why do historians describe Roosevelt's record on race as divided or mixed?
  **A:** He invited Booker T. Washington to dine at the White House and appointed some Black Southerners to office, yet he discharged 167 Black soldiers after the Brownsville shooting without a trial, did little to stop Black disfranchisement, and, as Gary Gerstle argues, combined a civic nationalism open to European immigrants with a racial nationalism that excluded nonwhite Americans.

### [Thomas Jefferson](../articles/presidents-and-founders/thomas-jefferson.md) — Person

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Thomas Jefferson wrote that the government is best which governs least. | The Thomas Jefferson Foundation finds no such line in his writings; the earliest known printing of the wording is John L. O'Sullivan's 1837 introduction to the Democratic Review, and the earliest known attribution to Jefferson dates only from 1853. |

**Why it matters:** The phrase was long associated with the political heirs of Jefferson's Democratic-Republican party, so it drifted onto the party's founder, and a familiar name makes a slogan sound authoritative.

- **Q:** Why did the House of Representatives, not the voters or the electors, decide the presidential election of 1800?
  **A:** Electors then cast two undifferentiated votes, so Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr tied at 73 electoral votes each; the Federalist-controlled House deadlocked for 35 ballots before choosing Jefferson on the 36th, on February 17, 1801.
- **Q:** How did the Louisiana Purchase test Jefferson's own reading of the Constitution?
  **A:** Jefferson doubted that the Constitution gave the president power to acquire territory and considered an amendment, but public support and the land's value led him to set aside his strict reading, a choice that helped build the principle of implied federal powers.
- **Q:** How did Jefferson's 1803 letter to William Henry Harrison propose to obtain Native land?
  **A:** He proposed government trading houses that would draw Native leaders into debt, which they would pay off by ceding land, and he expected Native nations either to be absorbed as citizens or to move west of the Mississippi.

## regions-cities-and-landmarks

### [Chicago](../articles/regions-cities-and-landmarks/chicago.md) — City

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lantern and started the Great Chicago Fire of 1871. | The fire did begin in the barn of Catherine and Patrick O'Leary, but the police and fire board's 1871 inquiry said it was unable to determine the cause, found the household asleep when the fire started, and in 1997 the City Council formally exonerated Mrs. O'Leary and her cow. |

**Why it matters:** An illustrated weekly named her the likely culprit within three weeks of the fire, and the vivid cow-and-lantern image became a local legend that outlived the inconclusive official record.

- **Q:** Why did Chicago's location make it a natural transportation hub?
  **A:** It sits at a low divide between the Great Lakes and the Mississippi drainage, which Jolliet noticed in 1673; once the Erie Canal linked the Great Lakes to the Atlantic in 1825 and the Illinois & Michigan Canal and railroads arrived in 1848, Chicago connected eastern markets to the western interior, and by 1856 ten trunk railroads ended there.
- **Q:** How did the land Chicago stands on pass out of Native hands?
  **A:** Through a series of treaties, beginning with a 1795 tract around the river's mouth and continuing in 1816, 1829 and 1832; the 1833 Treaty of Chicago forced the Potawatomi to cede their last Illinois and Wisconsin lands and move west of the Mississippi, a removal the Newberry's Indigenous Chicago project describes as coercive and unequal.
- **Q:** Why was the Chicago River reversed in 1900?
  **A:** The Sanitary and Ship Canal was built for shipping and water quality; reversing the river sent the city's sewage south toward the Illinois River instead of into Lake Michigan, the source of its drinking water.
- **Q:** How did the Great Migration change Chicago, and what limits did Black migrants face?
  **A:** The Black population grew from about 40,000 in 1910 to 278,000 in 1940 and 813,000 in 1960 as southerners came for wartime industrial jobs, encouraged by the Chicago Defender; but most were confined to an overcrowded, overpriced South Side Black Belt, faced job discrimination and union exclusion, and in 1919 suffered a riot that killed 23 Black Chicagoans.

### [Detroit](../articles/regions-cities-and-landmarks/detroit.md) — City

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Detroit's decline began with the 1967 uprising, which drove people and jobs out of a thriving city. | Historian Thomas Sugrue notes that Detroit lost more than 130,000 manufacturing jobs between 1948 and 1967, as automakers moved plants to suburbs, small towns and the Sunbelt and automated production, and that white flight and disinvestment were already under way before 1967; white departures did accelerate sharply afterward. |

**Why it matters:** A single dramatic event is easier to remember than decades of gradual industrial and metropolitan change, and blaming the uprising draws attention away from corporate decisions and housing segregation that shaped the city earlier.

- **Q:** Why did the French found a settlement at Detroit in 1701, and who already lived there?
  **A:** Cadillac persuaded French officials to back a settlement on the Detroit River to forestall British expansion and build a colony supported by the region's soil and fur trade; the land had long been home to Anishinaabe peoples, and Odawa, Bodewadmi (Potawatomi) and Ojibwe families were encouraged to settle near the fort.
- **Q:** How did the automobile industry change Detroit's size and workforce?
  **A:** Ford's Model T, the moving assembly line at Highland Park in 1913 and the five-dollar day in 1914 drew workers from many places; Detroit grew from the nation's 13th-largest city in 1903 to its fifth-largest by 1950, with nearly two million people.
- **Q:** How did World War II both open opportunities for and constrain Black Detroiters?
  **A:** War production opened factory jobs previously closed to Black workers and drew about 350,000 migrants, but restrictive covenants confined Black residents to overcrowded areas such as Black Bottom and Paradise Valley, and housing conflict and racial tension erupted in the 1943 riot.
- **Q:** Why does Thomas Sugrue argue that the 1967 uprising did not begin Detroit's decline?
  **A:** Because the city had already lost more than 130,000 manufacturing jobs between 1948 and 1967 to decentralization and automation, with white flight and disinvestment under way, so the uprising came after, not before, the structural decline.

### [Gatun Locks](../articles/regions-cities-and-landmarks/gatun-locks.md) — Landmark

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Giant pumps lift ships up through the Panama Canal's locks. | No pumps are used; valves let fresh water from Gatun Lake flow down by gravity through culverts in the lock walls and up through holes in the chamber floors, and to lower a ship the chamber is drained downward through the same culverts. |

**Why it matters:** The scale of the lift suggests heavy machinery, but the system's real limit is the supply of lake water, about 52 million gallons for each ship that transits.

- **Q:** Why do the Gatun Locks need no pumps to raise a ship?
  **A:** Gatun Lake sits about 85 feet above sea level, so opening valves lets lake water flow down by gravity through 18-foot culverts and up through 100 floor holes per chamber, filling a chamber in about eight minutes; emptying it reverses the process.
- **Q:** Why does every transit through the Gatun Locks depend on Gatun Lake?
  **A:** The lake supplies the water that fills each chamber, roughly 26.7 million gallons per filling and about 52 million gallons of fresh water per ship, while the Gatun Dam that holds the lake also generated electricity for canal operations, including the gates.
- **Q:** Why did the United States begin, and then stop, a third set of locks near Gatun?
  **A:** By 1939 ships, especially warships, were growing too wide for the 110-foot chambers and officials feared one act of sabotage could close the canal, so Congress authorized larger locks; excavation ran from 1940 until Secretary of War Stimson ended it on May 23, 1942, as wartime money shifted elsewhere.
- **Q:** Who did much of the dangerous work of building the lock gates?
  **A:** Rivet gangs made up mostly of West Indian workers, who under the canal's segregated gold-and-silver-roll system did some of the riskiest jobs, drove more than six million rivets into the canal's hollow steel gates.

### [Hoover Dam](../articles/regions-cities-and-landmarks/hoover-dam.md) — Landmark

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Workers who died building Hoover Dam are entombed in its concrete. | The Bureau of Reclamation states that no one is buried in the dam; concrete was placed in five-foot-high blocks that rose gradually as crews packed each layer, which made an unnoticed burial virtually impossible. The official toll counts 96 industrial deaths at the site. |

**Why it matters:** The dam's sheer mass and its real death toll make the legend feel plausible, and confusion between the official 96 and a broader count of 112 deaths keeps the story of "missing" men alive.

- **Q:** Why was the dam called Boulder Dam for fourteen years, and how was the Hoover name restored?
  **A:** Wilbur announced the Hoover name in 1930 to honor Hoover's role in the Colorado River Compact, but after Hoover's reputation fell during the Depression, Interior Secretary Harold Ickes ordered it renamed Boulder Dam in May 1933, arguing a structure should not be named for a sitting president and that it was already known by that name; a 1947 congressional resolution restored "Hoover Dam."
- **Q:** Why do counts of construction deaths at Hoover Dam differ (96 versus 112)?
  **A:** The official 96 counts only industrial fatalities at the site, such as drownings, blasts and falling rock; the larger figure adds deaths from before the dam was authorized, like the 1922 surveyors, and deaths from causes such as heat, pneumonia or heart trouble.
- **Q:** How did the dam's builders keep its enormous mass of concrete from cracking as it cured?
  **A:** They poured it as interlocking vertical columns rather than one block and embedded about 582 miles of steel pipe through which river water and then refrigerated water circulated to carry off the heat of curing, before grouting the joints.
- **Q:** How did racial discrimination shape who built the dam?
  **A:** After Las Vegas's Colored Citizens Labor and Protective Association protested in 1931 that none of the first thousand workers were Black, only 24 African Americans had been hired by 1933; they were barred from living in Boulder City, assigned to the hot Arizona gravel pits and bused in on segregated buses.

### [New Orleans](../articles/regions-cities-and-landmarks/new-orleans.md) — City

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| All of New Orleans lies below sea level. | A 2005 USGS statement to Congress put more than half of the city below sea level, some areas by as much as 10 feet, while the original city sat on natural river levees about 5 to 10 feet above sea level, and those old levees and sandy ridges remain above it. |

**Why it matters:** Katrina's floodwaters covering most of the city made the "bowl" image memorable, but the oldest neighborhoods stand on the high ground the founders chose, and the low areas are largely drained swamp that has sunk since the early 1900s.

- **Q:** Why did the French choose the site of New Orleans, and what Indigenous knowledge shaped that choice?
  **A:** Bienville was told to build a city near the Mississippi's mouth to keep British and Spanish rivals from moving up the river; Native people had told his brother Iberville's 1699 expedition of a ridge (now Esplanade Ridge) linking the river to Bayou St. John and Lake Pontchartrain, which made the spot strategically valuable.
- **Q:** Why did Jefferson want New Orleans, and how did that lead to the Louisiana Purchase?
  **A:** Its port was the outlet for produce from more than a third of the country, and Spain's refusal in 1802 to let Americans deposit goods there angered westerners; Jefferson told Livingston to buy the city, and the negotiations ended in the April 30, 1803 treaty for all of Louisiana.
- **Q:** How did events in New Orleans shape Reconstruction and the fight against segregation?
  **A:** The 1866 Mechanics' Institute massacre helped bring about Radical Reconstruction and stronger congressional action, including the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship guarantees; in 1891 the city's Comité des Citoyens organized and funded the legal challenge to the Separate Car Law that became Plessy v. Ferguson.
- **Q:** Why did so much of the city flood in 2005 when its oldest districts stayed relatively high?
  **A:** Most of the city was built on drained swamp that has subsided since the early 1900s, creating a bowl below sea level; when levees separating the city from Lake Pontchartrain broke during Katrina, at least 80 percent of New Orleans flooded, while the original city on natural levees stood 5 to 10 feet above sea level.

### [The West](../articles/regions-cities-and-landmarks/the-west.md) — Region

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The West was an empty wilderness of free land waiting for American settlers. | Native nations controlled most of the continent west of the Mississippi deep into the nineteenth century, and the Mexican Cession brought Mexican citizens whose property the 1848 treaty promised to respect; the land became available to settlers through wars, treaties, removal and allotment. |

**Why it matters:** Turner's emphasis on free land, dime novels and Wild West shows told the story from the settlers' side, leaving out the peoples whose homelands were taken.

- **Q:** Why did Frederick Jackson Turner treat 1890 as a turning point, and how did Patricia Nelson Limerick challenge his view?
  **A:** Turner took the 1890 census's finding that there was no longer a frontier line as the end of the westward advance that he believed had shaped American individualism and democracy; Limerick rejected the "closing frontier" model and described the West instead as a place of continuing conquest over land, resources and peoples, and wrote Native, Mexican, Chinese, Black and women's histories into the story.
- **Q:** Why did John Wesley Powell argue that the 160-acre homestead did not suit much of the West?
  **A:** Beyond about the 100th meridian, average rainfall is less than twenty inches a year, too little for most crops, so Powell recommended irrigation-based farming and ranching rather than small dry farms on the eastern model.
- **Q:** How did the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo shape the West, and what promise was weakened before ratification?
  **A:** Mexico ceded 55 percent of its territory, including present-day California, Nevada, Utah, New Mexico, most of Arizona and Colorado, for $15 million; the Senate struck Article X, which guaranteed protection of Mexican land grants, though Article VIII still promised to respect Mexicans' property.
- **Q:** Why does the federal government own so much more land in the West than in the East?
  **A:** The Congressional Research Service points partly to early treaties, land settlement laws and settlement patterns, along with the reservation of western parks and other reserves from 1872 on, and since 1976 federal law has generally kept the remaining public domain in federal hands; by 2024 about 46 percent of the 11 contiguous western states and 61 percent of Alaska was federal.

## rights-and-law

### [Dred Scott v. Sandford](../articles/rights-and-law/dred-scott-v-sandford.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Dred Scott v. Sandford was the first time the Supreme Court struck down an act of Congress. | The first was Marbury v. Madison in 1803; Dred Scott was the first time since then that the Court invalidated a federal statute, the Missouri Compromise's restriction on slavery. |

**Why it matters:** Remembering it as the second use of judicial review against Congress, after a 54-year gap, shows how unusual and consequential the Court's choice to rule on territorial slavery was.

- **Q:** Why did Dred Scott's case end up in federal court after he lost in the Missouri Supreme Court?
  **A:** Scott sued John F. A. Sanford, Irene Emerson's brother and the executor of John Emerson's estate, who was a citizen of New York; because the parties lived in different states, Scott could sue under the federal courts' diversity jurisdiction, which raised the question of whether he was a citizen at all.
- **Q:** How did the Court's ruling on citizenship let it avoid deciding Scott's case on the merits, and why did it go further anyway?
  **A:** Holding that Scott was not a citizen meant the federal courts lacked jurisdiction and the case had to be dismissed; but after re-argument the majority also ruled that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories, partly because dissenting justices planned to argue that Congress had that power.
- **Q:** How did the decision deepen the sectional crisis before the Civil War?
  **A:** It made the Republican goal of keeping slavery out of the territories unconstitutional, which Republicans saw as proof of the Slave Power's reach, and it undercut Stephen Douglas's popular sovereignty, prompting his Freeport Doctrine, which alienated Southern Democrats and helped split the Democratic Party.
- **Q:** Which constitutional change reversed the decision's ruling on citizenship?
  **A:** The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, whose first sentence made all persons born or naturalized in the United States citizens, following the Civil Rights Act of 1866.

### [Due process](../articles/rights-and-law/due-process.md) — Right

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Due process is only about criminal trials. | The clauses protect against any government deprivation of life, liberty, or property, including civil and administrative actions such as cutting off government benefits (Goldberg v. Kelly, 1970; Mathews v. Eldridge, 1976), and courts weigh how much process each situation requires. |

**Why it matters:** The most familiar procedural rights, such as indictment, jury trial and counsel, sit beside due process in the Fifth and Sixth Amendments, so people assume the clause belongs only to the courtroom.

- **Q:** Why does the Constitution contain two Due Process Clauses?
  **A:** The Fifth Amendment's clause was held in Barron v. Baltimore (1833) to bind only the federal government, so after the Civil War the Fourteenth Amendment (ratified 1868) added a parallel clause forbidding the states to deprive any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.
- **Q:** How does a court decide how much process is "due" when the government ends someone's benefits?
  **A:** Under Mathews v. Eldridge (1976) it balances three factors, the private interest affected, the risk of an erroneous deprivation under the procedures used, and the government's interest, against a baseline of notice and an opportunity to be heard.
- **Q:** Why is substantive due process more controversial than procedural due process or incorporation?
  **A:** Critics argue the clause's text guarantees lawful procedure, not limits on what laws may say, and note the doctrine's roots in Dred Scott and Lochner; defenders answer that more than a century of precedent and the Ninth Amendment support protecting unenumerated rights, and that the real question is how judges should identify them.

### [Equal protection](../articles/rights-and-law/equal-protection.md) — Right

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Equal Protection Clause forbids government from ever treating people differently. | Governments classify people constantly, by age, income, or occupation, and most such lines stand if they are "rationally related to a legitimate state interest"; only suspect classifications such as race, or laws burdening fundamental rights, face the demanding test of strict scrutiny. |

**Why it matters:** Equal protection requires equal treatment of those who are "similarly situated," not identical treatment of everyone, so the real question a court asks is which differences a law may lawfully draw on.

- **Q:** Why did the framers of the Fourteenth Amendment write an equal protection guarantee into the Constitution rather than rely on ordinary legislation?
  **A:** They wanted to give civil rights measures such as the Civil Rights Act of 1866 a constitutional footing that a later simple majority in Congress could not repeal, so that the equal legal standing of formerly enslaved people could not be undone by shifting politics.
- **Q:** How can two laws that both classify people be judged so differently under the Equal Protection Clause?
  **A:** Courts apply different levels of review depending on the classification. Most laws need only a rational relationship to a legitimate interest, but a law that sorts people by race (a suspect classification) or burdens a fundamental right must survive strict scrutiny, meaning it must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest.

### [Fourteenth Amendment](../articles/rights-and-law/fourteenth-amendment.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| By making women citizens, the Fourteenth Amendment gave them the right to vote. | Section 2 penalized states only for denying the vote to male citizens, and in Minor v. Happersett (1875) the Supreme Court held that citizenship did not carry the right to vote; women won a constitutional guarantee only with the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920. |

**Why it matters:** Assuming citizenship and voting rights always went together hides how long women, and Native Americans after the 1924 citizenship act, were citizens who could still be kept from the polls.

- **Q:** What problems was the amendment meant to solve after the Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery?
  **A:** Southern states were passing Black Codes to limit freed people's rights while white southerners terrorized them, and Dred Scott's rule still denied Black Americans citizenship; the amendment made all persons born or naturalized in the country citizens, barred states from denying due process or equal protection, and gave Congress power to enforce those guarantees.
- **Q:** How did the Slaughter-House Cases change what the amendment meant in practice?
  **A:** The Court held that the Privileges or Immunities Clause protected only rights of national citizenship, leaving most rights to the states and turning the clause into a practical nullity, so later protection of Bill of Rights guarantees against the states came instead through the Due Process Clause, case by case.
- **Q:** Why were former Confederate states required to ratify the amendment?
  **A:** White southerners rejected it almost unanimously in 1866 and 1867; after Northern voters backed Republicans in the 1866 elections, Congress made ratification a condition for restoring a state's representation, and Southern ratifications in 1868 completed the three-fourths.
- **Q:** Who was left out of the citizenship clause at first, and how did that change?
  **A:** Native people under tribal rather than U.S. authority were excluded, and Elk v. Wilkins (1884) held that Native Americans were not U.S. citizens even after leaving their nations; Congress made all Native Americans citizens only with the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924.

### [Marbury v. Madison](../articles/rights-and-law/marbury-v-madison.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Marbury v. Madison was celebrated from the start and was constantly cited throughout the 1800s as the foundation of judicial review. | Courts did cite the case in the nineteenth century, but mostly on jurisdiction, mandamus and executive appointments; a 2012 study found that only about 8% of the Supreme Court's nineteenth-century citations invoked it for judicial review, and the case was recast as the fountainhead of that power only around the turn of the twentieth century. |

**Why it matters:** Reading the case's modern fame back into 1803 hides how its reputation was built later, by commentators, the 1903 centennial and Cooper v. Aaron in 1958, and makes the early history of judicial power look more settled than it was.

- **Q:** Why did Marbury lose his case even though the Court agreed he was entitled to his commission?
  **A:** Marshall held that the part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 letting the Court issue a writ of mandamus in a case like Marbury's gave the Court original jurisdiction that Article III did not allow, so that provision was void and the Court had no power to issue the writ; the rule was discharged.
- **Q:** How did the Federalist and Republican fight over the courts in 1801–1802 shape the timing and politics of the case?
  **A:** The lame-duck Federalists created new judgeships and Adams filled offices at the last minute; the Republican Congress then repealed the 1801 act and, through the Judiciary Act of 1802, cancelled the Court's June 1802 term, so the Court did not hear Marbury's motion until February 1803, while Madison declined even to appear.
- **Q:** How do historians explain why Marbury came to be seen as the founding case of judicial review?
  **A:** One view stresses Marshall's skill in avoiding a confrontation with Jefferson while claiming a larger power for the Court; another, backed by citation studies, argues that the case was mostly cited for other points in the 1800s and was turned into the canonical source of judicial review by later commentators, the 1903 centennial and Cooper v. Aaron.

### [McCulloch v. Maryland](../articles/rights-and-law/mcculloch-v-maryland.md) — CourtCase

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| McCulloch v. Maryland gave Congress unlimited power to pass any law it considers useful. | Marshall's opinion restated that the national government is one of enumerated powers; it allowed only means clearly suited to a legitimate end within the Constitution's scope, and it said the Court would have to declare void a law passed under the pretext of executing a power for objects not entrusted to the government. |

**Why it matters:** Reading the case as a blank check obscures the real argument it started, which is about how far implied means can reach, not whether limits exist at all.

- **Q:** Why did Marshall reject Maryland's argument that "necessary" in the Necessary and Proper Clause meant "absolutely necessary"?
  **A:** He said the word admits of degrees and is commonly used to mean convenient, useful or conducive to an end, and he pointed out that Article I, Section 10 uses the phrase "absolutely necessary," which showed the framers knew how to demand strict necessity when they wanted it; a narrow reading would also leave the government unable to carry out its purposes.
- **Q:** How did the idea that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy" lead the Court to strike down Maryland's tax?
  **A:** Because the national government derives its authority from the people of the whole Union and is supreme within its sphere, a single state cannot use its taxing power to burden or control the means the national government uses; allowing such a tax would let a state defeat what Congress had created.
- **Q:** How did Andrew Jackson's 1832 bank veto challenge the authority of the McCulloch decision?
  **A:** Jackson argued that each officer swears to support the Constitution as he understands it, that the Court's opinion must not control Congress or the President, and that whether the bank's particular features were necessary and proper was for the political branches to judge, so he could veto the recharter even though the Court had upheld the bank.
- **Q:** What did James Madison object to in the decision, even though he had signed the Second Bank's charter in 1816?
  **A:** He objected to the Court's broad rule of construction, warning that letting Congress treat almost anything as a means to an enumerated end could erase the limits set by enumerating its powers, and that few ratifiers would have accepted so pliant a rule.

## science-and-innovation

### [Apollo 11](../articles/science-and-innovation/apollo-11.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Apollo 11 Moon landing was staged on Earth. | Physical evidence still testifies to the landing — astronomers continue to bounce lasers off the retroreflector the crew set up, and NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed the abandoned descent stage and the astronauts' footpaths. |

**Why it matters:** The very scale of the achievement makes it easy for some to doubt, and the claim keeps circulating even though polls in the 1990s found only about 6 percent of Americans believed it.

- **Q:** Why did the United States set out to land astronauts on the Moon in the 1960s?
  **A:** Soviet firsts — Sputnik in 1957 and Yuri Gagarin's orbit in April 1961 — fed fears that America was falling behind in the Cold War, and in May 1961 Kennedy asked Congress to fund a crewed landing by the end of the decade.
- **Q:** How did the three crew members divide the work of the landing?
  **A:** Commander Neil Armstrong and lunar module pilot Buzz Aldrin flew Eagle to the surface and walked on the Moon, while command module pilot Michael Collins stayed in lunar orbit in Columbia, which carried all three home.
- **Q:** What evidence from Apollo 11 is still being used by scientists today, and what does it show?
  **A:** The laser ranging retroreflector the crew left behind still returns laser pulses from Earth-based telescopes, letting scientists measure the Earth–Moon distance to about a millimetre and study gravity and the Moon's interior; it also refutes claims that the landing was faked.
- **Q:** Why was the Moon program not universally celebrated at the time?
  **A:** Critics argued that federal money spent on space was needed for Americans facing hunger and poverty — a point the Poor People's Campaign made at the launch site — and polls showed public support for paying any cost was limited.

### [Electric telegraph](../articles/science-and-innovation/telegraph.md) — Industry

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Samuel Morse invented the electric telegraph single-handedly, and chose its famous first message himself. | Morse built on Joseph Henry's electromagnet and long-distance signaling experiments of 1831–1835, relied on Leonard Gale's advice about Henry's relay and on Alfred Vail's mechanical skill, and competed with British systems such as Charles Wheatstone's. The words "What hath God wrought" (Numbers 23:23) were suggested by Annie Ellsworth, the young daughter of a friend. |

**Why it matters:** Morse held the patent, the code bears his name, and the 1844 demonstration was a dramatic public event, while an 1845 book by his assistant Vail barely mentioned Henry's foundational work.

- **Q:** Why did Morse turn to Congress, and what did its 1843 appropriation pay for?
  **A:** He needed public money for a full-scale line after the Panic of 1837 delayed his project; Congress voted $30,000 to build an experimental line of about forty miles from Washington to Baltimore, which carried the first message in May 1844.
- **Q:** How did the transcontinental telegraph change communication with the Far West in 1861?
  **A:** Completed on October 24, 1861, it carried messages from coast to coast almost instantly at about $7 for ten words, which was faster and cheaper than horseback mail, so the Pony Express shut down.
- **Q:** Why did the Supreme Court in O'Reilly v. Morse reject Morse's eighth patent claim while upholding the rest?
  **A:** The first seven claims described his actual apparatus, but the eighth claimed the use of electromagnetism, however developed, for marking characters at any distance; the Court held it too broad because it would cover improvements Morse had not invented.
- **Q:** How did Western Union come to dominate the industry?
  **A:** Early competition among many firms gave way to regional pools and mergers; by 1866 Western Union had absorbed its major rivals, and for the rest of the century it carried over 90 percent of U.S. telegraph business.

### [Huffman Prairie Flying Field](../articles/science-and-innovation/huffman-prairie.md) — Landmark

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Wright brothers had a finished, practical airplane once they flew at Kitty Hawk in 1903. | The 1903 flights were brief, straight-line hops; the brothers learned to turn, circle, bank and stay up for half an hour only at Huffman Prairie in 1904–1905, with the redesigned Flyer III. |

**Why it matters:** The single dramatic date of December 17, 1903, is easy to remember, while two seasons of unpublicized trial and error in an Ohio pasture drew little press attention.

- **Q:** Why did the Wrights need a catapult at Huffman Prairie when they had not needed one at Kitty Hawk?
  **A:** The Ohio pasture lacked the Outer Banks' steady strong winds and sand hills, so the brothers built a falling-weight derrick to get the heavier Flyer up to flying speed.
- **Q:** How did the experiments at Huffman Prairie change the Wrights' machine from a novelty into a practical airplane?
  **A:** Through repeated flights and crashes they enlarged and moved the elevator, enlarged the rudder and gave the pilot separate control of it, so that the 1905 Flyer III could bank, circle, fly figure-eights and stay aloft for more than half an hour.
- **Q:** Why did the Wrights stop flying after October 1905, just as they had succeeded?
  **A:** They feared that public demonstrations would let others copy their design before their patent was secure and before they had a buyer, so they took the Flyer apart and did not fly again for nearly three years.

### [Lunar Laser Ranging Experiment](../articles/science-and-innovation/lunar-laser-ranging-experiment.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Telescopes on Earth can see the reflectors and other equipment the Apollo astronauts left on the Moon. | The arrays are far too small to be seen from Earth; scientists find them only by the laser light they send back, and spacecraft cameras in lunar orbit are what have pinpointed their positions to within a metre. |

**Why it matters:** Because observatories aim lasers at the reflectors every night, it is easy to assume astronomers can simply look at them.

- **Q:** How does bouncing a laser off a mirror on the Moon measure the Earth–Moon distance?
  **A:** Corner-cube prisms send light straight back toward its source, so scientists time how long a laser pulse takes to make the roughly 2.5-second round trip and convert that time into distance.
- **Q:** Why did the Apollo 11 experiment become far more precise over the following decades, even though the reflector never changed?
  **A:** The reflector needs no power, so better lasers, timing electronics and larger telescopes on Earth could keep improving the measurement — from an uncertainty of about 5 metres in August 1969 to about 2 centimetres by 1999 and about a millimetre with APOLLO.
- **Q:** What has lunar laser ranging revealed about the Moon and about gravity?
  **A:** It shows the Moon receding about 3.8 cm a year because of tides, indicates the Moon has a fluid core, measures how tides flex the Moon, and finds that the Sun pulls on Earth and Moon alike despite their different makeup, a test of Einstein's theory of gravity.
- **Q:** Why was the experiment also a Cold War race?
  **A:** Soviet scientists were also firing lasers at the Moon, and the U.S. sponsors urged American teams to get the first returns before the Soviets did.

### [Morrill Land-Grant College Act](../articles/science-and-innovation/morrill-land-grant-college-act.md) — Law

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Land-grant universities are so called because the federal government gave them the land their campuses stand on. | The act gave states land or scrip — vouchers for distant federal land — to sell; the proceeds became a permanent endowment that could not be spent on buildings, and most of that land had been taken from Native nations through cessions, many of them coerced. |

**Why it matters:** The campus story hides where the money came from — New York's scrip, sold to fund Cornell, and Dakota land ceded in Minnesota endowed colleges far from the land itself.

- **Q:** Why could the Morrill Act pass in 1862 when a similar bill had failed in 1859?
  **A:** President Buchanan vetoed the 1859 bill on fiscal and constitutional grounds; after Southern states seceded, Morrill reintroduced it with added emphasis on military instruction and Lincoln signed it on July 2, 1862.
- **Q:** How did a law about colleges in the East depend on Native land in the West?
  **A:** States without enough federal land received scrip to claim and sell Western land, and Western states selected land within their borders; researchers traced about 10.7 million acres to cessions from nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities, for which the United States paid less than $400,000, often nothing.
- **Q:** How did the 1890 Morrill Act reshape the system the 1862 act began?
  **A:** It added annual federal funding and barred racial discrimination in admissions, but let states meet that rule with separate institutions "of like character," which produced the 19 historically Black 1890 Institutions in the segregated South.

### [Wright brothers' first flight](../articles/science-and-innovation/wright-brothers-first-flight.md) — Event

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Wright brothers first flew in the town of Kitty Hawk. | The flights took off from the sand dunes at Kill Devil Hills, about four miles south of the village of Kitty Hawk; the brothers walked to Kitty Hawk afterwards to send the telegram announcing their success. |

**Why it matters:** Kitty Hawk was the nearest village — its weather station first invited the brothers and their telegram was sent from there — so its name travelled with the news.

- **Q:** Why did two Ohio bicycle makers test their aircraft on the North Carolina Outer Banks?
  **A:** They wanted steady winds, hills, soft sand and few trees; of the weather stations they wrote to, only Kitty Hawk replied, and its weatherman Bill Tate offered to host them.
- **Q:** How did the flight of December 17, 1903, differ from earlier hops and glides by other experimenters?
  **A:** Others had glided or lifted off briefly, but the Wrights' Flyer took off under its own engine power and flew in sustained flight under the pilot's complete control — the first such demonstration of a heavier-than-air machine.
- **Q:** Why did the 1903 Flyer spend twenty years outside the Smithsonian?
  **A:** After Langley's death the Smithsonian displayed his rebuilt Aerodrome as the first airplane capable of sustained flight, based on modified 1914 tests by Glenn Curtiss; Orville sent the Flyer to London in protest and it returned only after the Smithsonian published a correction in 1942, arriving in 1948.

### [Wright Company School of Aviation](../articles/science-and-innovation/wright-company-school-of-aviation.md) — Organization

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Wright brothers' flying school began in Dayton, Ohio. | Its first classes met in Montgomery, Alabama, in March–May 1910, on land that later became Maxwell Air Force Base; only then did the school settle at Huffman Prairie near Dayton. |

**Why it matters:** The school is remembered through Dayton and its aviation park, while the short, troubled Alabama camp produced a single graduate and is mostly recalled locally.

- **Q:** Why did the Wrights start their school in Montgomery rather than in Dayton, and why did it move back so soon?
  **A:** Dayton's winter weather was poor for flying, and Montgomery offered less rain and suitable winds; but engine trouble, weather and the need to prepare for exhibition tours in June led Wilbur to close the camp in May 1910 and move training to Huffman Field.
- **Q:** How did the school help shape American military aviation?
  **A:** Army officers such as Henry H. Arnold and Thomas Milling learned to fly there in 1911 and then helped start the Army's first permanent flight school at College Park, Maryland; Arnold later commanded the Army Air Corps.
- **Q:** How did the school serve the Wright Company's business as well as teaching?
  **A:** It shared a hangar with the company's test flying and exhibition team, produced pilots for the exhibition circuit, and marketed the Wrights' airplanes to civilian and military buyers.

## society-and-people

### [African Americans](../articles/society-and-people/african-americans.md) — Community

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Most of the Africans carried across the Atlantic in the slave trade were taken to what became the United States. | Of about 10.7 million Africans who survived the crossing, an estimated 388,000 (about 3.5 percent) landed in British North America and the United States; about 40 percent went to Brazil and about 35 percent to the non-Spanish Caribbean. |

**Why it matters:** The U.S. Black population grew mainly through births rather than imports, so a comparatively small number of arrivals became a large population, which can make the United States seem the center of a trade that was mostly directed elsewhere.

- **Q:** How did a relatively small number of African arrivals become one of the largest Black populations in the Americas?
  **A:** Only about 388,000 captive Africans landed in British North America and the United States, but in places like the Chesapeake the enslaved population became self-sustaining through births from about 1730, so the population grew by natural increase rather than continual imports.
- **Q:** Why did free Black Americans build their own churches and societies in the late 1700s?
  **A:** Facing segregation in white-led institutions (Richard Allen could lead only segregated services at St. George's in Philadelphia), they founded independent bodies such as the Free African Society (1787), Mother Bethel (1793) and the AME Church (1816), and mutual aid and fraternal groups that provided help, leadership and self-determination.
- **Q:** What pushed about six million Black Southerners to move north and west between 1910 and 1970?
  **A:** They sought to escape racial violence and Jim Crow oppression and to find economic and educational opportunity, and wartime labor shortages in both world wars opened industrial jobs in cities from Chicago and Detroit to Los Angeles and Oakland.
- **Q:** Why does the census give two different counts of the Black population in 2020?
  **A:** The "Black alone" count (41.1 million, 12.4 percent) includes only people who chose that single race, while "Black alone or in combination" (46.9 million, 14.2 percent) also includes people who reported Black together with one or more other races.

### [Angel Island Immigration Station](../articles/society-and-people/angel-island-immigration-station.md) — Landmark

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Angel Island was simply the "Ellis Island of the West", and every Chinese arrival was locked up there for months or years. | Unlike Ellis Island, Angel Island was built to enforce exclusion laws aimed at Asian immigrants; yet passenger records from 1913–1919 show many Chinese arrivals were not detained at all, Japanese arrivals were detained at an even higher rate, and Chinese detentions usually ran weeks to months, with only a few lasting years. |

**Why it matters:** The carved poems and the longest detentions became the most vivid memories of the station, and the Ellis Island nickname hides the racial exclusion that made Angel Island different.

- **Q:** Why did the U.S. government build its West Coast immigration station on an island, and why did Chinese arrivals face special scrutiny there?
  **A:** Officials chose Angel Island for its isolation, which made it easier to hold and question arrivals; Chinese immigrants faced special interrogations because the Chinese exclusion laws from 1882 barred laborers, so each applicant had to prove membership in an exempt group or a family tie to a U.S. citizen.
- **Q:** How did the 1906 San Francisco earthquake help create "paper sons", and how did the government respond?
  **A:** The earthquake and fire destroyed birth and immigration records, so Chinese residents could claim U.S. birth and overseas children; some sold these slots as false identities. Inspectors answered with long interrogations about family and village details, and mismatched answers could lead to deportation.
- **Q:** How do we know about detainees' feelings at Angel Island, and why are no women's poems known?
  **A:** Detainees, mostly rural men from southern China, carved more than 200 documented poems into the barrack walls, rediscovered in 1970. Women were held in the administration building, which burned in 1940, so any writing there was lost.
- **Q:** Why is the common "one million immigrants" figure misleading?
  **A:** The Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation notes that it comes from annual reports counting both arrivals and departures at the port of San Francisco; arrivals were about 550,000, and about 300,000 people were detained on the island.

### [Chinese Americans](../articles/society-and-people/chinese-americans.md) — Community

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Chinese railroad workers were left out of the 1869 Golden Spike ceremony entirely. | A Chinese contingent from the Central Pacific attended, and eight Chinese workers moved the final rail into place; photographers at the scene recorded them, but the most famous image, A. J. Russell's "East and West Shaking Hands," does not show them. |

**Why it matters:** The real erasure was in how the event was remembered, not who was there — the iconic photograph and company records left out the Chinese workers' names and faces, which is why descendants and historians have worked to restore them to the story.

- **Q:** Why did the Chinese American population shrink between 1882 and 1900, and what kept families from forming for decades?
  **A:** The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 (extended in 1892 and made permanent in 1902) cut off new laborers, and racial violence peaked in 1885–1887, so the population fell from about 133,000 to about 90,000; the 1875 Page Law had already restricted the immigration of Chinese women, leaving a heavily male community until war-bride admissions after 1946.
- **Q:** How did the Wong Kim Ark case protect Chinese Americans born in the United States?
  **A:** The Supreme Court ruled 6–2 in 1898 that the Fourteenth Amendment made almost everyone born on U.S. soil a citizen, so a U.S.-born child of Chinese parents could not be barred under the exclusion laws even though his parents could not naturalize.
- **Q:** How did World War II change the legal position of Chinese Americans?
  **A:** With China a wartime ally, Congress repealed exclusion in 1943 and opened naturalization; as many as 20,000 Chinese Americans served in the armed forces, about 40 percent of them not citizens, and the 1946 War Brides Act let their wives immigrate outside the small quota.
- **Q:** Why did the Chinese American community grow and diversify so much after 1965?
  **A:** The 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act made family reunification the main basis for admission, so immigrants from Mainland China, Hong Kong and Taiwan, plus students, professionals and refugees, could come and sponsor relatives.

### [Gullah Geechee](../articles/society-and-people/gullah-geechee.md) — Community

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Gullah is simply broken or old-fashioned English. | Gullah is a creole language; Lorenzo Dow Turner documented thousands of items of African origin in it, including about 3,600 African-derived "basket names" given to children. |

**Why it matters:** Earlier white scholars such as Guy B. Johnson dismissed it as the "peasant English" of an earlier century, and the name Geechee itself was long used as an insult.

- **Q:** Why did Gullah Geechee communities preserve more African language and custom than most other African American communities?
  **A:** Their ancestors were concentrated in large numbers on coastal rice, indigo and Sea Island cotton plantations and on islands that could be reached only by boat until bridges were built from the early 1950s, so that isolation let them keep and recombine West and Central African traditions.
- **Q:** How did the Civil War and Reconstruction both open and close a path to landownership for Sea Islanders?
  **A:** After Union forces occupied the area, the Port Royal Experiment brought schools such as Penn School and sold some land to freedpeople, and Sherman's Field Order No. 15 settled about 40,000 people on forty-acre plots; but in the fall of 1865 President Johnson returned most of that land to the former planters.
- **Q:** Why is rice central to the Gullah Geechee story?
  **A:** Planters sought captives from Africa's Windward or "Rice Coast", from Senegambia to Liberia, for their rice-growing knowledge, and crafts such as the fanner basket used to winnow rice survive in today's sweetgrass baskets.

### [Okies](../articles/society-and-people/okies.md) — Community

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Okies were mostly dirt farmers who all ended up picking crops in California's fields. | At least half of the migrants had lived in towns or cities and worked blue-collar or white-collar jobs, and many went to California's cities, where they usually found work fairly quickly; at the Barstow junction about 38 percent headed for Los Angeles, while others went to the farm valleys. |

**Why it matters:** The best-known images of the migration — Steinbeck's Joad family and Dorothea Lange's photographs of farm labor camps — focused on rural families in the fields, so the much larger urban side of the story faded from popular memory.

- **Q:** How did "Okie" change from a simple nickname into an insult?
  **A:** It began as a shortening of "Oklahoman", but in 1930s California reporters and residents applied it to every poor migrant from Oklahoma, Arkansas, Texas or Missouri, and it came to carry the sting of an ethnic slur, reinforced by the destitute image of the Joads in The Grapes of Wrath.
- **Q:** If the Dust Bowl was not the main cause, why did so many Oklahomans leave in the 1930s?
  **A:** More migrants left southeastern Oklahoma than the Panhandle Dust Bowl. They were pushed out by drought combined with a long agricultural depression, farm foreclosures, widespread tenancy, and mechanization and federal crop-reduction policies that idled land, and pulled by family ties and earlier migration to California.
- **Q:** How did the Okie migration lead to a Supreme Court ruling on the right to travel between states?
  **A:** California made it a crime to bring a poor nonresident into the state, and Fred Edwards was convicted for bringing his unemployed brother-in-law from Texas; in Edwards v. California (1941) the Court held that the law unconstitutionally burdened interstate commerce, and concurring justices said free movement was a right of national citizenship.
- **Q:** How did the migrants reshape California's culture?
  **A:** They built "Little Oklahomas" in the Central Valley, brought evangelical Protestantism (especially Baptist and Pentecostal churches) and a love of country music that shaped the state's country music scene, from Bob Wills to Merle Haggard.

### [Slavery in the United States](../articles/society-and-people/slavery-in-the-united-states.md) — Concept

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Slavery was a purely Southern institution that the North never practised. | Slavery was legal in the North too; enslaved people were 30 percent of Kings County (Brooklyn) residents in 1790, New York's final emancipation came only in 1827, and about 16 people in New Jersey were still legally enslaved in December 1865. |

**Why it matters:** Treating slavery as only Southern hides how gradual and incomplete northern emancipation was, and how northern banks and merchants profited from the cotton economy.

- **Q:** Why did Virginia's 1662 law matter so much for slavery's growth?
  **A:** It made a child's status follow the mother's rather than the father's, so every child of an enslaved woman was born enslaved; slavery became hereditary and could grow through births rather than only through imports.
- **Q:** How did the enslaved population grow from about 700,000 in 1790 to nearly 4 million in 1860 even though importing enslaved people was banned from 1808?
  **A:** Because children of enslaved mothers were born enslaved, the population grew through births, and the domestic slave trade then moved about a million people from the Upper South to the cotton-growing Deep South.
- **Q:** How did slavery reach into the free states before the Civil War?
  **A:** The Constitution's fugitive slave clause required people escaping bondage to be returned, and the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 used federal commissioners without juries to send about 1,000 Black Americans South, provoking resistance in northern communities.

## states-and-territories

### [Alaska](../articles/states-and-territories/alaska.md) — State

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Americans in 1867 overwhelmingly ridiculed the Alaska purchase as "Seward's Folly." | Critics did use nicknames such as "Seward's Folly" and "Seward's Icebox," but the Senate approved the treaty 37–2, the House later funded it 113–43, and much of the press and correspondence Seward received favored the acquisition. |

**Why it matters:** Retelling the purchase as one far-sighted man against a mocking public makes a tidier story, and later gold and oil strikes made the jokes seem prophetic, so the nicknames outlived the more mixed and largely favorable record.

- **Q:** Why did Russia sell Alaska, and why is the story of universal American ridicule misleading?
  **A:** The Crimean War had shown how vulnerable Russian America was, and the tsar sold it rather than risk losing it in another war. In the United States some critics mocked the deal, but the Senate approved the treaty 37–2, the House funded it 113–43, and much of the press favored it.
- **Q:** How did statehood lead to the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act?
  **A:** The 1958 Statehood Act let the new state select about 105 million acres of federal land, but Alaska Native claims had never been settled. Native protests led the Interior Department to freeze selections in 1969, and the state and oil companies then pressed Congress, which in 1971 extinguished claims to more than 360 million acres in exchange for about 45 million acres and about $962.5 million, managed largely through Native corporations.
- **Q:** How did World War II affect the Unangax̂ (Aleut) people?
  **A:** After Japan attacked the Aleutians in 1942, U.S. authorities evacuated 881 Unangax̂ to abandoned canneries and camps in Southeast Alaska without plumbing or electricity and often without medical care; at least 74 died in four camps, while some Unangax̂ men served in the armed forces.
- **Q:** Why was Alaska's statehood delayed, and how did Cold War concerns shape it?
  **A:** Statehood bills were introduced year after year; Eisenhower supported statehood but wanted concerns settled first, including national security on the border with the Soviet Union and the fact that about 99 percent of the land was federally owned. A compromise on land transfers allowed the 1958 act to pass.

### [California](../articles/states-and-territories/california.md) — State

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Because California entered the Union as a free state in 1850, it had no slavery or legalized forced labor. | The 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians let white settlers and Californios hold Native people as indentured servants, and laws legalizing Indian indenture stood until 1863; slavery of Black Californians also persisted in some regions under lax oversight, and an 1852 state law made it illegal for enslaved people to flee within California. |

**Why it matters:** The national story of the Compromise of 1850 centers on the free-state label and the balance between North and South in the Senate, which hides how the new state's own laws treated Native and Black Californians.

- **Q:** Why did the Gold Rush speed California's path to statehood?
  **A:** The discovery of gold in January 1848 brought more than 300,000 people by 1850, creating a pressing need for civil government; Californians wrote a constitution in 1849 and sought statehood that year, and Congress admitted California on September 9, 1850, as part of the Compromise of 1850.
- **Q:** How and why did California's Native population fall between 1769 and 1860?
  **A:** From about 310,000 people in 1769 it fell to about 150,000 by 1848 and about 30,000 by 1860, because of diseases introduced in the mission and Mexican eras, deaths tied to the missions, violence by miners during the Gold Rush, and state laws that allowed Native people to be held in indenture.
- **Q:** What did the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo promise Californios, and how did those promises fare?
  **A:** It made them U.S. citizens unless they chose to keep Mexican citizenship and promised to protect their property, but the Senate struck the article protecting Mexican land grants, and families such as the Peraltas lost most of their land to lawyers, taxes, squatters and speculators.

### [Hawaii](../articles/states-and-territories/hawaii.md) — State

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Native Hawaiians accepted annexation, and Hawaiʻi joined the United States through a ratified treaty. | More than 21,000 Native Hawaiians—over half of those counted in 1897—signed petitions against annexation, and the 1897 treaty died in the Senate with only 46 supporters; annexation came instead by a joint resolution that needed only simple majorities, signed July 7, 1898. |

**Why it matters:** The petitions were long absent from Hawaiian history books and few people knew they existed until a scholar found them in the National Archives, while the 1959 statehood referendum is easily read back onto 1898.

- **Q:** Why was Hawaiʻi annexed by a joint resolution of Congress rather than by treaty?
  **A:** A treaty needed a two-thirds Senate vote, and after more than 21,000 Native Hawaiians petitioned against annexation, only 46 senators would support the 1897 treaty. When the Spanish–American War revived interest in the islands, supporters used a joint resolution, which needed only simple majorities, and McKinley signed it on July 7, 1898.
- **Q:** How did the sugar plantation economy change who lived in Hawaiʻi?
  **A:** Planters recruited contract laborers from Japan, China, the Philippines, Portugal and elsewhere, while disease after Western contact sharply reduced the Native Hawaiian population. Native Hawaiians fell from 97 percent of the population in 1853 to 16 percent in 1923, when people of Japanese ancestry were the largest group.
- **Q:** Why did statehood take until 1959, and how was it tied to Alaska?
  **A:** Territorial requests for statehood began in 1903 and a 1937 congressional committee found Hawaiʻi qualified, but plantation interests, the Pearl Harbor attack and Senate inaction delayed it. Congress paired Hawaiʻi with Alaska; once Alaska was admitted in early 1959, Hawaiʻi's bill passed in March, voters accepted it in June, and it became the fiftieth state on August 21, 1959.
- **Q:** What does the 1993 Apology Resolution do, and what does it not do?
  **A:** It acknowledges that U.S. agents and citizens took part in the 1893 overthrow and apologizes for depriving Native Hawaiians of self-determination, but it states that it does not settle any claims against the United States.

### [Pennsylvania](../articles/states-and-territories/pennsylvania.md) — State

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Penn's "Great Treaty" with the Lenape under the elm at Shackamaxon was the one treaty never broken, so colonial Pennsylvania dealt honestly with Native people. | No original written record of a 1682 treaty exists and historians debate whether and when it happened; and in 1737 Thomas Penn and agent James Logan used an unsigned draft deed in the Walking Purchase to take a vast tract of Lenape land, which a scholarly encyclopedia describes as defrauding the Delawares. |

**Why it matters:** Voltaire's praise and Benjamin West's 1771–72 painting turned a possible meeting into a founding legend that still appears in textbooks, while the dispossession that followed is less visible in public memory.

- **Q:** Why did Charles II grant Pennsylvania to William Penn, and how did Penn's Quaker beliefs shape the colony's government?
  **A:** The most plausible explanation is that the king owed Penn a large debt inherited from his father, Admiral Penn, and paid it in land. Penn's Frame of Government created an elected assembly, and the 1701 Charter of Privileges guaranteed worship to anyone who believed in one God, making Pennsylvania an early leader in separating religion from government, although Quakers were always a minority.
- **Q:** How did the Walking Purchase change the Lenape's place in Pennsylvania, and what did that lead to?
  **A:** Using an unsigned 1686 draft deed, proprietary agents had pre-scouted walkers cover more than 60 miles in a day and a half and angled the boundary to take perhaps over a million acres. Most Delawares in eastern Pennsylvania moved west to the Ohio Valley, traded with the French, and many sided with France in the Seven Years' War; the Lenape were later pushed on to Indiana, Kansas and Indian Territory.
- **Q:** Why did slavery linger in Pennsylvania for decades after the 1780 abolition law?
  **A:** The act freed no one at once; children born to enslaved mothers after it passed were bound as servants until age 28, and enslavers could keep registered people enslaved. The state recorded its last known enslaved person in the 1850s, and enslavers like President Washington rotated enslaved people in and out of Philadelphia to evade the residency rules.
- **Q:** How did Pennsylvania's resources make it the "Arsenal of America," and what labor conflicts came with that growth?
  **A:** Coal, oil (from 1859 near Titusville), more than 10,000 miles of railroad at its peak, and steel (60 percent of the nation's output in 1900) made it an industrial center that produced almost a third of U.S. steel in World War II. Growth brought violent conflicts such as the 1877 railroad strike in Pittsburgh, the 1892 Homestead Strike, and the 1897 Lattimer massacre of Slavic miners.

### [United States](../articles/states-and-territories/united-states.md) — Country

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| When Europeans arrived, the land that became the United States was a thinly peopled, untouched wilderness. | Millions of Indigenous people lived in the Americas before 1492; they farmed corn, beans and squash, burned underbrush to shape forests, and built towns such as Cahokia, which may have held 10,000–20,000 people (some estimates run to 30,000) around 1050–1150. |

**Why it matters:** Picturing an empty continent erases the Native nations whose homelands became the United States, and whose 575 federally recognized governments still keep a government-to-government relationship with it.

- **Q:** How did the United States grow from thirteen states on the Atlantic seaboard into a country reaching the Arctic and the Pacific, and who paid the cost?
  **A:** The 1783 Treaty of Paris set its western boundary at the Mississippi River; the Louisiana Purchase (1803) added about 530 million acres, and Alaska was bought from Russia in 1867; Alaska and Hawaii became states in 1959. The land was Native homeland, and expansion came at Native nations' expense; under the 1830 Indian Removal Act nearly 50,000 eastern Indians were relocated, and about 4,000 of 16,000 Cherokees died on the Trail of Tears.
- **Q:** Why does the Constitution apply differently in Puerto Rico or Guam than in a state?
  **A:** They are unincorporated territories, insular areas that are not part of any state or the federal district, where Congress has decided that only selected parts of the Constitution apply. Puerto Rico and the Northern Mariana Islands are also commonwealths with a more developed relationship set out in written agreements.
- **Q:** Why does the Declaration of Independence speak of the "united States of America" with a lowercase "united," and when did the name become official?
  **A:** In mid-1776 the states saw themselves as separate states acting together. Congress ordered "United States" to replace "United Colonies" in official documents on September 9, 1776, and the Articles of Confederation's first article made "The United States of America" the confederacy's formal name.
- **Q:** How do Native nations fit into the U.S. political system today?
  **A:** The 575 federally recognized tribes hold inherent rights of self-government (tribal sovereignty) and a government-to-government relationship with the United States, alongside the federal government, the fifty states, the District of Columbia and the territories.

### [Wyoming](../articles/states-and-territories/wyoming.md) — State

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Wyoming was the first place in the United States where women voted. | Under New Jersey's 1776 constitution some unmarried women who met a property requirement voted until the legislature limited the vote to white male taxpayers in 1807. Wyoming's 1869 law was the first by a U.S. territory or state to grant women the vote outright, and unlike New Jersey's it was never taken back. |

**Why it matters:** New Jersey's experiment was brief, rested on loosely worded law and was reversed, while Wyoming's lasting law became central to the state's identity as the "Equality State."

- **Q:** Why did Wyoming's all-Democratic territorial legislature give women the vote in 1869?
  **A:** Historians point to mixed motives. Lawmakers hoped the publicity would attract settlers, especially women, when men outnumbered women about six to one; some expected women to vote Democratic or wanted to embarrass the Republican governor, John Campbell, whom they expected to veto it; many argued that if Black and Chinese men could vote, white women should too; and some, including the bill's sponsor William Bright, believed it was right.
- **Q:** How did women's suffrage nearly cost Wyoming statehood?
  **A:** The 1889 constitution kept equal suffrage, and House Democrats attacked that article as a reason to deny statehood to a Republican-leaning territory. The bill passed the House only 139 to 127 in March 1890 and the Senate 29 to 18 before Harrison signed it on July 10, 1890.
- **Q:** How did the Union Pacific's coal mines shape the Rock Springs massacre?
  **A:** The railroad owned the mines that fueled its trains and, after an 1884 strike, had managers hire only Chinese workers, so by 1885 nearly 600 Chinese miners worked beside about 300 white miners. On September 2, 1885, white miners attacked the Chinese, killing 28; no one was charged.

## symbols-and-national-identity

### [Betsy Ross flag myth](../articles/symbols-and-national-identity/betsy-ross-flag-myth.md) — Myth

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| George Washington asked Betsy Ross to design and sew the first Stars and Stripes in 1776, and she did, adding five-pointed stars in a circle. | No contemporary document records the visit or credits Ross with the design. Congress adopted the flag only in June 1777, Francis Hopkinson claimed the design in 1780, and Ross's documented work is as one of several Philadelphia flag makers. |

**Why it matters:** The story entered print in 1870 from family memory, then spread through a widely copied 1893 painting, school lessons and patriotic tourism because it offered a founding heroine for a newly revered symbol.

- **Q:** Why do historians doubt that Betsy Ross made the first flag at Washington's request in 1776?
  **A:** The story first appeared in 1870, almost a century later and 34 years after Ross died, and rests on family recollections from people who had not witnessed the events. No document records a congressional flag committee in 1776 or any dealing between Washington and Ross, and Congress did not adopt a flag until June 14, 1777. Even Canby's own research found no supporting documents.
- **Q:** How did a family story become a national legend between 1870 and 1909?
  **A:** Canby's 1870 paper was repeated by national journals by 1873, embellished in books such as Reigart's 1878 history, and dramatized in Charles Weisgerber's painting, which crowds saw at the 1893 Chicago fair and which was reproduced widely. The flag was becoming a revered symbol after the Civil War, and the 1909 book by Canby's relatives, school lessons and toys fixed the story in memory.
- **Q:** What does the documentary record actually show about Betsy Ross's work?
  **A:** She ran an upholstery business, was paid by the Pennsylvania State Navy Board in May 1777 for making ship's colours, and went on making flags for decades, including many under government contracts around the War of 1812. Historian Marla Miller calls her one of Philadelphia's most important flag makers of the Revolution, alongside others such as Rebecca Young, Margaret Manney and Sarah Barry.

### [E pluribus unum](../articles/symbols-and-national-identity/e-pluribus-unum.md) — Symbol

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| E pluribus unum was the official national motto of the United States until 1956, when Congress replaced it with "In God We Trust." | E pluribus unum has been the motto of the Great Seal since 1782 and is a required coin inscription, but no statute designated it the national motto; the 1956 act declaring "In God we trust" is the law that names one, and E pluribus unum stayed on the seal and coins. |

**Why it matters:** The phrase appeared on the seal, coins and the dollar bill for so long that it was widely treated as the country's motto, and even an editor's note on the online edition of a 1923 scholarly study says it was replaced in 1956.

- **Q:** Why does the Great Seal's motto describe "many" becoming "one", and what did the "many" originally mean?
  **A:** In 1776 and 1782 the motto referred to the thirteen states joining in one union; Du Simitière's design ringed the shield with thirteen escutcheons for the states, and the official 1782 remarks say the motto alludes to the union of the states under Congress.
- **Q:** How did a motto from a London magazine end up on the seal, and why is it uncertain who chose it?
  **A:** The phrase was printed on the title page of the widely read Gentleman's Magazine, which educated colonists knew. It first appears in the seal record in Du Simitière's August 1776 design for the Franklin-Adams-Jefferson committee, but no document says who suggested it, so the State Department credits Du Simitière, Deutsch argued for Franklin, and the Jefferson Papers editors leave it open.
- **Q:** Why is it inaccurate to say "In God We Trust" replaced E pluribus unum as the national motto in 1956?
  **A:** E pluribus unum had never been designated the national motto by law. The 1956 act was the first to declare a national motto, and E pluribus unum remained on the Great Seal and is still required by law on the reverse of every U.S. coin.

### [Great Seal of the United States](../articles/symbols-and-national-identity/great-seal-of-the-united-states.md) — Symbol

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The pyramid and all-seeing eye on the back of the dollar bill are a Masonic emblem that Freemason founders put on the Great Seal. | The Eye of Providence came from artist Pierre Eugène Du Simitière's 1776 design for the first seal committee, the pyramid from William Barton's 1782 reverse, and Charles Thomson finished both; Franklin is the only known Freemason involved at any stage, and the eye-over-pyramid has never been adopted as a symbol by any grand lodge. |

**Why it matters:** Some Masonic lodges have used an eye as a design element, and the reverse, never cut as a die, became familiar through the 1935 one-dollar bill rather than through Thomson's 1782 explanation of its religious and patriotic meaning.

- **Q:** Why did it take six years and three committees to produce the Great Seal?
  **A:** The 1776 committee's elaborate design, with emblems of European nations and a Red Sea scene, was tabled; a 1780 committee's report was recommitted; only in 1782 did Congress give the material of all three committees to Charles Thomson, who combined elements such as the 1776 eye and motto and Barton's eagle and pyramid into a design Congress accepted within a week.
- **Q:** How does the front of the seal express the powers Congress held under the Articles of Confederation?
  **A:** The eagle holds an olive branch and thirteen arrows, standing for the powers of peace and war, which under the Confederation belonged to Congress alone; the shield's thirteen stripes for the states are joined under a blue chief representing Congress.
- **Q:** Why does the reverse of the seal appear on the dollar bill but never on an official document?
  **A:** No die for the reverse has ever been cut, so documents are sealed only with the obverse; the reverse became widely seen after Roosevelt approved a 1935 dollar-bill design that printed both sides.
- **Q:** What evidence contradicts the claim that the eye and pyramid are a Masonic emblem?
  **A:** The design record traces the eye to the non-Mason artist Du Simitière in 1776 and the pyramid to Barton in 1782, with Thomson finishing the reverse; Franklin was the only known Mason involved, and no grand lodge has adopted the eye over a pyramid as a symbol.

### [Liberty Bell](../articles/symbols-and-national-identity/liberty-bell.md) — Symbol

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The Liberty Bell rang out on July 4, 1776, to announce the Declaration of Independence, and cracked doing so. | The National Park Service finds no evidence the bell rang on July 4 or July 8, 1776; the July 4 story comes from George Lippard's 1847 fiction, and the crack that silenced the bell dates from the 1840s. |

**Why it matters:** Lippard's tale was retold as fact in books and schoolrooms, and it gave a beloved object a dramatic founding moment that the record does not supply.

- **Q:** Why did abolitionists, rather than the Revolutionary generation, give the Liberty Bell its name?
  **A:** The inscription from Leviticus 25:10, about proclaiming liberty to all inhabitants, went largely unnoticed during the Revolution. In the 1830s antislavery activists saw the gap between those words and the enslavement of millions, and the Anti-Slavery Record of February 1835 called the State House bell "The Liberty Bell" and said its peals were a mockery while slavery lasted.
- **Q:** How did a Philadelphia bell become a national symbol between 1885 and 1918?
  **A:** It traveled by rail to seven fairs and expositions between 1885 and 1915, drawing huge crowds along the way, and during World War I the government used it in Liberty Loan drives that raised more than $17 billion. These tours and campaigns turned a local relic into a symbol recognized across the country.
- **Q:** Why is it uncertain whether the bell rang for the Declaration of Independence?
  **A:** No record shows the bell ringing on July 4 or July 8, 1776, and the steeple was in poor condition. The National Park Service says there is no evidence, while historian Gary Nash writes that it is believed to have rung at the July 8 public reading; the July 4 version is Lippard's fiction.

### [National symbols of the United States](../articles/symbols-and-national-identity/national-symbols-of-the-united-states.md) — Concept

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Benjamin Franklin wanted the wild turkey, not the bald eagle, to be the national bird and proposed it for the Great Seal. | Franklin's 1776 seal proposal showed Moses and Pharaoh at the Red Sea, with no bird at all. His praise of the turkey comes from a January 1784 letter to his daughter, written after the seal was adopted, that mocked the eagle on the Society of the Cincinnati's badge; he never sent it, and the full English text was not printed until 1817. |

**Why it matters:** The joke is vivid and fits Thanksgiving, so it is retold as if Franklin had campaigned for the turkey; and because the bald eagle was not formally named the national bird until 2024, it is easy to imagine an open contest between birds.

- **Q:** Why did the bald eagle need an act of Congress in 2024 to become the national bird if it had been on the Great Seal since 1782?
  **A:** The 1782 decision adopted the eagle as part of the seal's coat of arms, not as a national bird. No statute named a national bird until Public Law 118-206, approved December 23, 2024, added a section on the national bird to title 36 of the U.S. Code.
- **Q:** How have Supreme Court decisions limited what government can require of people with respect to national symbols?
  **A:** In Barnette (1943) the Court held that schools could not compel students to salute the flag and recite the pledge, and in Texas v. Johnson (1989) it held that burning a flag in protest is expressive conduct a state cannot punish in order to preserve the flag as a symbol of national unity. The Flag Code's display rules are likewise customs without penalties.
- **Q:** Why do the laws designating the bison and the bald eagle refer to Native nations?
  **A:** Their findings record that bison were tied to the economic and spiritual lives of many tribes, more than 60 of which take part in the Intertribal Buffalo Council's restoration work, and that bald eagles are integral to the spiritual lives of most Indigenous peoples and Tribal communities.
- **Q:** How is the origin of Uncle Sam different from that of the flag or the national anthem?
  **A:** The flag and anthem were adopted by acts of Congress, while Uncle Sam grew out of popular usage during the War of 1812 and was shaped by cartoonists and poster artists; Congress only honored Samuel Wilson as the figure's progenitor in 1961, and the National Park Service still calls the name's origin disputed.

### [The Star-Spangled Banner](../articles/symbols-and-national-identity/star-spangled-banner.md) — Symbol

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| The anthem's tune was an old English drinking song. | 'To Anacreon in Heaven' was the club song of the Anacreontic Society, a London gentlemen's music society; it was sung in four-part harmony with harpsichord after the society's dinners and concerts. Its words toast wine and the members drank, but musicologist Mark Clague notes it was far too sophisticated for a pub. Key chose the tune himself and had used it for an earlier song. |

**Why it matters:** The lyric's convivial toasts invite the label, and in the 1920s opponents of making it the anthem, especially in dry areas during Prohibition, attacked it as a drinking song.

- **Q:** Why was Francis Scott Key on a boat near Fort McHenry during the bombardment, rather than in the fort or the city?
  **A:** With President Madison's approval he had sailed under a flag of truce with prisoner-exchange agent John Skinner to win the release of Dr. William Beanes. The British agreed to free Beanes but held all three under guard on their truce boat so they could not warn Baltimore of the coming attack.
- **Q:** How did the song go from a popular patriotic tune to the legal national anthem?
  **A:** It spread through newspapers within weeks of the battle, became a Union favourite during the Civil War, was adopted by the Navy in 1889 and the armed forces in 1916, and became a ballpark ritual after the 1918 World Series. Representative John Linthicum's bill, backed by a petition of more than five million signatures, passed the House in 1930 and the Senate in 1931, and Hoover signed it on March 3, 1931.
- **Q:** Why do historians and critics disagree about the third stanza's line about the "hireling and slave"?
  **A:** Key never explained it. Some read "slave" as the Colonial Marines, formerly enslaved men fighting for the British, and see the line as hostile to their bid for freedom; Mark Clague agrees it refers to them but argues it condemns the enemy rather than celebrating slavery; others read the phrase as stock language for the whole British force.
