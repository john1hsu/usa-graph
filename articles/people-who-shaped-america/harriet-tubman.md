  myth: Tubman made 19 trips into the South and rescued more than 300 people, with a $40,000 bounty on her head.
  fact: Records and her own accounts show about 13 trips, all to Maryland's Eastern Shore, freeing about 70 people, while she gave directions to about 70 more who escaped on their own.   why: The larger numbers come from Sarah Bradford's 1868–69 biography and an 1867 letter supporting her pension claim; they stuck because they suited a legend, but they obscure how carefully she chose people she knew and trusted.

# Harriet Tubman

Harriet Tubman (c. 1822–1913) escaped slavery on Maryland's Eastern Shore in 1849 and spent the next decade going back for the people she loved. Born Araminta "Minty" Ross, she became the best-known conductor of the Underground Railroad and, the National Archives notes, arguably the most successful at personally leading enslaved people to freedom.[^s3] During the Civil War she served the Union Army as a scout, spy, cook and nurse and helped lead an armed raid in South Carolina, and in later life she campaigned for women's suffrage and cared for elderly and poor African Americans in Auburn, New York.[^s1][^s5]

## Life and work

Minty Ross was born in Dorchester County, Maryland, most likely in early 1822 on Anthony Thompson's plantation at Peter's Neck. Her parents, Harriet "Rit" Green and Ben Ross, were enslaved by different men and had nine children.[^s4][^s2] Her mother's enslaver, Edward Brodess, moved Rit and the children to his farm at Bucktown, separating them from Ben, and sold three of Minty's older sisters to the Deep South.[^s1] In the mid-1830s, after she refused to help an overseer stop a freedom seeker, he threw a two-pound weight that struck her head; the injury caused spells of sudden sleep or unconsciousness for the rest of her life.[^s1][^s4] Work in the Eastern Shore's marshes taught her the landscape she later used to guide people out.[^s1] Around 1844 she married John Tubman, a free Black man, and took the name Harriet.[^s5][^s2]

When Brodess died in March 1849, Tubman feared she would be sold south. She fled alone that fall and reached Philadelphia.[^s1][^s5] Within a year Congress passed the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which left any freedom seeker anywhere in the United States at risk of recapture.[^s4] Tubman's first rescue, in 1850, brought out her niece Kessiah and Kessiah's two children; on Christmas Day 1854 she rescued her three remaining brothers, and in 1857 she brought her aging parents north.[^s5] Between 1850 and 1860 she returned to Maryland about 13 times and freed about 70 people, mostly family and friends, guiding many of them to St. Catharines, Ontario, beyond the reach of U.S. law. She gave instructions to roughly 70 more who escaped on their own.[^s1][^s2] She relied on trusted helpers, coded letters and disguises, and changed the tempo of hymns to signal danger or safety.[^s2] In 1858 she met the abolitionist John Brown at St. Catharines and gathered support for his plans, though she did not join his 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry.[^s4][^s5] In 1859 she bought a house and seven acres near Auburn, New York, from Senator William H. Seward, and settled her family there.[^s5][^s1]

In 1862, at the urging of Massachusetts governor John Andrew, Tubman went to Port Royal, South Carolina, where she worked for the Union Army as a cook, nurse, laundress, scout and spy.[^s1][^s5] On the night of June 1–2, 1863, she helped plan and guide a raid up the Combahee River with Colonel James Montgomery and about 150 Black Union soldiers of the regiment the Park Service calls the 2nd Carolina Colored Infantry. The raiders burned plantations and destroyed Confederate supplies, and more than 700 enslaved people escaped to the Union boats; sources give figures from "over 700" to 750.[^s6][^s1][^s3] The Smithsonian describes her as the first woman to lead a major U.S. military operation.[^s6]

After nursing soldiers at Fort Monroe, Virginia, in 1865, Tubman settled in Auburn. In 1869 she married Nelson Davis, a veteran of the 8th U.S. Colored Infantry; they later adopted a daughter, Gertie.[^s5] Because she had never been formally enlisted or regularly paid, Tubman spent decades seeking a federal pension for her own service. After Davis died in 1888 she received an $8-a-month widow's pension; in 1899 Congress passed H.R. 4982, raising it to $20 a month for her service as a nurse, after a Senate committee rejected the House's proposed $25.[^s11][^s7] From the 1890s she spoke at both Black and white suffrage conventions, and she was a founding member of the National Association of Colored Women.[^s5][^s1] In 1896 she bought 25 acres next to her land for a home for aged and sick African Americans, transferred it to the AME Zion Church in 1903, and saw the Harriet Tubman Home open in 1908. She died of pneumonia in Auburn on March 10, 1913, and is buried in Fort Hill Cemetery.[^s5][^s1]

## Legacy

Tubman's fame began in her own lifetime, partly through Sarah Bradford's biographies of 1869 and 1886 (the second titled *Harriet Tubman: The Moses of Her People*), which also inflated her record to 19 trips and 300 rescues, figures her close friends contradicted.[^s2][^s5] The National Park Service's myth-and-fact sheet, drawing on court and family records, corrects those figures and rejects later inventions such as quilt-pattern codes.[^s2] On March 25, 2013, Proclamation 8943 created the Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad National Monument in Dorchester County, covering landscapes of her early life such as Peter's Neck.[^s8] In December 2014 Congress established national historical parks in Maryland and in Auburn, New York, through the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2015 (P.L. 113-291).[^s9][^s10] Nearly a century after the Combahee raid, a Black feminist organization took the name Combahee River Collective in her honor.[^s6]

## Connections

Tubman is a central figure of **African American history**, and **slavery in the United States** is the background to her life.[^s1] She **participated in** the **Underground Railroad**, as its best-known conductor, and in **abolitionism**, working with John Brown and other activists.[^s1][^s4] She **participated in** the **American Civil War** as a Union scout, spy and nurse, and in the **Combahee River raid** of June 1863.[^s3][^s6] Later she **participated in** the **women's suffrage movement**, attending suffrage conventions in the 1890s, and was a **member of** the **National Association of Colored Women**.[^s5][^s1] The **Fugitive Slave Act of 1850** shaped her routes into Canada, like **Frederick Douglass** she was born enslaved in **Maryland**, and her own escape ended in Philadelphia, **Pennsylvania**.[^s4][^s1]
