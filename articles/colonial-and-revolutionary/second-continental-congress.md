  - question: Why did the Second Continental Congress spend more than a year fighting Britain before it declared independence?
    answer: Most delegates in 1775 still hoped for reconciliation; Congress described its armed resistance as defence, denied any aim of separating, and sent the Olive Branch Petition to George III. Only after the king refused to receive it and declared the colonies in open rebellion did calls for independence grow, leading to the vote of July 2, 1776.
  - question: How could a body created only to coordinate colonial protest end up acting as a national government?
    answer: Because war had already begun when it convened, Congress had to raise and command an army, issue bills of credit to pay for it, conduct diplomacy and negotiate with Native nations; in doing so it exceeded the authority the colonial governments had given it and governed de facto until the Articles of Confederation took effect in 1781.
  - question: Why was the question of how states would vote in Congress so contentious when the Articles of Confederation were debated in 1776?
    answer: Congress had voted by colony, one vote each, and the Dickinson draft kept that rule; Benjamin Franklin argued that votes should follow population and contributions, but his amendment was defeated, so small and large states kept equal votes in the Confederation Congress.

# Second Continental Congress

The Second Continental Congress was the gathering of colonial delegates that met in Philadelphia from May 10, 1775, and, with no written constitution behind it, became the working government of the American Revolution. It raised an army, issued money, conducted diplomacy, declared independence and drafted the Articles of Confederation, governing until those Articles took effect on March 1, 1781.[^s1][^s3]

## Origins

The First Continental Congress, meeting at Carpenters' Hall in the fall of 1774 with delegates from every colony except Georgia, had organized a boycott of British goods in protest of the Coercive (Intolerable) Acts and agreed to reconvene on May 10, 1775.[^s1][^s12] By the time delegates returned, British troops and Massachusetts militia had already fought at Lexington and Concord, so the new Congress assembled in the Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, in the middle of a war.[^s1][^s2] It elected John Hancock as its president.[^s3]

Congress was divided. Delegates such as John Dickinson still hoped to reconcile with Britain, and many Philadelphians opposed independence.[^s12][^s8] On July 6, 1775, Congress justified armed resistance as self-defence and, in a declaration drafted by Thomas Jefferson and reworked by Dickinson, stated plainly, "we mean not to dissolve that Union" with Great Britain.[^s5] Two days later delegates of twelve colonies (Georgia's had not yet arrived) signed the Olive Branch Petition, written mainly by Dickinson, asking George III to help restore harmony. When it reached London in September the king would not receive it and declared the colonies to be in "open and avowed rebellion," and talk of independence in Congress grew louder.[^s8]

## Powers and structure

Congress had only the authority the colonial governments delegated to it, and in practice it exceeded that authority to act as a de facto national government.[^s1] Each colony's delegation cast a single vote; when Congress debated a permanent confederation in the summer of 1776, Benjamin Franklin argued that votes should be proportioned to population and contributions, but his amendment failed and the one-state, one-vote rule carried into the Articles.[^s10] In the same debate Samuel Chase's motion to count only white inhabitants when setting each state's share of common expenses was defeated on August 1, with the seven northern states against it, a dispute over enslaved people that prefigured later fights over representation.[^s10]

Lacking executive departments, Congress governed through committees and often as a Committee of the Whole. It created the Committee of Secret Correspondence on November 29, 1775, to contact friends abroad, renamed it the Committee for Foreign Affairs in 1777, and frequently bypassed it.[^s2] Without any power to tax, it paid for the war by issuing bills of credit that the twelve represented colonies pledged to redeem, and by borrowing; it was bankrupt by the war's end.[^s3][^s12]

## What it did

**Army.** In June 1775 John Adams urged Congress to adopt the forces besieging Boston as a Continental army. Congress chose George Washington to command it on June 15; he accepted the next day, declining a salary, and his commission of June 17 named him "General and Commander in chief" of the army of the United Colonies, bound to follow Congress's orders.[^s3][^s11][^s7] The army's ranks were contested: that fall Washington barred the recruiting of Black men, and Congress allowed him to re-enlist only those who had already served at Boston. As enlistments fell the limits were lifted, and by summer 1778 more than 750 Black soldiers were serving in the Continental Army, according to a National Archives historian.[^s9]

**Native nations.** Acting as an independent government, Congress appointed commissioners to make peace with Native nations.[^s3] On July 13, 1775, it approved a speech asking the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy to remain at home and join neither side, describing the war as "a family quarrel between us and Old England."[^s6] Congress generally preferred Native neutrality early in the war, judging alliances more costly than useful, but in May 1776 it authorized the use of Native warriors in the northern campaign, and in June it allowed Washington to recruit up to 2,000.[^s9]

**Independence and diplomacy.** Congress opened American ports to all nations except Britain on April 6, 1776, and sent Silas Deane to seek French aid.[^s1] It received Richard Henry Lee's resolution for independence on June 7, appointed a committee of five to draft a declaration on June 11, voted for independence on July 2 and adopted the Declaration on July 4, 1776.[^s4] It adopted a Model Treaty that September, concluded an alliance with France in 1778, and in 1779 set peace terms and named John Adams to negotiate with Britain.[^s1]

**On the move.** War repeatedly drove Congress out of Philadelphia. It sat in Baltimore from December 20, 1776, to February 27, 1777, and after the British captured Philadelphia it met briefly at Lancaster and then in the York, Pennsylvania, court house from September 30, 1777, to June 27, 1778, before returning to the city.[^s2][^s12]

## Legacy

The Congress that began as a meeting of protesting colonies created many of the first institutions of the United States: its army, its first diplomacy and its founding declaration.[^s1] Its committee drafted the Articles of Confederation in 1776, and Congress sent them to the states at the end of November 1777.[^s10][^s13] The Second Continental Congress continued to meet until Maryland's ratification on March 1, 1781, when the Articles established a new national government and the body continued as the Confederation Congress.[^s1][^s13]

## Connections

Congress **met in** the Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, and was **led by** its president John Hancock.[^s2][^s3] It **led to** the Continental Army under Washington, the Declaration of Independence, the Articles of Confederation and, through its diplomacy, the 1778 alliance with France.[^s1][^s4][^s10] Its 1775 appeal for neutrality **affected** the Haudenosaunee, whose nations Congress first tried to keep out of the war.[^s6][^s9] It built on the First Continental Congress of 1774, which had called it into being.[^s1]
