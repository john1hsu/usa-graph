# Martin Luther King Jr.

Martin Luther King Jr. (1929–1968) was a Baptist minister who became the most prominent leader of the civil rights movement, joining Black churches' organizing networks to Gandhian nonviolent protest against segregation and disfranchisement.[^s1] He led the Montgomery bus boycott, headed the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), received the 1964 Nobel Peace Prize, and in his last years turned to poverty and the Vietnam War before he was assassinated in Memphis at 39.[^s1][^s7]

## Life and work

**Atlanta and education.** He was born Michael King Jr. in Atlanta on January 15, 1929, and grew up on Auburn Avenue near Ebenezer Baptist Church, which his grandfather Adam Daniel Williams led from 1894 and his father later pastored; in 1934 his father renamed them both Martin Luther King.[^s1] The family was active in Atlanta's NAACP branch, and the Depression made him aware of economic inequality.[^s1] At Morehouse College (1944–1948) he came to see Christianity as a force for social change; he then studied at Crozer Theological Seminary in Pennsylvania and earned a doctorate in systematic theology at Boston University in 1955, though Carson notes that his academic papers included plagiarism.[^s1] He married Coretta Scott in Marion, Alabama, on June 18, 1953.[^s1]

**Montgomery and the SCLC.** In 1954 he became pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church in Montgomery, Alabama.[^s1] After Rosa Parks's arrest in December 1955, local Black leaders chose him to head the Montgomery Improvement Association, which ran a yearlong boycott of the city's segregated buses; Bayard Rustin and other pacifists encouraged his embrace of Gandhian nonviolence.[^s1] After the Supreme Court struck down Alabama's bus segregation, he founded the SCLC in 1957 and became its president, the role the Nobel Prize records list for him.[^s1][^s7] In 1958 Ella Baker came to Atlanta to run SCLC's voter-registration Crusade for Citizenship, and after the 1960 sit-ins she and King called the Shaw University conference that founded the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC).[^s3] King moved to Atlanta in 1960 as co-pastor of Ebenezer; his Albany, Georgia, campaign of 1961–1962 ended without a clear victory.[^s1]

**Birmingham, Washington and Selma.** SCLC's 1963 Birmingham campaign was the largest civil rights protest to that point; King's April arrest produced his *Letter from Birmingham Jail*, and televised police violence against demonstrators in May pushed the Kennedy administration to propose major civil rights legislation.[^s1] On August 28, 1963, he addressed more than 200,000 people at the March on Washington, invoking the nation's founding promises.[^s1] President Lyndon Johnson signed a civil rights law the next year, and in December 1964 King received the Nobel Peace Prize "for his non-violent struggle for civil rights."[^s7] After state police attacked marchers at Selma's Edmund Pettus Bridge on March 7, 1965, King delayed a second march until it had court approval, which some activists criticized; when the march reached Montgomery he spoke at the capitol on March 25, and the campaign helped bring about the Voting Rights Act of 1965.[^s1]

**Surveillance.** The FBI monitored King from December 1955, and in October 1963 Attorney General Robert Kennedy authorized wiretaps on his home and SCLC offices.[^s2] In November 1964 Director J. Edgar Hoover publicly called him the "most notorious liar in the country," and the Bureau sent King an anonymous letter with a recording that his staff read as urging him to kill himself.[^s2] A 1976 Senate committee report concluded that the FBI tried to discredit King himself and that its campaign plainly harmed the movement; surveillance found no evidence of the Communist influence it claimed to be investigating.[^s2]

**Chicago, Vietnam and Memphis.** In 1966 King moved into a Chicago neighborhood to fight poverty and housing discrimination, faced opposition from Mayor Richard Daley, was hit by a stone during a march in Cicero, and won no significant gains.[^s1] On April 4, 1967, at New York's Riverside Church, he condemned the Vietnam War, which cost him influence on federal racial policy and increased FBI scrutiny.[^s1][^s2] He announced the Poor People's Campaign that November and joined the Memphis sanitation workers' strike in 1968; on April 3 he spoke at Mason Temple, and on April 4 he was shot dead on a balcony of the Lorraine Motel.[^s1]

## Legacy

James Earl Ray pleaded guilty to the murder in March 1969, later tried to withdraw the plea, and maintained his innocence until he died in 1998.[^s5] Coretta Scott King founded the King Center for Nonviolent Social Change in Atlanta.[^s1] On November 2, 1983, Public Law 98-144 made King's birthday a federal holiday on the third Monday in January, taking effect in 1986, when the holiday was first observed.[^s4][^s1] Public opinion shifted sharply over time: Gallup found King viewed favorably by 41% of Americans in May 1963 and unfavorably by 63% in August 1966, while 94% viewed him favorably in 2011; a 2023 Pew survey found 81% saying he had a positive impact on the country.[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians debate how much the movement depended on King. Ella Baker rejected his charismatic, leader-centered style, urged SNCC to cultivate group-centered leadership, and argued that the movement made King rather than the reverse.[^s3] Clayborne Carson's biography credits King as a moderating force within an increasingly divided Black militancy while stressing his tactical tensions with younger SNCC activists and his limited influence in Northern cities.[^s1]

His death is also disputed. In December 1999 a Tennessee civil jury in *King v. Jowers* found that Loyd Jowers and others, including government agencies, had conspired to kill him.[^s5] The Justice Department's June 2000 investigation found "no reliable evidence" for those claims, judged the trial heavily reliant on hearsay, and noted that the 1979 House Select Committee on Assassinations had also concluded Ray was the killer, while suggesting his brothers might have helped him.[^s5] The Nobel Prize's own summary of King's life says it has not been determined whether his killer acted alone.[^s7]

## Connections

King **took part in** the civil rights movement and its signature campaigns: the **Montgomery bus boycott**, the **March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom**, the **Selma-to-Montgomery marches** and the **Memphis sanitation strike**.[^s1] He was a **member of**, and the founding president of, the **Southern Christian Leadership Conference**.[^s7][^s1] His work connects to Ella Baker and SNCC, to Rosa Parks, to the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and to the FBI's campaign against him.[^s3][^s1][^s2] The federal holiday in his name was created by Public Law 98-144.[^s4]
