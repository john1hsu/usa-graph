# Shelby County v. Holder

*Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) is the Supreme Court case that ended the Voting Rights Act's system of federal "preclearance" as it had worked since 1965. Ruling 5 to 4 on June 25, 2013, the Court held that Section 4(b), the formula deciding which states and counties had to get federal approval before changing their voting rules, was unconstitutional.[^s1][^s2] It did not strike down the preclearance requirement in Section 5 or the nationwide ban on racial discrimination in Section 2, but without a formula Section 5 no longer applies anywhere.[^s1][^s3]

## Background

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 attacked the literacy tests, intimidation and other devices that Southern states had used for decades to keep Black citizens from voting. Section 5 required "covered" jurisdictions to obtain approval from the Attorney General or a three-judge federal court in Washington, D.C., before any voting change took effect. In 1965 the formula covered Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina and Virginia, along with 39 North Carolina counties and one county in Arizona.[^s1] Congress extended the formula in 1970 and 1975, updating it with 1968 and then 1972 election data, but never again.[^s1]

By 2013 nine states were wholly covered, including Alaska, Arizona and Texas, and parts of six others, such as California, New York and South Dakota.[^s3] In 2006 Congress renewed preclearance for another 25 years, by votes of 390 to 33 in the House and 98 to 0 in the Senate, keeping the old formula and strengthening Section 5's standard.[^s1] Three years later, in *Northwest Austin Municipal Utility District No. One v. Holder*, the Court let a Texas utility district apply to "bail out" of coverage and so avoided ruling on the Act's constitutionality, while signaling serious doubts about it. Congress did not respond.[^s1]

Shelby County was covered because Alabama was. It could not bail out, because the Justice Department had recently objected to voting changes within the county, so in 2010 it sued Attorney General Eric Holder, asking a court to declare Sections 4(b) and 5 unconstitutional on their face. The district court and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit both upheld the Act.[^s1][^s2]

## The decision

Chief Justice John G. Roberts Jr. wrote for the majority, joined by Justices Antonin Scalia, Anthony Kennedy, Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito.[^s1][^s2] He reasoned that the Constitution assumes a basic principle of equal sovereignty among the states, so a law treating some states differently must be justified by present conditions rather than history. The formula, he wrote, still turned on tests and turnout from four decades earlier, although conditions had changed dramatically: by 2004, census-based figures showed Black and white registration in Alabama nearly equal, and Black registration in Mississippi slightly higher than white.[^s1] The Court stressed that it issued no holding on Section 5 itself and that Congress could draft a new formula based on current conditions.[^s1] Justice Thomas, concurring, would have struck down Section 5 as well.[^s1]

Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg dissented, joined by Justices Stephen Breyer, Sonia Sotomayor and Elena Kagan. She argued that the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments give Congress, not the Court, the choice of remedies, and that Congress's 2006 record documented "subtler second-generation barriers" such as discriminatory redistricting.[^s1][^s2] She pointed to Calera, a city in Shelby County whose redistricting plan would have eliminated its only majority-Black council district. Abandoning preclearance because it was working, she wrote, was like "throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet."[^s1]

## Legacy

The practical result was immediate: covered jurisdictions no longer needed federal approval for voting changes. Preclearance can now be imposed only if a federal court orders it under Section 3 after finding certain constitutional violations; the Justice Department soon asked a court to do so for Texas.[^s3][^s4] Voting-rights protection shifted to Section 2 lawsuits, which challenge rules after they take effect.[^s4]

Congress has not enacted a new formula. The House passed the John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act in the 116th and 117th Congresses, but in November 2021 the Senate failed to invoke cloture, and later versions have not moved beyond introduction.[^s4] In *Louisiana v. Callais* (2026) the Court narrowed Section 2 in redistricting cases, adding to the debate over whether Section 2 alone is adequate.[^s4]

## Debates and interpretations

How much the ruling changed voting access remains disputed. The U.S. Commission on Civil Rights, in a report adopted unanimously in 2018, found that strict voter ID laws, polling-place closures, early-voting cuts and voter-roll purges followed in formerly covered places, that some elections were held under laws courts later found intentionally discriminatory, and that Section 2 litigation was slow and costly; it urged Congress to restore preclearance.[^s5] Testifying to the same Commission, Hans von Spakovsky of the Heritage Foundation argued the opposite: that Black registration and turnout in formerly covered states had matched or exceeded white rates, that Shelby County itself had been covered only because of Alabama, and that Section 3 lets courts impose preclearance where discrimination is proven, so a new Section 5 was unnecessary.[^s6]

## Connections

The case **interprets** the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the background needed to understand it, and specifically the coverage formula in Section 4(b).[^s1] It also **interprets** Congress's power to enforce the Fifteenth Amendment, which the parties disputed.[^s2] Its majority opinion was **authored by** Chief Justice John Roberts,[^s1] and it was **influenced by** *Northwest Austin* (2009), whose warning the Court said Congress had ignored.[^s1] The case arose in and **affected** Alabama, one of the six states covered in full since 1965.[^s1] It **led to** proposals for a new formula, chiefly the John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act, which has not been enacted.[^s4] It is the Court's main departure from *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* (1966), which had upheld preclearance, and it sits alongside *Louisiana v. Callais* in the history of voting rights in the United States.[^s3][^s4]
