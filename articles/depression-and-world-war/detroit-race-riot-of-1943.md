# 1943 Detroit race riot

On the evening of Sunday, June 20, 1943, fighting between Black and white young people on Belle Isle, Detroit's island park in the Detroit River, set off more than a day of racial violence on the World War II home front. By the time federal troops took control of the streets, 34 people were dead — 25 Black and 9 white — about 675 to 700 were injured, and property damage was put at about $2 million.[^s1][^s2] Detroit police killed 17 of the Black victims and none of the white ones.[^s1][^s3] The riot exposed how sharply segregation in housing, work and policing divided a city busy with war production.[^s2][^s3]

## Background

War production drew a flood of workers to Detroit. A 1944 analysis by Earl Brown estimated that about 500,000 people moved into the area between mid-1940 and mid-1943, some 50,000 of them Black, many from the South.[^s5] Housing did not keep up. The city's roughly 200,000 Black residents were confined by discrimination to overcrowded, subdivided buildings in a few districts, chiefly Paradise Valley and Black Bottom on the East Side.[^s1][^s5]

Attempts to open new housing to Black families met force. When Black war workers' families tried to move into the federally funded Sojourner Truth Homes in February 1942, white protesters burned a cross and crowds turned them away; Mayor Edward Jeffries Jr. postponed occupancy and federal officials briefly reassigned the project to white tenants. After protests by the Detroit Urban League, the Rev. Charles A. Hill and the young union activist Coleman Young, Black families moved in on April 29, 1942, guarded by more than 1,500 troopers and police.[^s6][^s1]

Workplaces were tense as well. In the first half of 1943 white workers staged "hate strikes" against the hiring or promotion of Black workers in war plants.[^s1][^s5] At the Packard Motor Car Company some 25,000 white workers walked out in a three-day wildcat strike after three Black men were promoted to the aircraft assembly line; Brown counted 26,883 strikers, and he called such walkouts "the fuses that touched off the dynamite."[^s7][^s5]

## What happened

Nearly 100,000 people crowded Belle Isle on the hot Sunday of June 20. Scuffles between Black and white youths grew into brawls, which police had broken up by about midnight.[^s1] Overnight, two false rumors raced across the city: among Black Detroiters, that white men had thrown a Black woman and her baby off the Belle Isle bridge; among white Detroiters, that Black men had raped a white woman near the bridge.[^s1]

In Paradise Valley, around the Forest Social Club and Hastings Street, Black crowds attacked white passers-by and looted white-owned stores. Joseph DeHoratiis, a white physician making a house call there, was beaten to death.[^s1] From about 4 a.m., white mobs gathered on Woodward Avenue near the Roxy Theatre, beat Black patrons leaving it, stoned Black motorists and overturned and burned cars owned by Black residents, often while white police officers watched.[^s1][^s5] Brown described police at the Roxy refusing to help Black patrons leave safely.[^s5]

Black community leaders urged Mayor Jeffries to call for federal troops, and he turned to Governor Harry Kelly.[^s1] Under the Constitution's guarantee to protect states against domestic violence, the President first had to order the rioters to "disperse and retire peacefully to their respective abodes immediately," which Franklin D. Roosevelt did in Proclamation 2588 on June 21, noting that the governor had reported the state could not suppress the violence.[^s4] Troops arrived that evening — accounts range from about 3,500 to more than 6,000 soldiers, with jeeps and armored vehicles — and the streets were largely quiet by midnight.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Brown calculated that about 13 hours passed between the first requests and the troops' arrival, the hours in which most of the killing and burning took place.[^s5]

## Aftermath and legacy

The official reckoning fell heavily on Black Detroiters. About 85 percent of those arrested were Black.[^s3] Governor Kelly's fact-finding committee issued its report in August 1943 and largely ignored the social inequities behind the violence; Brown called it an attempt to whitewash local authorities.[^s1][^s5] Wayne County prosecutor William Dowling privately blamed the NAACP and the Black weekly *Michigan Chronicle*, and the police commissioner defended his department.[^s5] The NAACP answered with *What Caused the Detroit Riot?*, a July 1943 pamphlet by Walter White and Thurgood Marshall, and the Reuther Library notes that investigations blaming Black residents deepened their sense of injustice.[^s2]

Some institutions changed. Late in 1943 Mayor Jeffries appointed an Interracial Committee to investigate discrimination and racial tension and to recommend better city services; it evolved into the Detroit Commission on Community Relations.[^s3] Detroit's NAACP branch grew into one of the largest in the country, and the city became an early center of both civil rights organizing and Black nationalism.[^s8] Long overshadowed in memory by the 1967 uprising, the 1943 riot is now often read as its forerunner, rooted in the same conflicts over housing, jobs and policing.[^s8][^s2]

## Connections

The riot **occurred in** Detroit, beginning on Belle Isle and spreading to Paradise Valley and Woodward Avenue.[^s1] It was **influenced by** the wartime migration of Black southerners to Detroit's war plants, by the fight over the Sojourner Truth Homes and by the Packard hate strike, all of which contemporaries such as Earl Brown named as causes.[^s5] It **led to** Roosevelt's Proclamation 2588 and the deployment of federal troops,[^s4] and to the Interracial Committee that Mayor Jeffries created in its wake.[^s3]
