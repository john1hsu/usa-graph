# New Orleans

New Orleans is the largest city in Louisiana, built on a bend of the lower Mississippi River. The 2020 census counted 383,997 residents, and the Census Bureau estimated 364,136 in July 2023; 55.2 percent of residents identified as Black or African American alone.[^s11] Founded as a French colonial outpost in 1718, it passed through Spanish rule to the United States in 1803 and became one of the nation's great ports, its largest slave market, a crucible of civil rights law and the place where jazz took shape.[^s2][^s3][^s5][^s8]

## Geography

The city sits between the Mississippi River and Lake Pontchartrain.[^s2][^s9] Its French founders built on the natural levees the river had raised along its banks, about 5 to 10 feet above sea level, while the rest of the land was swamp at or near sea level.[^s10] Over the following centuries, levees, lakeshore landfill, drainage canals and pumps reclaimed that swamp, but draining it caused the soil to compact and sink. A U.S. Geological Survey geologist told Congress in October 2005 that more than half the city lay below sea level, some areas by as much as 10 feet, in a bowl-shaped landscape that cannot drain naturally.[^s10] The same testimony tied the region's vulnerability to coastal wetland loss, which averaged about 24 square miles a year in the preceding decade.[^s10] The Census Bureau measured the city's land area at 169.49 square miles in 2020.[^s11]

## History

Long before the French arrived, the area was a crossroads of Indigenous trade and exchange. Choctaw and Chickasaw speakers call it Bulbancha, roughly "the place of other languages," a name reflecting the many peoples — among them Choctaw, Chitimacha, Biloxi, Natchez and others — whose speakers met there and used the trade language Mobilian to communicate.[^s1] Choctaw people lived in the area and still do, and a grassroots revival since 2014 has restored the name to public use.[^s1]

France sent the brothers Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville and Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne de Bienville to the lower Mississippi in 1699. Native guides told Iberville's party about a ridge, now Esplanade Ridge, linking the river to Bayou St. John and Lake Pontchartrain — a valuable shortcut. In 1718 Bienville founded La Nouvelle-Orléans on that site, near the river's mouth, to keep British and Spanish rivals from moving upriver, and in 1722 France made it the capital of French Louisiana.[^s2] The city later passed to Spain. By the early 1800s its port was the outlet for produce from more than a third of the United States, and when Spain refused Americans the right to deposit goods there in 1802, President Thomas Jefferson told his envoy to buy the city. The talks produced the Louisiana Purchase treaty of April 30, 1803, and the U.S. flag was raised in New Orleans on December 20, 1803.[^s3]

On January 8, 1815, American forces under Andrew Jackson defeated a British army below the city; the British commander, Sir Edward Pakenham, was killed.[^s4] The battle came after the Treaty of Ghent was signed on December 24, 1814, but the war legally continued until both governments ratified the treaty about two months later, and British officials had been told to keep fighting unless the United States ratified it unchanged.[^s4]

Slavery shaped the antebellum city. Its slave market was the nation's largest, where an estimated 135,000 people were bought and sold.[^s5] The enslaved population reached 23,448 in 1840, nearly a quarter of all residents, and Louisiana restricted manumission after 1830 and outlawed it in 1857.[^s5]

After the Civil War, the city became a battleground of Reconstruction. On July 30, 1866, white mobs, joined by police, attacked a convention at the Mechanics' Institute that sought to give Black men the vote, killing almost fifty people; General Philip Sheridan called it "an absolute massacre by the police."[^s6] The violence helped bring about Radical Reconstruction and the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship guarantees.[^s6] As white supremacy returned, Creole of color activists including Louis A. Martinet and Rodolphe Desdunes announced the Comité des Citoyens in September 1891 to challenge Louisiana's 1890 Separate Car Law, raising money for the legal test that became Plessy v. Ferguson.[^s7]

Around the turn of the twentieth century, the city's mix of French, Spanish, African, Caribbean, Italian, German and Irish musical traditions, its brass bands, parades and social aid and pleasure clubs, and dancers' demand for livelier music gave rise to jazz. Cornetist Buddy Bolden formed his own band in 1895, Kid Ory led bands in the city from 1908 to 1919, and from about 1914 New Orleans musicians toured widely; Tom Brown took a band to Chicago in 1915.[^s8] The National Park Service notes that for most residents music was "not a luxury as it often is elsewhere."[^s8]

## Today

In late August 2005, Hurricane Katrina came ashore in southeast Louisiana and Mississippi as a Category 3 storm. Breaks in the levees separating New Orleans from Lake Pontchartrain flooded at least 80 percent of the city by August 31; the storm killed 1,833 people across the region and caused about $108 billion in damage, at the time the costliest U.S. hurricane.[^s9] Census estimates show the population falling from 383,997 in 2020 to 364,136 in 2023, and the subsidence and wetland loss described by federal scientists remain central to the challenge of protecting the city.[^s10][^s11] The National Park Service interprets the city's history through Jean Lafitte National Historical Park and Preserve and New Orleans Jazz National Historical Park.[^s4][^s8]

## Connections

New Orleans lies in Louisiana (`locatedIn`), as the Census Bureau's place records show.[^s11] It was founded by Bienville (`foundedBy`) and borders the Mississippi River and Lake Pontchartrain (`borders`), the two waterways that made its site valuable and that frame its flood risk.[^s2][^s9] Its port drove the Louisiana Purchase, and it saw the Battle of New Orleans, the 1866 massacre and the founding of the Comité des Citoyens behind Plessy v. Ferguson.[^s3][^s4][^s6][^s7] It is the home of the domestic slave trade's largest market and the birthplace of jazz, and it is linked to the Choctaw Nation through the name Bulbancha.[^s1][^s5][^s8]
