# Lattimer massacre

On September 10, 1897, a sheriff's posse fired on a column of striking anthracite miners on the road to Lattimer, a mining village near Hazleton in Luzerne County, Pennsylvania. At least nineteen marchers were killed and about thirty-two wounded, and nearly all of the dead and wounded were Polish, Slovak or Lithuanian immigrants.[^s1] Martin and his deputies were tried for murder and acquitted, but the killings became a turning point for the immigrant miners of northeastern Pennsylvania and for the United Mine Workers of America (UMW).[^s2]

## Background

Anthracite (hard coal) mining in northeastern Pennsylvania depended by the 1890s on recent immigrants from southern and eastern Europe, who worked for low wages in dangerous conditions and met widespread nativist hostility.[^s2] On August 21, 1897, the state enacted the Campbell Act, which taxed coal operators three cents a day for every non-citizen they employed; the UMW, then focused on native-born and naturalized miners, backed it.[^s5] Shackel and Roller note that the UMW's organizer in the district, John Fahy, supported the tax,[^s4] even as he persuaded immigrant miners at Harwood to pay dues and form a union local.[^s3]

A strike began in late August at the Honey Brook colliery in McAdoo and spread through the Hazleton area; by early September about 5,000 workers were out.[^s3] At Harwood and Lattimer, the mines of Calvin Pardee, workers demanded higher pay, lower prices for the supplies the company sold them, and the removal of company-town staff including the company doctor; Pardee refused.[^s3]

## What happened

On September 10, miners set out from Harwood to bring out the workers at Lattimer. The column began with about 250 men and grew to about 400; they had agreed not to carry firearms or clubs.[^s4] Luzerne County Sheriff James Martin had deputized a posse of roughly 86 to 90 men, many of them prominent Anglo-American townspeople and agents of the coal companies, armed with rifles and shotguns and joined by company police.[^s5] [^s4] [^s3] Martin first stopped the marchers at West Hazleton, citing a ban on demonstrations, but the West Hazleton police chief intervened on the strikers' behalf.[^s3]

Outside Lattimer the posse blocked the road again. After a scuffle over an American flag at the head of the column, a shot rang out and the deputies began firing into the unarmed crowd.[^s2] [^s3] Nineteen men died that afternoon and as many as five or six more died of their wounds in the following days; over a dozen marchers were shot in the back as they fled.[^s5] [^s4] None of the victims was a U.S. citizen.[^s4]

## Aftermath and legacy

Governor Daniel Hastings sent about 2,500 militia to the area, and funerals drew as many as 8,000 mourners.[^s2] Slavic organizations across the country raised relief money, and the Austro-Hungarian ambassador demanded compensation from the United States, which was never paid.[^s1] Warrants were issued for Martin and 78 deputies, and their trial ran from February 1 to March 2, 1898, in Wilkes-Barre.[^s3] Prosecutors used the death of one striker, Michael Cheslock, as a test case; no juror was Slavic, the defense played on anti-immigrant fears, and despite about 140 witnesses to the shooting of unarmed men, every defendant was acquitted.[^s4] The legislature placed no new limits on the private Coal and Iron Police.[^s2]

The union drew the opposite lesson. Within four months more than 15,000 anthracite workers had joined the UMW, and the massacre showed that Slavic and Italian miners would not be the docile workforce employers had expected.[^s1] Under John Mitchell, president from 1898 to 1908, the UMW grew from 34,000 to 300,000 members, led about 125,000 anthracite workers out in 1900, and struck again from May to October 1902, a strike that ended after President Theodore Roosevelt intervened and created the Anthracite Coal Strike Commission.[^s1] Mitchell urged unity across ethnic lines, reportedly telling miners: "The coal you dig isn't Slavish or Polish, or Irish coal. It's just coal."[^s5]

For decades the killings were remembered locally and little elsewhere; some newspapers still called them "the Lattimer riots" in the 1930s, and wider interest revived only with the civil rights movement of the 1960s.[^s5] Governor Milton Shapp declared 1972 Lattimer Labor Memorial Year, and on September 10, 1972, a memorial boulder and state marker were dedicated at the site, with farm-labor leader Cesar Chavez among the speakers.[^s5] [^s2] On the centennial in 1997, new state markers went up and Representative Paul Kanjorski entered a tribute in the Congressional Record.[^s5] [^s6] In 2010 archaeologists surveyed the site, and the massacre is now invoked by people on different sides of local debates over immigration in Hazleton.[^s4]

## Debates and interpretations

The trial records are missing from the Luzerne County Courthouse, so accounts rest on partisan newspapers and a defense lawyer's 1913 memoir, and many details remain contested.[^s4] Historians still disagree about whether Sheriff Martin gave an order to fire.[^s3] Archaeologists Paul Shackel and Michael Roller found period pistol bullets at the site in 2010 and argue that the evidence complicates the traditional picture of a nervous, untrained posse firing in panic, raising questions about the deputies' intent.[^s4] Shackel also notes that some accounts claimed perhaps 150 marchers were armed, while the state's 1997 marker describes them as unarmed.[^s5] Scholars explain the event's long absence from national memory in different ways: Shackel and Roller stress the victims' ethnicity and the racial attitudes of the era, while Shackel's later account adds the site's rural remoteness and coal operators' interest in forgetting the strikes.[^s4] [^s5]
