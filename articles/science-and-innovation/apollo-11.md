# Apollo 11

Apollo 11 was the NASA mission that first landed people on the Moon. On July 20, 1969, Neil Armstrong and Edwin "Buzz" Aldrin set the lunar module *Eagle* down on the Sea of Tranquility while Michael Collins circled overhead in the command module *Columbia*; a few hours later Armstrong became the first human being to step onto another world.[^s1][^s2] The eight-day flight fulfilled a national goal set eight years earlier and became one of the defining moments of the Cold War era and of twentieth-century science and engineering.[^s3]

## Background

The Moon race grew out of Cold War rivalry. The Soviet Union put the first artificial satellite, Sputnik 1, into orbit in 1957, and in April 1961 cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin became the first person to orbit Earth, deepening American fears of falling behind.[^s3] On May 25, 1961, President John F. Kennedy told Congress that the nation should commit itself to "landing a man on the moon and returning him safely to the earth" before the decade was out, and asked for an additional $7 billion to $9 billion for space over five years.[^s4] He pressed the case again at Rice University on September 12, 1962. Kennedy also proposed a joint U.S.–Soviet Moon mission in June 1961, which Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev rejected.[^s3]

The goal was never uncontroversial. Some doubted it could be done; others argued the money was needed at home. Some Americans wrote to the president asking that qualified people be admitted to the space program regardless of race or gender, and critics pointed to the astronaut corps' lack of diversity.[^s3] The program also relied on Wernher von Braun, the rocket engineer who had been a Nazi Party member and weapons researcher in wartime Germany, which made his central role controversial.[^s3] Public support was more conditional than nostalgia suggests: in a 1965 poll only 39 percent of Americans said the country should do everything possible, regardless of cost, to reach the Moon first.[^s9]

## What happened

The day before launch, the Rev. Ralph Abernathy led hundreds of Poor People's Campaign demonstrators to demand that the nation also address hunger and poverty.[^s3] At 9:32 a.m. EDT on July 16, 1969, a Saturn V rocket lifted Apollo 11 off Launch Pad 39A at Cape Kennedy, Florida, with Armstrong as commander, Collins as command module pilot and Aldrin as lunar module pilot.[^s1] On July 19 a burn of the main engine placed the joined spacecraft in lunar orbit.[^s1]

On July 20 Armstrong and Aldrin undocked in *Eagle*. Armstrong partly took over manual control and landed about four miles beyond the planned point, touching down at 4:17 p.m. EDT with roughly 30 seconds of fuel to spare.[^s1][^s2] At 10:56 p.m. EDT he stepped onto the surface, and an audience estimated at 500 million to 650 million people watched on television.[^s2][^s1][^s3] His first words are recorded as "That's one small step for (a) man; one giant leap for mankind." Listeners heard "for man"; Armstrong said afterwards he meant to say "a man," and NASA's surface journal notes the article is not audible in the recordings.[^s5]

Aldrin joined him about 20 minutes later. During a moonwalk of about two and a half hours they planted a U.S. flag, set up a seismometer and a laser ranging retroreflector, collected surface samples, and took photographs, ranging up to about 300 feet from the lander.[^s1][^s2] They left a plaque on one of *Eagle*'s legs declaring that the visitors came in peace for all mankind, along with a disk carrying goodwill messages from 73 countries.[^s1][^s2] After 21 hours and 36 minutes on the surface they lifted off on July 21 and redocked with *Columbia*.[^s1][^s7] The crew splashed down in the Pacific Ocean, off Hawaii, at 12:50 p.m. EDT on July 24 and was recovered by the aircraft carrier USS *Hornet*.[^s1][^s6]

## Aftermath and legacy

Ten more astronauts walked on the Moon in the three and a half years that followed.[^s2] Looking back, Armstrong credited the "hundreds of thousands" of people whose work made the flight possible.[^s2] President Lyndon B. Johnson had renamed Florida's launch center for Kennedy after his assassination in November 1963, and the Park Service notes that Apollo-era work is linked to advances such as integrated circuits, water purification and fire-resistant materials.[^s3]

The mission is still doing science. The retroreflector — an aluminum frame holding 100 fused-silica prisms — returns laser pulses fired from Earth, including from the Apache Point Observatory in New Mexico, letting researchers measure the Earth–Moon distance to about a millimetre and test general relativity and the Moon's interior.[^s7] NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed the descent stage left at Tranquility Base and the footpaths the astronauts wore into the soil.[^s8] That physical record refutes the claim that the landing was faked, a belief that Gallup and Time/CNN polls in the 1990s found among about 6 percent of Americans.[^s9] Public enthusiasm for paying for spaceflight remained mixed after 1969: in 1979, 53 percent said the program's benefits did not justify its costs.[^s9]

*Columbia* toured American cities and reached the Smithsonian in 1971; it has been on display at the National Air and Space Museum in Washington since the museum opened in 1976.[^s6]

## Connections

Apollo 11 was **part of** NASA's **Apollo program**, the effort that carried crews to the Moon into the 1970s.[^s3] It **occurred during** the **Cold War**, the rivalry with the Soviet Union that made space a contest of prestige.[^s3] The mission was **led by** its commander, **Neil Armstrong**.[^s1] It launched from Cape Kennedy, so it **occurred in** **Florida**.[^s1] The goal it achieved was set by **John F. Kennedy**, whose 1961 message to Congress **influenced** the whole program.[^s4] The command module now belongs to the **Smithsonian Institution**, which first put it on display in 1971.[^s6] With the Wright brothers' first flight of 1903, it is one of the landmark moments of American flight.
