# Presidency of the United States

The presidency is the office at the head of the executive branch of the federal government. Article II of the Constitution vests the executive power in a single president, who is chosen through the Electoral College for a four-year term and serves at once as chief executive, commander in chief and the nation's voice in foreign affairs.[^s1][^s6] A president must be a natural-born citizen, at least 35 years old and a U.S. resident for at least 14 years.[^s6] The office is one of three branches in the Constitution's separation of powers, and nearly every major presidential power is checked by Congress or the courts.[^s1][^s6]

## Origins

The framers who met in Philadelphia in 1787 had to decide what kind of executive to create. On June 1, 1787, James Wilson moved that the executive consist of a single person. Edmund Randolph strongly opposed the idea, calling a unitary executive "the foetus of monarchy," while Wilson answered that one person would be the best safeguard against tyranny; the delegates agreed only that there would be a national executive and postponed the rest.[^s3] The convention eventually chose one president. Defending that choice during ratification, Alexander Hamilton wrote in Federalist No. 70 (March 18, 1788) that "Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government," and listed unity, duration, adequate support and competent powers as its ingredients.[^s4]

George Washington took the first oath of office on April 30, 1789, on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York City.[^s5] Because the Constitution said little about how the office should work day to day, his choices became precedents: he recommended the plain title "Mr. President," organized a cabinet, proposed legislation to Congress, delivered the State of the Union in person and stressed the civilian character of the office. He also retired after eight years, starting a two-term tradition.[^s5]

The founding-era office was bound up with slavery. At least thirteen presidents, from Washington to Ulysses S. Grant, enslaved people at some point in their lives; at least nine brought enslaved people to work in the White House or hired them out there; and enslaved laborers helped at every stage of building the White House itself.[^s10]

## Powers and structure

**Powers.** Article II makes the president commander in chief of the armed forces and gives the office the power to grant pardons and to require written opinions from department heads. Treaties and the appointment of senior officers are shared with the Senate. The president must see that the laws are faithfully executed, reports to Congress on the state of the union, recommends legislation and receives ambassadors.[^s1] Through Article I the president can veto bills, but Congress may override a veto by a two-thirds vote of both houses.[^s6] Presidents also issue executive orders that direct executive officers or clarify and carry out existing laws.[^s6] Because the vesting clause does not list the full extent of "executive Power," scholars and officials have debated the scope of the president's implied or residual powers since the early republic.[^s11]

**Election and term.** Electors originally voted without distinguishing between president and vice president; the Twelfth Amendment, ratified June 15, 1804, required separate ballots for each office. The Twentieth Amendment, ratified January 23, 1933, ends each term at noon on January 20.[^s2] The two-term custom held until Franklin D. Roosevelt won a third and a fourth term, and the Twenty-second Amendment, passed by Congress on March 21, 1947, and ratified February 27, 1951, now provides that no one may be elected president more than twice.[^s5][^s2]

**Succession and removal.** When William Henry Harrison died in April 1841, John Tyler became the first vice president to succeed a president who died in office. Some argued that he was only an acting president, but Tyler insisted on the full powers of the office, setting a precedent for orderly transfers of power.[^s7][^s8] The Twenty-fifth Amendment, ratified February 10, 1967, wrote the rule into the Constitution and added procedures for filling a vice-presidential vacancy and for a president who is unable to serve.[^s2] Under Article II, Section 4, a president can be removed by impeachment and conviction for treason, bribery or other high crimes and misdemeanors.[^s1]

**The executive branch.** The president leads a branch whose cabinet is made up of the heads of the 15 executive departments, and which, according to the White House's archived description of the early 2020s, employed more than 4 million people including the armed forces.[^s6] The office's own staff grew in the twentieth century: in 1937 the Brownlow Committee, chaired by Louis Brownlow, told Franklin D. Roosevelt that "The President needs help," and in 1939 Roosevelt created the Executive Office of the President, the president's immediate staff.[^s9][^s6]

## Connections

The presidency is established by the United States Constitution, in Article II.[^s1] It is part of the federal government of the United States and works alongside Congress, which can override vetoes and confirm appointments.[^s6] James Wilson proposed the single executive at the Constitutional Convention, and Hamilton defended it in The Federalist.[^s3][^s4] George Washington's conduct in office shaped its customs, including the two-term tradition that the Twenty-second Amendment later made law.[^s5][^s2] The Electoral College chooses each president, and the vice presidency supplies the successor when the office falls vacant.[^s2][^s7]
