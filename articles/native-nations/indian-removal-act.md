# Indian Removal Act

The Indian Removal Act was the federal law, signed by President Andrew Jackson on May 28, 1830, that authorized the president to negotiate with Native nations living east of the Mississippi River to exchange their homelands for land in the West.[^s2] Written as an offer of exchange, it became the legal framework for one of the largest forced migrations in American history: within little more than a decade the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole nations had been pushed out of the Southeast, and many northern nations were moved as well.[^s6][^s7]

## Background

Removal was older than the law. When Georgia ceded its western lands in 1802, the federal government promised to extinguish Indian title within the state, and after the Louisiana Purchase Thomas Jefferson urged eastern nations to trade their lands for western ones; few agreed.[^s6] After Andrew Jackson's forces defeated the Creeks at Horseshoe Bend in 1814, the Creeks surrendered more than twenty million acres, and over the next decade Jackson helped negotiate nine of the eleven major removal treaties.[^s3]

The southeastern nations resisted on their own terms. The Cherokees adopted a written constitution in 1827, which angered removal advocates in Georgia.[^s5] Between 1827 and 1831 the Georgia legislature extended state jurisdiction over Cherokee territory, passed laws meant to abolish the Cherokee government and set up lotteries to give Cherokee land to white Georgians.[^s5] The discovery of gold on Native land in 1828 sharpened the demand for removal.[^s4][^s5]

## What it says

The act ran to eight sections.[^s2] It let the president divide unsettled federal land west of the Mississippi into districts for nations that chose to exchange their lands, and to trade those districts for land the nations held within a state or territory.[^s2] The president could assure them that the new country would be theirs permanently — with the land reverting to the United States if a nation died out or abandoned it — and was to pay individuals for improvements such as houses and fields.[^s2] He could fund the journey and a year of support after arrival, protect the emigrants from interference, and oversee them in the West, provided no existing treaty was violated. Congress appropriated $500,000.[^s2] The law did not require that the western land be of equal value.[^s4]

Opposition was strong and organized. The missionary Jeremiah Evarts, writing as "William Penn," argued against removal, and Catharine Beecher urged women to petition Congress, though many critics objected mainly to Georgia's coercion rather than to removal itself.[^s7] The bill passed the Senate 28 to 19 and the House by just 102 to 97.[^s7]

## Legacy

Jackson told Congress in December 1830 that the policy was nearing a "happy consummation," presenting removal as generous to Native peoples and as a way to strengthen the southwestern frontier and let the states grow.[^s1] By the end of his presidency he had signed almost seventy removal treaties, and nearly 50,000 eastern Native people had been moved to Indian Territory, later eastern Oklahoma; about 25 million acres opened to white settlement, which the National Archives notes also extended slavery across the South.[^s1][^s3]

The Cherokees fought removal in court. In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) the Supreme Court called them a "domestic dependent nation" and declined to decide their case; in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) it held that Georgia had violated Cherokee sovereignty, but Jackson refused to enforce the ruling.[^s5] In December 1835 a minority Treaty Party led by Major Ridge, John Ridge and Elias Boudinot signed the Treaty of New Echota without the authority of Principal Chief John Ross or the Cherokee government. Ross gathered about 16,000 signatures against it, yet the Senate ratified it by a single vote.[^s5][^s4] In May 1838, under orders from President Martin Van Buren, soldiers began confining Cherokees in stockades and then marched them about 800 miles west on what the Cherokee remember as the trail where they cried.[^s4][^s5] Estimates of the dead range from more than 3,000 to between 4,000 and 5,000.[^s4][^s5][^s3]

The other nations suffered parallel removals: the Choctaws from 1831, most Creeks in 1836 and the Chickasaws in 1837–38.[^s6] The Seminoles declared the act unlawful and fought the Second Seminole War of 1835–1842; a small number were never removed from Florida.[^s4][^s3] Northern nations including the Delawares, Ho-Chunks, Potawatomis, Sauks and Mesquakies, Shawnees and Wyandots were also moved west.[^s7] Removal did not end these nations: the Cherokees adopted a new constitution at Tahlequah in 1839, and the Cherokee Nation numbered about 450,000 citizens by 2024 (Ostler, NEH, 2024).[^s4][^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

**Motives.** Jackson framed removal as benevolent, arguing that it would free Native nations from state power and let them govern themselves under their own customs.[^s1] Historians of the Southeast emphasize instead the pressure of Georgia's land lotteries, the gold rush and the federal promise of 1802.[^s5][^s6]

**Whose policy.** Popular memory often treats removal as Jackson's personal project. Andrew Frank and Jeffrey Ostler stress that it began under Jefferson and Monroe and reflected a broad national consensus, and Ostler cautions against treating it as a story only of the Cherokees or only of the South.[^s6][^s7]

**Scale and naming.** Counts differ with scope: about 50,000 under Jackson's treaties, about 70,000 from the southeastern nations, and about 88,000 people removed in all in the 1830s and 1840s, of whom Ostler estimates 12,000 to 17,000 died.[^s3][^s6][^s7] Ostler notes that many historians describe removal as ethnic cleansing; he argues that the death toll and the decision to continue the policy once its effects were known make genocide the more accurate term.[^s7]

## Connections

The act was enacted by the United States Congress (`enactedBy`) and signed by Andrew Jackson (`signedBy`).[^s2] It affected the Cherokee Nation, the Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek) and Chickasaw nations (`affected`), and through the treaties it authorized it led to the Treaty of New Echota, the Trail of Tears and the Second Seminole War (`ledTo`).[^s5][^s6][^s3][^s4] It belongs to the longer federal policy of Indian removal (`broader`), and its background includes the Louisiana Purchase, which gave the United States the western claim it offered in exchange (`requires`).[^s6] The Supreme Court's Cherokee cases and the principle of tribal sovereignty are closely related nodes.[^s5]
