# Manifest Destiny

Manifest Destiny was the belief, held by many white Americans in the 1840s, that the United States had a God-given right and duty to spread across North America.[^s9][^s2] The phrase was coined in 1845, but the confidence behind it was older, and it was used to justify the annexation of Texas, the division of the Oregon Country, the war with Mexico and the seizure of Native nations' lands.[^s2][^s7] It was a slogan for an outlook rather than a statute or treaty, and Americans argued about it bitterly at the time.[^s1][^s2]

## Origins

The phrase first appeared in "Annexation," an unsigned essay in the July/August 1845 issue of the *United States Magazine and Democratic Review*.[^s1] The essay argued that Texas now belonged to the nation, and that Britain and France had meddled in the question to check what it called "our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence."[^s1] It also predicted that American settlers pouring into Mexican California would soon break it away from Mexico.[^s1] The essay is usually credited to the magazine's editor, John L. O'Sullivan, who wrote much of the magazine, though its editors note that some scholars credit another writer.[^s1] The Smithsonian's teaching guide says O'Sullivan used the phrase to promote both the annexation of Texas and the acquisition of Oregon.[^s7]

The idea itself had deeper roots. The Smithsonian guide connects it to American exceptionalism, the belief that the nation held a special place in the world, going back to the 1630 sermon in which Puritan leader John Winthrop said the colonists would be watched as a city upon a hill.[^s7] OpenStax describes the westward movement after 1800 as driven by Americans' certainty of their right and duty to control the continent and spread what they saw as a superior culture.[^s3]

## Significance

Manifest Destiny shaped a burst of territorial growth in the 1840s. In 1844 voters elected James K. Polk, a slaveholder from Tennessee, who pledged to annex Texas and take Oregon; in December 1845 he formally accepted the annexation of Texas as a slave state.[^s2] The Office of the Historian calls Polk a supporter of Manifest Destiny who also had his eye on the Mexican Southwest and California.[^s4] In the Pacific Northwest, where the United States and Britain had shared occupation since 1818, Polk compromised: the Oregon Treaty, signed June 15, 1846, set the boundary at the 49th parallel, and the Senate approved it 41–14 on June 18.[^s5][^s4] Against Mexico there was no compromise. Congress declared war on May 12, 1846, and the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo transferred California, New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada, Utah and parts of Colorado and Wyoming.[^s2]

The idea carried an explicit racial meaning. OpenStax describes it as the belief in "the divine right and duty of White Americans to seize and settle the American West."[^s2] In Texas, Americans treated Tejano and Native residents with contempt, and in 1839 President Mirabeau B. Lamar began a campaign of ethnic cleansing to drive all Native nations out of the republic.[^s9] Wartime enthusiasm drew on claims that Mexicans were lazy and controlled by a corrupt Catholic clergy, and some expansionists wanted Mexican land but not its mixed-race, Catholic people.[^s2] For Native nations, the Smithsonian guide notes, the ideology justified violent displacement and the taking of land by force.[^s7]

The idea was also contested. Annexing Texas was controversial because it would add slave territory, and John Quincy Adams came to see it as a plot to expand slavery.[^s1] A small but vocal antislavery faction and many Whigs denounced the Mexican War, and Abraham Lincoln and others in Congress issued the Spot Resolutions questioning it.[^s2] By opening vast new territory, expansion sharpened the slavery debates that led to the Civil War.[^s3]

The idea is still remembered through images such as John Gast's 1872 painting *American Progress*, reproduced about 1873 as a chromolithograph by George A. Crofutt, who offered it free to subscribers of his magazine; one of its alternate titles was "Manifest destiny." The Library of Congress describes an "allegorical female figure of America leading pioneers westward" past Native Americans and bison.[^s6][^s3]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what happened but differ on what the idea meant. One reading takes its promoters at their word. The Teaching American History editors present O'Sullivan's version as a mostly peaceful process of Americanization in which settlers would carry democratic institutions with them and territories would ask to join the Union.[^s1] A second reading, reflected in OpenStax and the Smithsonian guide, stresses that the language of mission covered a program of racial conquest, slavery's expansion and the dispossession of Native peoples and Mexicans.[^s2][^s7]

The phrase's authorship is also debated. Biographer Linda S. Hudson, in a book published by the Texas State Historical Association, argues that the journalist Jane McManus Storm Cazneau, who wrote as Cora Montgomery and was a staff writer for O'Sullivan, coined it.[^s8] Others still attribute the unsigned essay to O'Sullivan.[^s1]

## Connections

The 1845 "Annexation" essay introduced the phrase, which is usually credited to O'Sullivan.[^s1] The Smithsonian guide ties the idea to older beliefs in American exceptionalism, and it says the doctrine was used to justify displacing Native nations.[^s7] Expansionist pressure over Oregon shaped the 1846 Oregon Treaty.[^s4][^s5] The same spirit drove the annexation of Texas and the Mexican–American War, and gold was found in California just as that land passed to the United States, setting off the Gold Rush.[^s2] Gast's *American Progress* turned the idea into a lasting image of white settlers advancing west as Native peoples are driven away.[^s6][^s3]
