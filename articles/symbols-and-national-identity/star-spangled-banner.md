# The Star-Spangled Banner

"The Star-Spangled Banner" is the national anthem of the United States. Federal law designates its words and music as the anthem and asks people to stand facing the flag while it is played, with a hand over the heart or, for those in uniform and for veterans, a military salute.[^s4] Francis Scott Key wrote it in September 1814, after watching the British bombardment of Fort McHenry at Baltimore during the War of 1812, and it was sung for more than a century before Congress made it the anthem in 1931.[^s1][^s5]

## Origins

In August 1814 British troops had routed American forces at Bladensburg, where Key, a Georgetown lawyer and militia volunteer, served as an aide, and had burned Washington.[^s1][^s9] When the British seized Dr. William Beanes of Upper Marlboro, Key went with prisoner-exchange agent John Skinner, with President Madison's approval, to seek his release. They sailed on a truce boat to the British fleet. The British agreed to free Beanes but kept all three under guard so they could not warn Baltimore of the coming attack.[^s1] Musicologist Mark Clague stresses that Key was aboard his own American truce ship during the battle, not imprisoned on a British warship.[^s7]

From the Patapsco River, Key watched the bombardment of Fort McHenry on September 13, 1814, and through that night. At dawn on the 14th the British withdrew, and the fort's huge garrison flag, 30 by 42 feet, still flew.[^s1] The flag had been sewn by Mary Pickersgill, an experienced maker of ship and signal flags, with her daughter, two nieces and Grace Wisher, a 13-year-old African American indentured servant; Pickersgill herself held enslaved people.[^s1][^s9] Released that day, Key finished his verses in Baltimore.[^s1] By September 17 printed copies headed "Defence of Fort M'Henry" were circulating; within weeks newspapers had printed the song as "The Star-Spangled Banner," at least seventeen of them by mid-October, and Thomas Carr's Baltimore music store issued the first sheet music.[^s1][^s3]

Key wrote the words to fit a tune he chose: "To Anacreon in Heaven," the club song of London's Anacreontic Society, whose melody was most likely shaped by the composer John Stafford Smith. The tune was already popular in the United States, and Key had used it about 1805–1806 for an earlier song, "When the Warrior Returns."[^s2][^s7] The idea that he wrote a poem someone else later set to music is therefore mistaken.[^s7]

## Meaning and practice

The song's four stanzas turn on a question, whether the flag still waves, answered at dawn; Key later asked, in an 1836 speech quoted by the Park Service, whether such a country and its defenders did not "deserve a song."[^s1][^s3] It became one of the most popular patriotic songs of the nineteenth century, and Northerners embraced it during the Civil War because it honoured the flag as an emblem of the Union.[^s3] The Navy adopted it for flag ceremonies in 1889, and Woodrow Wilson named it the anthem of the armed forces in 1916.[^s1][^s5] At Game 1 of the 1918 World Series a Navy band played it and the crowd joined in, starting the tradition of the anthem at ball games.[^s5]

Making it the official anthem took a long campaign. Representative John Linthicum of Maryland introduced H.R. 14 on April 15, 1929, and presented a petition with more than five million signatures and letters from 25 governors. The House passed the bill on April 21, 1930, the Senate on March 3, 1931, and President Herbert Hoover signed it that day without public comment.[^s6][^s5] Opponents had argued that the song was hard to sing, that its tune came from a drinking song, that its militant verses were unsuited to an ally of Britain, and that an anthem should not be set by law.[^s5][^s6] Clague notes that no single official arrangement exists.[^s7] The flag itself passed to the Smithsonian after 1907.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

The third stanza, rarely sung, says that no refuge could save "the hireling and slave" from the terror of flight or the gloom of the grave.[^s3] During the war the British promised freedom to enslaved people who joined them; at least 3,000 men, women and children escaped to British lines, and some men trained on Tangier Island as Colonial Marines.[^s12] Key himself grew up on a plantation worked by enslaved people, held six people in slavery by 1820, opposed abolition and helped found the American Colonization Society, though he also freed several people and represented some enslaved people suing for freedom.[^s9] As the District's federal prosecutor he charged Dr. Reuben Crandall in 1836 for possessing abolitionist pamphlets.[^s11]

The National Park Service states that Key's meaning is unclear: the line may refer to foreign troops serving Britain or to escaped enslaved men in the Colonial Marines.[^s9] Jason Johnson of Morgan State University reads it as celebrating the deaths of Black men fighting for their freedom, and so as pro-slavery.[^s10][^s9] Mark Clague accepts that "slave" points to the Colonial Marines but argues the verse vilifies an enemy army of both white and Black soldiers and does not celebrate slavery.[^s8] Others read "hireling and slave" as Revolutionary-era rhetoric against the whole British force, contrasting paid professionals with volunteer citizen soldiers.[^s10][^s9]

## Connections

The anthem symbolizes the United States, and its status was established by the Act of March 3, 1931, now codified at 36 U.S.C. 301.[^s4] It was introduced by Francis Scott Key, originated in Baltimore and commemorates the Battle of Baltimore and the defence of Fort McHenry.[^s1] Its melody was influenced by "To Anacreon in Heaven."[^s2] It requires the War of 1812 as background and belongs among the national symbols, beside the flag it celebrates; Herbert Hoover signed the 1931 law.[^s1][^s5]
