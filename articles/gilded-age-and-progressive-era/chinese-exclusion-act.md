# Chinese Exclusion Act

The Chinese Exclusion Act was the federal law, approved by President Chester A. Arthur on May 6, 1882, that suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers for ten years and forbade any state or federal court to admit Chinese immigrants to citizenship.[^s1] The National Archives calls it the first significant law restricting immigration into the United States, and the Immigration and Ethnic History Society describes it as the first law to single out a group by race and class for severely limited entry.[^s1][^s3] Extended, tightened and made permanent, exclusion lasted until 1943.[^s1]

## Background

Chinese immigration began with the California Gold Rush of 1849, and the 1868 Burlingame Treaty secured free migration between the two countries.[^s4][^s3] According to the Law Library of Congress, the Chinese immigrant population grew from 35,565 in 1860 to 105,613 in 1880; many were men who meant to return home, and many worked under contract in mining, agriculture and railroad construction, often for lower pay than white workers.[^s4] Employers sometimes hired them to cut costs or to replace strikers, and many white Americans came to see them as rivals for jobs who could not assimilate.[^s4]

Hostility became law locally before it became law nationally. California passed an act in 1858 to stop further Chinese immigration, San Francisco adopted ordinances aimed at Chinese residents in the 1870s, and California's 1879 constitution barred corporations from employing Chinese workers.[^s4] During the depression of the 1870s Denis Kearney's Workingmen's Party rallied crowds with the slogan "The Chinese Must Go."[^s4] The federal Page Act of 1875 barred Chinese and Japanese contract laborers, prostitutes and felons, and because officials presumed Chinese women immoral it kept the Chinese community overwhelmingly male.[^s8][^s6] By 1880 both the Republican and Democratic platforms called for restriction, and the Angell Treaty of that year let the United States limit, but not end, Chinese immigration.[^s6][^s3]

## What it says

Arthur vetoed the first bill on April 4, 1882. A twenty-year suspension, he argued, broke the treaty, which no one had meant to permit a ban lasting nearly a generation; he also objected that registering Chinese residents and making them carry passports was "undemocratic and hostile to the spirit of our institutions."[^s2] Congress then cut the term to ten years.[^s1]

The act, opening with the claim that Chinese laborers endangered good order in certain localities, suspended their entry from 90 days after passage and defined laborers to include skilled and unskilled workers and miners.[^s1] Laborers already in the country on November 17, 1880, could leave and return with a certificate, and merchants and their families, students, teachers, diplomats and travelers could enter with papers from the Chinese government.[^s1][^s3] Ship captains who knowingly landed Chinese laborers faced fines of up to $500 for each and up to a year in prison, and Chinese found unlawfully present could be removed after a court hearing.[^s1][^s3] Section 14 barred Chinese immigrants from naturalization.[^s3]

## Legacy

Congress kept tightening the law. The Scott Act of October 1, 1888, voided the return certificates of Chinese laborers abroad, stranding about 20,000 people; when Chae Chan Ping, a San Francisco resident since about 1875, was turned back with a valid certificate, the Supreme Court upheld the law in 1889, a ruling that helped establish Congress's broad, plenary power over immigration.[^s5][^s6] The Geary Act of 1892 extended exclusion for ten years and required every Chinese resident to carry a certificate of residence or face deportation — a rule that also burdened American-born Chinese citizens — and in 1902 exclusion was made permanent.[^s1][^s6]

Chinese communities resisted and adapted. Immigrants such as Chae Chan Ping contested the laws in court, entered as paper sons and daughters through claimed family ties, or crossed from Canada and Mexico — crossings that helped lead to the creation of the Border Patrol.[^s6][^s5] After 1910 most arrivals were processed at Angel Island in San Francisco Bay.[^s6] Efforts to enforce exclusion laid foundations for later federal immigration enforcement, and the Immigration Act of 1924 later barred all immigrants ineligible for citizenship.[^s3][^s7]

During World War II, with China an ally and Japanese propaganda pointing to exclusion, Congress repealed the exclusion laws in 1943; President Franklin D. Roosevelt called exclusion a "historic mistake."[^s7] The repeal set an ethnic quota of about 105 visas a year and let Chinese immigrants naturalize; racial quotas ended only with the Immigration Act of 1965.[^s7][^s1] In 2011 and 2012 the Senate and the House unanimously adopted resolutions condemning the exclusion laws.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

**Why exclusion?** One explanation, voiced by exclusion's supporters at the time, stresses economic competition: cheaper Chinese labor, strikebreaking and the hard times of the 1870s.[^s4] Other historians emphasize racial ideology. Moon-Ho Jung argues that branding all Chinese workers as "coolies" let activists present exclusion as a defense of free labor after slavery, and historians have disputed the image of Chinese workers as coolies with no control over their wages.[^s8][^s6]

**How much did it matter?** Jung sees the act as setting the precedent for U.S. immigration policy, and the Immigration and Ethnic History Society calls it the first immigration law actively enforced.[^s8][^s3] Andy Urban places it within a broader American effort to sort immigrants into the desirable and the undesirable, a story often missed when immigration history centers on Europeans.[^s6]
