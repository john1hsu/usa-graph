# Gilded Age

The Gilded Age is the name historians give to the decades after the Civil War and Reconstruction, conventionally about 1870 to 1900, when the United States became an industrial power. The label comes from *The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today* (1873), a novel by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner that satirized postwar corruption.[^s3] Historians have kept the name because it captures a contrast between dazzling wealth and progress and the inequality and corruption beneath them.[^s1] Exact dates vary: H. Wayne Morgan placed the era roughly from 1868 to 1900, while other scholars stretch it to 1865–1905.[^s5][^s4]

## Background

The country that entered this period was still mostly rural. The 1870 census counted 38,558,371 people in 37 states, and New York, the largest city, had 942,292 residents.[^s8] By the 1900 census the population had nearly doubled, to 76,212,168 in 45 states, and New York City alone held 3,437,202 people.[^s9] Most Americans born in the 1840s and 1850s grew up in a farming country; as new technology raised output, competition and falling crop prices pushed many young people toward the cities.[^s10]

## Key developments

**Railroads and big business.** National railroad mileage tripled in the twenty years after the Civil War began and tripled again over the next four decades, tying ranches, mines and farms into a national market.[^s1] Chicago's meatpackers turned Texas cattle into nearly 500 million pounds of dressed beef a year by 1885, and Thomas Edison's laboratory pioneered organized industrial research.[^s1] By 1900 the United States was the world's leading manufacturing nation, and a wave of mergers from 1895 to 1904 swallowed about 4,000 companies.[^s1]

**Wealth and inequality.** Fortunes such as those of Cornelius Vanderbilt, John D. Rockefeller, Andrew Carnegie and J. P. Morgan earned their owners the nickname "robber barons." Estimates cited in *The American Yawp* suggest the richest 1 percent held about a quarter of the nation's assets in 1890, and the top 10 percent perhaps 90 percent by 1900.[^s1] Social Darwinism, drawing on Herbert Spencer, offered a justification for that inequality, while Henry George's *Progress and Poverty* (1879) became a bestseller attacking it.[^s1]

**Workers and farmers.** Wages were low and many workers were unemployed for part of each year,[^s10] and with fewer wage earners able to become independent, many joined unions.[^s2] The Knights of Labor passed 700,000 members by 1886, and the period saw more than 20,000 strikes and lockouts.[^s1] Many were crushed: federal troops ended the Great Railroad Strike of 1877; after the 1886 Haymarket bombing in Chicago four anarchists were hanged after a trial that produced no direct evidence against them; the state militia broke the 1892 Homestead strike; and federal troops broke the 1894 Pullman strike.[^s1] Indebted farmers organized the Farmers' Alliances and, in 1892, the People's Party, whose Omaha Platform demanded nationalized railroads, a graduated income tax and the direct election of senators.[^s1]

**Immigrants and cities.** More than 25 million immigrants arrived between 1870 and 1920, and by 1890 most new arrivals came from southern and eastern Europe; Chicago grew from about 300,000 people around 1870 to about 1.7 million by 1900.[^s2] Chinese immigrants were singled out. The Chinese Exclusion Act, signed by President Chester A. Arthur on May 6, 1882, banned Chinese laborers for ten years and barred Chinese residents from naturalization; it was extended in 1892, made permanent in 1902 and not repealed until 1943.[^s6]

**Native nations.** The Dawes Act of February 8, 1887, authorized the president to break up reservation land that tribes held in common into individual allotments and to sell "surplus" land to settlers; tribes lost vast acreage, and later acts tied allotment of the Five Tribes' lands to abolishing their governments.[^s7]

**Black Americans and the South.** From about 1890 to 1908, Southern states used literacy tests and poll taxes to strip Black men of the vote despite the Fifteenth Amendment, while Jim Crow laws segregated schools, transport and public facilities.[^s2] Lynching reached two to three killings a week in the late 1890s; Ida B. Wells documented the terror in *Southern Horrors* (1892).[^s2] Southern Democrats met the biracial Populist challenge with fraud and racial appeals, and the Colored Farmers' Alliance, which had as many as 250,000 members, declined in 1891 after violent white repression of cotton pickers' strikes.[^s1]

**Politics.** Patronage and graft were widespread, and city machines such as New York's Tammany Hall were corrupt but effective.[^s3] Elections were extremely close: two presidents won the Electoral College without a popular majority, and in 1876 Rutherford B. Hayes won the electoral vote 185–184.[^s3] After President James Garfield's assassination in 1881, Congress passed the Pendleton Civil Service Act in 1883 to begin replacing the spoils system with merit hiring.[^s3] The money question peaked in 1896, when William McKinley defeated William Jennings Bryan, and the Gold Standard Act of 1900 effectively settled it.[^s1]

## Legacy

The era's conflicts shaped the century that followed. Populism lost at the polls, but historians count it as the most significant third-party movement in American history and credit it with laying groundwork for Progressive reform.[^s1] Women's clubs, temperance workers and settlement-house reformers such as Jane Addams organized for suffrage, municipal reform and labor rights.[^s2] In 2020 the *Journal of the Gilded Age and Progressive Era* asked whether the United States has entered a "Second Gilded Age"; historian Richard White argued that the two eras, taken as wholes, share structural similarities stronger than their particular differences.[^s11]

## Debates and interpretations

**Robber barons or builders?** Early twentieth-century historians such as Charles Beard and Matthew Josephson, author of *The Robber Barons* (1934), portrayed an age ruled by corporations and corrupt politicians.[^s5] Later scholars, including Allan Nevins in his 1940 Rockefeller biography and Edward C. Kirkland, argued that businessmen faced ruthless competition and boom-and-bust cycles, and Alfred D. Chandler focused on the rise of corporate management; revisionists also argued that 1930s writers had read their own admiration for strong, interventionist government back into the period.[^s5]

**Issueless politics?** Revisionist political historians found that tariffs and currency divided Americans along regional and economic lines, and quantitative studies of Midwestern states found that ethnic and religious tensions, over issues such as Prohibition and English-language schooling, shaped voting at least as much as economics; critics replied that such studies explained who voted, not why.[^s5]

**Should the label survive?** Rebecca Edwards argues that "Gilded Age" is "not a very useful or accurate term," because a name meant to indict the elite misses the grassroots ferment of the period, and she treats periodization schemes as in flux.[^s4] Textbooks such as *The American Yawp* and OpenStax keep the term while stressing labor, farm and civil rights struggles alongside elite wealth.[^s1][^s3]
