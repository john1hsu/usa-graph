# September 11 attacks

On the morning of September 11, 2001, 19 hijackers trained by the Islamist terrorist network al Qaeda took control of four American airliners and turned them into weapons against targets in New York City and the Washington, D.C., area.[^s1][^s2] The FBI counts 2,976 people killed and thousands injured; the National September 11 Memorial lists 2,977 victims of that day.[^s2][^s5] It was the deadliest attack on U.S. soil in the nation's history, with a toll greater than Pearl Harbor, and it reshaped American security, foreign policy and public life for the following decades.[^s1][^s7]

## Background

Al Qaeda grew out of the war against the Soviet occupation of Afghanistan. Usama Bin Ladin, a wealthy Saudi, helped found it and built it into an organization able to plan large attacks, protected by an alliance with Afghanistan's Taliban rulers.[^s1] It had already struck U.S. targets, bombing the American embassies in Kenya and Tanzania in 1998 and attacking the destroyer USS *Cole* in October 2000.[^s1] The "planes operation" was conceived by Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and approved by Bin Ladin in late 1998 or early 1999.[^s1] The hijackers began entering the United States in January 2000; 15 were Saudi citizens, two were from the United Arab Emirates and one each from Egypt and Lebanon, and the pilots had taken flying lessons in the United States.[^s2]

## What happened

American Airlines Flight 11 struck the North Tower of the World Trade Center at 8:46 a.m., and a second hijacked jet hit the South Tower at 9:03 a.m.; at 9:37 a.m. a third crashed into the western face of the Pentagon.[^s1] Both towers later collapsed. More than 2,600 people died at the World Trade Center, 125 at the Pentagon and 256 aboard the four planes, according to the 9/11 Commission.[^s1] Among the dead were 441 first responders, including 343 New York City firefighters, 37 Port Authority police officers and 23 NYPD officers.[^s5]

United Airlines Flight 93, a delayed nonstop from Newark to San Francisco with 33 passengers and 7 crew members, was seized at about 9:28 a.m. and turned toward Washington.[^s4] Learning by phone what had happened in New York and Virginia, the passengers and crew voted to fight back; at 10:03 a.m. the plane hit an empty field in Somerset County, Pennsylvania, about 20 minutes' flying time from the capital and its likely target, the U.S. Capitol.[^s4][^s1]

## Aftermath and legacy

President George W. Bush, who learned of the attacks while visiting a Florida elementary school, signed the Authorization for Use of Military Force on September 18, 2001.[^s7] U.S. air strikes in Afghanistan began on October 7, and with Afghan Northern Alliance forces the campaign drove the Taliban from power by December, although Bin Ladin escaped into Pakistan.[^s7][^s8] At home, Bush signed the USA PATRIOT Act in October 2001 and that November signed legislation creating the Transportation Security Administration, federalizing airport screening.[^s7] A White House Office of Homeland Security, created eleven days after the attacks under Governor Tom Ridge, led to the Department of Homeland Security, which opened on March 1, 2003, by combining all or part of 22 federal agencies.[^s6] The FBI's investigation, code-named PENTTBOM, became the largest case in its history, involving more than 4,000 special agents.[^s2]

The bipartisan 9/11 Commission concluded in 2004 that the government had failed in imagination, policy, capabilities and management, writing that "The most important failure was one of imagination."[^s1] It recommended a National Counterterrorism Center and a single national intelligence director.[^s1] After Afghanistan the administration shifted its focus to Iraq, invading in March 2003 on the argument that Saddam Hussein's government held weapons of mass destruction, a case that rested on intelligence later shown to be flawed. That war, and policies such as indefinite detention at Guantánamo Bay, harsh interrogation and warrantless surveillance, provoked long-running legal and political debate, including the Supreme Court's 2006 ruling in *Hamdan v. Rumsfeld* against the administration's military tribunals.[^s7][^s8]

The attacks also brought a wave of bias-motivated assaults, threats, vandalism and arson against Arab, Muslim, Sikh and South Asian Americans and people perceived to belong to those groups, which the Justice Department made an enforcement priority.[^s9][^s8] They are remembered at the National September 11 Memorial, whose two reflecting pools sit in the footprints of the Twin Towers, at the Pentagon and at the Flight 93 National Memorial.[^s5][^s4] Engineers at NIST studied the towers' collapse and in 2005 traced it to impact damage and fire, saying they "found no corroborating evidence for alternative hypotheses" involving explosives.[^s3]

## Connections

The attacks took place at the World Trade Center, the Pentagon and a field in Somerset County, Pennsylvania, all within the United States.[^s1][^s4][^s2] They led to the war in Afghanistan and the USA PATRIOT Act,[^s7] to the creation of the Department of Homeland Security,[^s6] and to the National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States, whose report shaped later intelligence reforms.[^s1] They were planned and carried out by al Qaeda under Usama Bin Ladin and framed the presidency of George W. Bush, his "war on terror" and the Iraq War.[^s1][^s7][^s8]
