# Great Migration

The Great Migration was the movement of Black Americans out of the South between roughly 1910 and 1970. The National Archives and the Census Bureau estimate that about six million people took part, settling in cities of the North, Midwest and West.[^s1][^s3] In 1900 about 90 percent of African Americans lived in the South; by 1970 only about half did, and most lived in cities.[^s4][^s5] Historians usually divide it into a First Great Migration (1910–1940) and a Second Great Migration (1940–1970).[^s3]

## Background

At the start of the twentieth century most Black southerners worked the land as sharecroppers and farm laborers, and schooling for their children rarely went past the sixth grade.[^s4] Jim Crow segregation laws grew harsher after 1900, and voting was nearly impossible for Black citizens in states such as Georgia, where lynch mobs killed more than 450 people between 1880 and 1930.[^s5] The National Archives describes the migrants as escaping racial violence and Jim Crow oppression as much as seeking economic and educational opportunity.[^s1]

Leaving was not easy before 1914. Chicago, for example, offered freedom from legally sanctioned discrimination, but its industrial employers mostly turned Black workers away or hired them only as temporary strikebreakers, as in the 1904 meatpacking strike.[^s6]

## What happened

World War I opened the door. The war stopped the flow of European immigrants and drew white men into the military, and northern and midwestern factories began recruiting Black southerners.[^s1][^s2] In 1915–1916 the boll weevil and floods ruined cotton crops, and sharecroppers were often the first to lose their places.[^s5] Labor agents from coal, steel and railroad companies offered train tickets and higher wages. The *Chicago Defender*, edited by Georgia-born Robert S. Abbott, urged readers to come north to "the Promised Land," and letters from relatives spread the word.[^s5] Almost half a million Black southerners left in 1917–1918, and about 810,000 more left in the 1920s.[^s5] Many followed the rail lines to Philadelphia, New York, Detroit, Cincinnati, Chicago and Milwaukee.[^s2] The Depression slowed the outflow to nearly 400,000 in the 1930s.[^s5]

The second wave was larger. Defense industries created new jobs during World War II, while mechanization and falling cotton prices pushed people off southern farms. About 1.45 million Black southerners left in the 1940s and roughly two million more in the next two decades.[^s5][^s7] This wave also went west, to Oakland, Los Angeles, San Francisco, Portland and Seattle.[^s1] Jackie Robinson's family, for instance, moved from Cairo, Georgia, to Pasadena, California.[^s5] The Census Bureau's city data show the scale of change: from 1940 to 1970 the Black share of the population rose by 34.5 percentage points in Detroit and 24.6 points in Chicago.[^s3]

## Aftermath and legacy

Migrants generally found better pay, schools, the ballot and more personal safety, but they also met segregated neighborhoods, overcrowding, underemployment and harassment by white residents and police.[^s2][^s4] White resistance turned violent in the Red Summer of 1919, and after World War II restrictive covenants and redlining confined Black families to segregated neighborhoods, a pattern the National Archives links to today's racial gaps in wealth.[^s1][^s5] The Chicago Freedom Movement, one of Martin Luther King Jr.'s last campaigns, targeted that housing discrimination, and the Fair Housing Act followed in 1968.[^s5]

The migration made African Americans an urban, national people and moved them into the core of the national economy and politics.[^s7] In Chicago it built a Black industrial working class, political power, businesses and union activism, and it carried southern blues, food, churches and community ties north.[^s6] It also fed the New Negro movement and the Harlem Renaissance of the 1920s and later inspired Jacob Lawrence's *Migration Series*.[^s2][^s5] Since the late 1980s a reverse migration has drawn many Black Americans, especially retirees, back to the South, mostly to its cities.[^s5][^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

The facts of the movement are well documented, but historians frame it differently.

- **How big, and when did it end?** Federal agencies use about six million for 1910–1970.[^s1][^s3] The Smithsonian's museum gives both 1910 and 1916 as starting dates, and it puts the Black population still in the South in 1970 at 53 percent, where the New Georgia Encyclopedia gives about 47 percent.[^s4][^s5] Historian James Gregory argues that the second wave has been underestimated: he counts about five million departures from 1941 into the late 1970s and about eight million over the century, while warning that census birthplace data for those decades are uncertain.[^s7] The New Georgia Encyclopedia calls the migration's end "elusive."[^s5]
- **Push or pull?** Some accounts stress the pull of wartime jobs once immigration stopped.[^s2][^s6] Others put more weight on the push of boll weevils, floods, lynching, disfranchisement and segregation, and on the migrants' own networks and newspapers.[^s5]
- **Whose migration?** Many white southerners also left the South, but scholars use "Great Migration" for the Black movement, reflecting the history of African Americans as a racialized minority.[^s5]

## Connections

The Great Migration belongs to the wider history of African Americans and transformed that community's place in the nation (`affected`).[^s4] It was driven by Jim Crow and by the labor demands of World War I and World War II (`influencedBy`).[^s1][^s2] Chicago, Detroit and Los Angeles were among its main destinations (`occurredIn`).[^s1][^s3][^s6] It contributed to the Red Summer of 1919 and to the Harlem Renaissance (`ledTo`).[^s5] Related nodes include the *Chicago Defender*, sharecropping, redlining, the Fair Housing Act, the blues, the 1943 Detroit race riot and the Depression-era Okie migration.[^s1][^s5][^s6]
