# Women's suffrage movement

The women's suffrage movement was the long campaign to win American women the vote. Generations of activists lectured, petitioned, marched, lobbied, went to court and practiced civil disobedience, while opponents heckled, jailed and sometimes abused them.[^s1] It ended in its federal form with the Nineteenth Amendment, certified on August 26, 1920, which forbids the United States or any state to deny the vote on account of sex; even so, the National Archives notes that ratification did not fully enfranchise women.[^s1]

## Origins

The demand for the vote was first raised at the women's rights convention in Seneca Falls, New York, on July 19–20, 1848, whose Declaration of Sentiments called for it.[^s2][^s11] Early women's rights advocates such as Sarah Grimké also lectured against slavery, and in 1866 Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony helped form the American Equal Rights Association, whose leaders included Lucy Stone, Lucretia Mott, Sojourner Truth and Frederick Douglass, to seek the ballot for Black Americans and women together.[^s6][^s4]

The Reconstruction amendments broke that coalition. The Fourteenth Amendment of 1868 wrote the word *male* into the Constitution, and the proposed Fifteenth Amendment barred racial but not sex discrimination in voting.[^s4] Stone and Douglass backed it; Stanton and Anthony refused and made racist arguments against Black men voting, which Douglass condemned in May 1869.[^s4] Frances Ellen Watkins Harper had already warned that both sides ignored Black women: "You white women speak here of rights. I speak of wrongs."[^s4] In 1869 Stanton and Anthony founded the National Woman Suffrage Association to seek a federal amendment, while Stone and Julia Ward Howe founded the American Woman Suffrage Association to work state by state.[^s4][^s6]

## Goals and methods

Suffragists first tried the courts. In 1872 Anthony was arrested for voting in Rochester, New York, on the argument that the Fourteenth Amendment already protected her, and was convicted without being allowed to testify; that year Sojourner Truth was turned away from the polls in Michigan.[^s6][^s11] Black women also organized on their own terms, founding the National Association of Colored Women in 1896 with Mary Church Terrell, Ida B. Wells-Barnett, Harper and Harriet Tubman among its founders.[^s7][^s6]

Senator Aaron Sargent of California introduced the suffrage amendment on January 10, 1878; the Senate first voted on it, and defeated it 16 to 34, in 1887.[^s2] The rival associations merged in 1890 as the National American Woman Suffrage Association (NAWSA).[^s6] The West moved first: Wyoming Territory enfranchised women in 1869 and kept suffrage at statehood in 1890, followed by Colorado in 1893, Idaho in 1896 and California in 1911.[^s5] NAWSA courted white Southern support, asking Douglass in 1895 to stay away from its Atlanta convention, and Black suffragists protested such exclusions.[^s7]

A new generation brought new tactics. Alice Paul's procession of March 3, 1913, the day before Woodrow Wilson's inauguration, was attacked by crowds while police did little, and more than 100 women were hospitalized; Black marchers were told to walk at the rear, and Wells-Barnett joined the Illinois delegation instead.[^s8][^s11] NAWSA cut ties with Paul's Congressional Union in 1913, and it became the National Woman's Party in 1916.[^s11][^s6] That September NAWSA president Carrie Chapman Catt unveiled her Winning Plan, concentrating resources on winnable state campaigns, lobbying for a federal amendment and preparing for ratification.[^s10] From January 1917 the party's Silent Sentinels picketed the White House; many were jailed, staged hunger strikes and were force-fed.[^s8][^s11] Opponents, organized from 1911 in the National Association Opposed to Woman Suffrage, argued that politics was too dirty for women or that women had influence enough through clubs and churches.[^s6][^s11]

## Outcomes

New York adopted suffrage in November 1917, and in 1918 Wilson came out for the amendment, addressing the Senate on September 30; it still failed there twice more.[^s5][^s2] The House passed it on May 21, 1919, and the Senate on June 4 by 56 to 25, with 37 Republicans and 19 Democrats in favor.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Wisconsin ratified first, on June 10, 1919, and Tennessee became the thirty-sixth state on August 18, 1920; most Southern states rejected it, and Mississippi did not ratify until 1984.[^s5] NAWSA became the nucleus of the League of Women Voters.[^s6]

The victory was incomplete. Native American women could not vote until they were recognized as citizens in 1924, and Black women in the South faced the same disfranchisement and violence that kept Black men from the polls.[^s9][^s7] Mabel Ping-Hua Lee, who rode in New York's 1912 suffrage parade, was among the Chinese immigrant women whom exclusion laws kept from citizenship.[^s12] The Senate Historical Office ends its suffrage timeline with the Voting Rights Act of 1965.[^s2]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians disagree about who deserves credit and where the story begins. OpenStax treats Catt's and Paul's strategies as complementary, their combined pressure moving Congress, and notes that Wilson shifted after the jailed pickets' treatment became public.[^s11] The National Park Service records that Wilson denied Paul's tactics changed his mind and that the protests alienated some supporters.[^s8] Kimberly Hamlin argues that the two organizations' rival histories narrowed public memory to Anthony, Stanton and Paul, and calls suffrage "a shared victory brought about by the labors of generations of women."[^s13] Lisa Tetrault argues that Stanton and Anthony built up Seneca Falls as the origin story in their *History of Woman Suffrage*, strengthening their leadership but obscuring earlier and rival activism.[^s14] Sharon Harley holds that Black women had at least as much at stake as white women and that 1920 was a starting point for their political power, not an end.[^s7]

## Connections

The movement grew alongside abolitionism, and the Fifteenth Amendment both split it and redirected its strategy; Douglass, Truth and Tubman took part in it.[^s4][^s6] Its final campaign, during the Progressive Era, was led on parallel tracks by Catt of NAWSA and Paul of the National Woman's Party, against opposition organized by the National Association Opposed to Woman Suffrage.[^s10][^s8][^s11][^s6] It led to the Nineteenth Amendment and, through NAWSA, to the League of Women Voters.[^s1][^s6]
