# Dust Bowl

The Dust Bowl was the combination of drought and severe wind erosion that struck the Southern Great Plains during the 1930s. Its core lay in southwestern Kansas, southeastern Colorado, northeastern New Mexico and the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles, an area roughly 400 miles from north to south and 300 miles across whose boundaries shifted with the rain.[^s2] It arrived in the middle of the Great Depression, and drought, economic collapse and farming practices worked together to turn a dry spell into one of the worst environmental disasters in American history.[^s1]

## Background

Drought and blowing dust are natural on the semi-arid Plains, but from 1909 to 1929 farmers broke up about 32 million acres of Great Plains sod, many of them recent settlers with little experience of the regional climate.[^s1] Wheat farming spread especially fast after World War I, destroying the native grasses whose roots had held the soil.[^s2] By 1930 farmland covered nearly three-quarters of the Great Plains, and cropland reached its peak share of the land in the mid-to-late 1930s.[^s8]

## What happened

Drought ruined the wheat crop in the autumn of 1931, and by late January 1932 the wind was lifting soil from bare fields.[^s2] The worst drought years were 1934 and 1936. Dust storms numbered about forty in 1935 and sixty-one in 1938, and by 1935 wind erosion had damaged about 162 million acres, more than 80 percent of the High Plains.[^s1]

The most famous storm came on April 14, 1935, "Black Sunday." A dust front formed in central Nebraska in the morning and swept south to the Mexican border by dawn the next day, covering an area about 800 miles long. At Dodge City, Kansas, a cloud estimated at 500 to 600 feet tall turned midday to total darkness. A Red Cross report logged by the Weather Bureau counted 17 deaths in Kansas from "dust pneumonia" and three from suffocation.[^s4] After that storm, the *Washington Evening Star* reporter Robert E. Geiger coined the term "Dust Bowl," which the Soil Conservation Service and the public soon adopted.[^s2]

The crisis drove people off the land, though not always as it is remembered. Populations in some Texas and Oklahoma panhandle counties fell by as much as a quarter.[^s5] Yet the migrants who reached California, roughly half a million of them, came from across the Southern Plains, and many were tenant farmers from Arkansas, Missouri, eastern Oklahoma and East Texas. Falling cotton prices, foreclosures and the Agricultural Adjustment Act, which paid landowners to idle land and let many mechanize and evict sharecroppers, uprooted them as surely as the dust.[^s5] In California they were scorned as "Okies," crowded into squatter camps, and competed for farm work in ways that pushed down wages and displaced Mexican workers.[^s5]

## Federal response

The New Deal treated the Dust Bowl as both a relief emergency and a land problem. The Agricultural Adjustment Administration paid farmers to limit wheat and cotton production, the Resettlement Administration and Farm Security Administration lent to destitute farmers, and the government bought badly eroded land and returned it to grass, land that later became national grasslands.[^s2] Soil scientist Hugh Hammond Bennett, chief of the new Soil Erosion Service from 1933, timed his testimony to dust clouds darkening Washington in March 1935; on April 27, 1935, Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Soil Conservation Act, making the Soil Conservation Service a permanent agency of the Department of Agriculture.[^s3] It promoted terracing, strip cropping and grass seeding,[^s2] and from 1937 states began forming locally governed soil conservation districts under a model law the department drafted.[^s3]

After touring nine drought states, Roosevelt said in a September 1936 fireside chat that work relief, water projects and better land use would keep families on the land; Plains farmers, he said, "stand ready to fit, and not to fight, the ways of Nature."[^s6]

## Aftermath and legacy

Wind erosion eased as near-normal rain returned from 1938, and the drought ended by 1940.[^s2] By 1938 about 10 million acres had lost five inches of topsoil.[^s1] Cropland recovered to its pre-drought extent by 1945, but erosion-control and moisture-saving methods adopted from the 1930s onward made the shorter drought of the 1950s less severe.[^s8] The Dust Bowl lives on in conservation law, in the Resettlement Administration's 1935 film *The Plow That Broke the Plains*,[^s2] and in John Steinbeck's 1939 novel *The Grapes of Wrath*, whose image of displaced "Okies" shaped how the nation remembers the decade.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

**A human-made ecological disaster.** Donald Worster's *Dust Bowl* (1979) linked the Depression and the Dust Bowl as one crisis of American capitalism and culture, in which national expansion pushed commercial farming onto a volatile, marginal land and upset its ecological balance.[^s7] Paul Bonnifield's *The Dust Bowl*, reviewed alongside it, instead stressed the resilience of Plains people and saw their endurance as a victory; a 1981 review in *Great Plains Quarterly* judged Worster's case better supported.[^s7]

**Drought and nature in the lead.** Later researchers using county-level data, including historian Geoff Cunfer and colleagues, accept that plowed land blows far more readily than grassland but argue that plowing and drought were not the only causes, noting that the heaviest wheat areas did not necessarily suffer the worst drought or dust storms.[^s8] NASA climate scientists traced the drought itself to unusual tropical Pacific and Atlantic sea-surface temperatures, while finding that feedbacks between dry land and the atmosphere roughly doubled its severity in their models.[^s9]

## Connections

- The Dust Bowl occurred during the **Great Depression**, and the two crises compounded each other on Plains farms.[^s1]
- It took place on the **Great Plains**, above all in the panhandles of **Oklahoma** and Texas and in western **Kansas**.[^s1][^s2]
- It led to the **Soil Conservation Act of 1935** and the permanent **Soil Conservation Service**, built by **Hugh Hammond Bennett** and backed by **Franklin D. Roosevelt** as part of the **New Deal**.[^s3][^s6]
- Federal purchases of wind-eroded land became the **national grasslands**.[^s2]
- **Black Sunday** named the event, while the **Okies** migration and **The Grapes of Wrath** shaped its memory.[^s4][^s2][^s5]
