# Slavery in the United States

Slavery in the United States was a system of hereditary, race-based bondage in which people of African descent, and in the colonial era many Native Americans, were held as property for life and forced to work without pay. It took root in the English colonies in the 1600s, was protected by the Constitution, expanded with the cotton economy, and ended only with the Civil War and the Thirteenth Amendment, ratified on December 6, 1865.[^s4][^s5][^s8][^s12] The 1860 census, the last to count enslaved people, recorded 3,953,760 of them.[^s1]

## Origins

In late August 1619 the English privateer *White Lion* landed at Point Comfort, Virginia, and sold "20. and odd Negroes," in John Rolfe's words, for food. Seized from a Portuguese slave ship off Campeche, in present-day Mexico, they were the first Africans recorded in the Virginia colony.[^s2] The early Chesapeake workforce mixed enslaved Africans, European indentured servants who served fixed terms, and Native Americans. Lines of race and status were still loose, and some Africans, such as Anthony Johnson, later bought their freedom.[^s4]

Virginia's lawmakers then hardened those lines: a December 1662 act declared that a child would be bond or free "according to the condition of the mother," reversing the English rule that status followed the father and making slavery hereditary.[^s3] Later laws made lifelong slavery the rule for Africans, restricted Black people's travel and arms, and in 1705 classed enslaved people as real estate. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture explains the shift as enslavers' answer to alliances among poor workers and their search for cheaper labor, a process that sharpened the idea of whiteness.[^s4]

Native Americans were enslaved too. In Virginia, colonists and allied Native traders captured and sold Indigenous people, especially war captives, from soon after Jamestown's founding through the 1700s; scholars estimate that up to 50,000 Native people from the Southeast were sold into slavery in the late 1600s, many to the West Indies.[^s5]

Slavery was not only Southern. In Kings County (Brooklyn), New York, enslaved people made up 30 percent of residents in 1790. Pennsylvania passed a gradual emancipation law in 1780, and New York's final emancipation came on July 4, 1827.[^s10] New Jersey's 1804 law freed only children born afterward, and only after they served until age 21 or 25; about 16 people there were still legally enslaved in December 1865.[^s11]

## Growth and the cotton economy

The Constitution protected slavery without naming it. Its three-fifths clause counted enslaved people toward slaveholding states' seats in the House and electoral votes; Congress could not ban the African slave trade for twenty years; and the fugitive slave clause required people escaping bondage to be "delivered up" to their claimants.[^s8] The federal ban on importing enslaved people took effect on January 1, 1808. Of roughly 10.7 million Africans who survived the Middle Passage to the Americas, about 388,000, some 3.5 percent, landed in North America.[^s2]

Because children of enslaved mothers were born enslaved, the population kept growing: the census counted nearly 700,000 enslaved people in 1790 and 3,953,760 in 1860, nearly 500,000 of them in Virginia.[^s1][^s3] Cotton drove the expansion. Output rose from about 5 million pounds in 1793 to about 2 billion in 1860, when cotton made up more than 60 percent of U.S. exports, and banks in New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore and London extended the credit that bought cotton land.[^s6] About a million enslaved people were moved from the Upper South to the Deep South in the half century before the Civil War.[^s7] Sales and forced migration broke up an estimated one-fifth to one-third of enslaved marriages.[^s6]

## Resistance and abolition

Enslaved people resisted daily, by slowing work and breaking tools, and by flight and revolt. In August 1831 Nat Turner led a rebellion in Southampton County, Virginia, that killed fifty-seven white people; the white terror that followed brought harsher laws.[^s6] Freedom seekers fled to free states, Canada, Mexico, Spanish Florida and Indian territory, often unaided and sometimes helped by Black, Native and white allies, the network remembered as the Underground Railroad.[^s9] The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 denied accused people a jury trial or the right to testify, and about 1,000 Black Americans were sent South under it before 1861, though residents of upstate New York and northern Ohio made it largely unenforceable there.[^s8]

During the Civil War many enslaved people escaped to Union lines, and after the 1863 Emancipation Proclamation freedom spread as Union troops arrived to enforce it.[^s9][^s1] On June 19, 1865, General Order No. 3 announced freedom in Galveston, Texas, the origin of Juneteenth.[^s1] The Thirteenth Amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified on December 6, 1865, abolished slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for a crime.[^s12]

## Significance

Slavery shaped the nation's politics, economy and law. It gave slaveholding states extra power in Congress and the electoral college, underwrote the cotton trade that dominated American exports, and fuelled bitter conflict between free and slave states over freedom seekers.[^s8][^s6] Juneteenth, a federal holiday since June 17, 2021, now marks its end nationally.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians debate slavery's economic role. Edward Baptist, in *The Half Has Never Been Told* (2014), argues that wealth from enslaved labor was central to the rise of the United States as an economic power and that cotton productivity rose largely through quotas enforced by whipping.[^s13] Economic historians Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode respond that Baptist and other "New History of Capitalism" scholars misuse evidence and misstate the economic weight of cotton and slavery and their role in the Industrial Revolution.[^s14]

## Connections

Lifelong, inherited slavery **contrasts with** indentured servitude, which bound European workers for fixed terms.[^s4] Slavery **affected** the United States Constitution through its three-fifths, slave-trade and fugitive slave clauses.[^s8] Enslaved people's flight **led to** the Underground Railroad, and slaveholders' demands to recover freedom seekers **led to** the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850.[^s9][^s8] The Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves ended legal imports in 1808, while the domestic slave trade moved people south.[^s2][^s7] Slavery was ended by the Civil War, the Emancipation Proclamation and the Thirteenth Amendment, and its end is commemorated on Juneteenth.[^s1][^s12]
