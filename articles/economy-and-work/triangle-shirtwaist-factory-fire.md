# Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire

On Saturday, March 25, 1911, near closing time, fire swept through the Triangle Waist Company, a blouse ("shirtwaist") maker on the eighth, ninth and tenth floors of the Asch Building near Washington Square in New York City. In about eighteen minutes it killed 146 of the company's roughly 500 workers, 129 women and 17 men.[^s6] Most of the dead were young immigrant women and girls from southern and eastern Europe, many of them Jewish; it remains the deadliest industrial disaster in the city's history.[^s4] The fire became a turning point in American labor and safety law.

## Background

The ten-story Asch Building, completed in 1901 at 23–29 Washington Place, had shops on the ground floor and factory lofts above.[^s4] Garment work in New York was then dominated by sweatshop conditions: low wages, long hours and dangerous, unsanitary workrooms, much of it organized through subcontractors.[^s2] In 1909 Triangle workers joined the garment strike known as the "Uprising of the 20,000," which lasted about three months.[^s4] The building had no fire sprinklers, no fire drills and no alarm workers could trigger, and city law did not require them.[^s4]

## What happened

The fire probably began when a discarded match or cigarette ignited a bin of fabric scraps on the eighth floor, which was engulfed within about five minutes.[^s4] Workers on the eighth and tenth floors mostly escaped: two elevator operators made repeated trips and saved at least 150 people, and tenth-floor workers fled to the roof, where New York University law students next door helped them across with ladders.[^s4]

The ninth floor became a trap. Its door to the Washington Place stairwell was locked, a practice managers used to prevent unauthorized breaks and, by other accounts, theft; other exits were blocked by machinery, and the single elevator car could not carry everyone.[^s4] [^s6] [^s5] The fire escape collapsed under the weight of fleeing workers, and fire department ladders reached only to about the sixth or seventh floor.[^s6] [^s5] About forty people died jumping or falling to the sidewalk, and over a hundred died inside the building.[^s5] Frances Perkins was visiting a friend nearby and later recalled arriving as people had begun to jump.[^s3]

## Aftermath and legacy

On April 5, 1911, about 100,000 people followed the hearses up Fifth Avenue in a procession organized by the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union (ILGWU).[^s6] A week after the fire, union leader Rose Schneiderman gave a memorial speech blaming the public for ignoring dangerous conditions.[^s4] On April 11 a grand jury indicted the owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris, on manslaughter counts under a labor-law section requiring doors to be unlocked during working hours; on December 27, 1911, after a trial in which defense lawyer Max Steuer raised doubt over whether they knew the door was locked, both were acquitted.[^s1] Twenty-three civil suits were settled on March 11, 1914, for $75 per life lost, and in 1913 Blanck was fined $20 for locking a door at another factory.[^s1] Britannica adds that the owners profited from inflated insurance claims.[^s6]

The political response was larger. In October 1911 New York City created a Bureau of Fire Prevention, and the building code was amended to require fireproof materials, stairwells, alarms and extinguishers.[^s2] On June 30, 1911, the state legislature created the Factory Investigating Commission, chaired by state Senate majority leader Robert F. Wagner with Assemblyman Alfred E. Smith as vice-chair and labor leader Samuel Gompers among its members.[^s5] In 1911–1912 it held 59 public hearings, heard 472 witnesses and inspected 3,385 workplaces.[^s5] Sources count its legislative results differently, from 13 of 17 bills it submitted becoming law in 1912–1914 to 36 or 38 laws in all; together these laws rewrote the state's rules on fire safety, ventilation, sanitation, machine guarding and the hours of women and children, and served as models for other states.[^s5] [^s2] [^s4]

Perkins, who testified for and investigated with the commission, later became Secretary of Labor under Franklin D. Roosevelt and the first woman in a presidential cabinet, and she applied its lessons nationally.[^s5] [^s4] In a 1964 lecture she said the fire had "paid the debt society owed to those children," crediting the victims with New York's reform laws.[^s3] Kheel Center historians note that the New Deal passed similar federal legislation about two decades later.[^s2] The building, renamed for Frederick Brown and owned by New York University since 1929, became a National Historic Landmark on July 17, 1991; in 2011 researcher Michael Hirsch identified the last six unidentified victims, and a memorial gathering is held on each March 25.[^s4]
