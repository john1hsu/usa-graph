# Deepwater Horizon oil spill

The Deepwater Horizon oil spill began on April 20, 2010, when BP's Macondo well blew out beneath a drilling rig in the Gulf of Mexico, killing 11 workers and sending oil into the Gulf for 87 days.[^s1][^s5] It is the largest marine oil spill in U.S. history, and it reshaped how the federal government regulates offshore drilling and how the Gulf Coast pays for restoration.[^s5][^s2]

## Background

By 2010 the oil industry was drilling ever deeper in the Gulf. The Deepwater Horizon was a semi-submersible rig owned by Transocean and working for BP on the Macondo well, less than 50 miles off Louisiana in about 5,000 feet of water.[^s5][^s2][^s1] Offshore leasing, permitting, inspection and enforcement were all handled by one agency, the Minerals Management Service, whose goal of raising lease revenue often pulled against safety; the agency was also under-resourced and short of technical expertise.[^s3]

## What happened

On the night of April 20 the well blew out, an explosion and fire engulfed the rig, and its blowout preventer failed to activate; 11 men were killed.[^s1][^s2] The rig sank two days later.[^s1] Officials first said the well might be leaking up to 1,000 barrels a day, then 5,000; later government estimates put the flow at about 62,000 barrels a day at the start, falling to 53,000 by the time it stopped.[^s4] The presidential commission found that the understated early figures impeded planning to stop the oil at its source.[^s3]

Neither industry nor government had proven tools to stop a leak at that depth.[^s5] Containment domes clogged with hydrates in early May, and the "top kill" attempt of May 26–28 failed.[^s1] Responders sprayed about 1.84 million gallons of chemical dispersant, including at the wellhead, and carried out 411 controlled burns of floating oil, while tens of thousands of workers laid boom and ran skimmers.[^s1][^s5] BP's Vessels of Opportunity program hired out-of-work local fishers and their boats for cleanup; by June 21 fishery closures covered about 37 percent of the Gulf.[^s5][^s1] On July 15 BP closed the valves on a new cap and the flow stopped, and on September 19 the well was declared permanently sealed with cement after a relief well intercepted it.[^s1]

Estimates of the total differ. The government's 2010 oil budget put the discharge at about 4.9 million barrels, a figure not reduced by oil captured at the wellhead; a final count in January 2015 set the amount released into the Gulf at about 134 million gallons, roughly 3.2 million barrels.[^s4][^s1] Either way it dwarfed the 1989 *Exxon Valdez* spill of about 260,000 barrels.[^s5] Slicks covered more than 43,000 square miles, and oil reached more than 1,300 miles of shoreline from Texas to Florida.[^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

President Barack Obama announced a national commission, co-chaired by Bob Graham and William K. Reilly, on May 22, 2010, and the federal government imposed a six-month moratorium on deepwater drilling at the end of May.[^s3][^s1] The commission's January 2011 report concluded that the blowout "could have been prevented," tracing it to mistakes by BP, Halliburton and Transocean and to complacency in both industry and government.[^s3] It urged an independent offshore safety agency; the Minerals Management Service was renamed and split into separate bodies, and the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management replaced it.[^s3][^s5]

The legal reckoning ran for years. BP Exploration and Production pleaded guilty and was sentenced in January 2013 to $4 billion in criminal fines and penalties, and Transocean paid $400 million in criminal penalties and a $1 billion civil penalty.[^s2] After a trial in which the United States proved BP's gross negligence, the Justice Department announced a $20.8 billion settlement on October 5, 2015, the largest with a single entity in its history: a $5.5 billion Clean Water Act penalty, 80 percent of it directed to the Gulf under the RESTORE Act; $8.1 billion for natural resource damages; and payments to Alabama, Florida, Louisiana, Mississippi and Texas and to local governments.[^s2] Louisiana planned to put $7 billion toward its Coastal Master Plan.[^s5]

The human costs reached beyond the rig. Gulf seafood and tourism nearly halted in 2010, and the moratorium hit Louisiana's oil-dependent economy.[^s5] The National Institutes of Health's GuLF STUDY enrolled nearly 33,000 people and found that cleanup workers were 60 percent more likely than non-workers to report asthma or asthma symptoms one to three years later.[^s6]

## The "oil budget" controversy

In August 2010 officials released an oil budget estimating where the oil had gone, and it was widely reported as showing that three-quarters of the oil was gone.[^s4] Commission staff found the budget was a rough operational tool: only wellhead recovery was measured directly, biodegradation was left out, and oil counted as dispersed or dissolved was not necessarily gone; the "gone" framing and claims of peer review were inaccurate and produced misleading coverage.[^s4] Much of the oil in fact sank to the seafloor.[^s5]

## Connections

The spill occurred in the Gulf of Mexico, off Louisiana, whose coast, fisheries and oil economy it affected most heavily.[^s1][^s5] It led to the National Commission on the BP Deepwater Horizon Oil Spill and Offshore Drilling, to the breakup of the Minerals Management Service and the creation of the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, and to the RESTORE Act framework that channels Clean Water Act penalties to Gulf restoration.[^s1][^s3][^s5][^s2] It unfolded under President Barack Obama, invites comparison with the *Exxon Valdez* spill, and belongs to the environmental history of the United States and the history of its oil industry.[^s3][^s5]
