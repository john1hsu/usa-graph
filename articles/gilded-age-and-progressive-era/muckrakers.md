# Muckrakers

The muckrakers were American investigative journalists of the early twentieth century who exposed monopoly power, political corruption, dangerous workplaces, child labor and unsafe food, mostly in popular magazines and books. Their reporting provoked public outrage, and Progressive activists turned that outrage into campaigns for legislation; OpenStax calls them an important spark of the Progressive movement.[^s1] The name came from President Theodore Roosevelt, who meant it as a rebuke.[^s2]

## Origins

Exposure journalism had forerunners in the Gilded Age. Jacob Riis's *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) used photographs to show New York City's tenements, and Henry Demarest Lloyd's *Wealth Against Commonwealth* (1894) attacked the excesses of Standard Oil.[^s1] In 1892, after her friend Thomas Moss was lynched in Memphis, Ida B. Wells traveled the South interviewing eyewitnesses and families and collecting records; her pamphlets *Southern Horrors* (1892) and *The Red Record* (1895) showed that the rape accusations used to justify lynching were almost always false. A white mob destroyed her newspaper office and drove her from Memphis.[^s8] Some reference works count her among the muckrakers.[^s2]

The movement's main platform was the cheap magazine. S. S. McClure and John S. Phillips founded *McClure's Magazine* in 1893, selling it for fifteen cents against rivals' thirty-five; by June 1898 its circulation had passed 400,000.[^s4] Ida Tarbell's *History of the Standard Oil Company* ran in *McClure's* from November 1902 to October 1904 before appearing as a book, and the January 1903 issue paired her installment with Lincoln Steffens's "The Shame of Minneapolis" and Ray Stannard Baker's "The Right to Work," under an editorial drawing the three together.[^s4]

## Goals and methods

Muckrakers aimed to inform a mass readership and push it to demand change. Steffens showed city officials working with business interests, and his collected articles, *The Shame of the Cities*, helped spur municipal reform.[^s9] Baker reported on unsafe conditions and low pay in coal mines, and later on the oppression of Black Southerners in *Following the Color Line* (1908); John Spargo's *The Bitter Cry of the Children* (1906) took on child labor.[^s1][^s9] Samuel Hopkins Adams exposed fraudulent and dangerous products on the market.[^s7]

Their method was long, documented research published in installments. Upton Sinclair spent almost two months working beside Chicago packinghouse workers before writing *The Jungle* (1906).[^s3] In February 1906 *Cosmopolitan*, owned by William Randolph Hearst, began David Graham Phillips's nine-part *Treason of the Senate*, linking senators to big business; the series doubled the magazine's circulation within two months, though other reformers later faulted its reliance on innuendo and exaggeration.[^s6][^s9]

Roosevelt, who saw the Phillips series as a politically motivated attack, answered in an April 14, 1906, address at the cornerstone laying of the House office building in Washington.[^s6][^s2] He borrowed from John Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress* the figure of a man raking muck who never looks up at the crown offered him, warning against writers who saw only filth, while granting that such men were "indispensable to the well-being of society."[^s2] Tarbell later wrote that Roosevelt had "misread his Bunyan."[^s2]

## Outcomes

The clearest legislative result came in food and drug law. Sinclair sent Roosevelt *The Jungle*; Roosevelt ordered the packinghouses investigated, and federal inspectors found conditions like those in the novel.[^s3] The FDA describes the book as the final precipitating force behind both a meat inspection law and the Food and Drugs Act, which Roosevelt signed on June 30, 1906.[^s7] Sinclair, a socialist, had meant to indict capitalism through the suffering of an immigrant family, and he later wrote that he "aimed at the public's heart, and by accident hit its stomach."[^s3] Congress responded with consumer laws rather than labor laws.[^s9]

The Senate's own historians credit *The Treason of the Senate* with breaking Senate resistance to direct election of senators, an amendment long blocked by Southern senators who feared it would strengthen Black voters; the Seventeenth Amendment was ratified in 1913.[^s6] More broadly, exposés of child labor, monopolies and unsafe workplaces gave Progressive activists causes to carry into lobbying for legislation.[^s1]

The movement itself faded within a few years. *McClure's* star writers left in 1906 to run *The American Magazine*; *McClure's* never again paid dividends, lost advertising in the 1907 depression, and was surrendered to creditors in 1911.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians weigh the muckrakers' influence differently. OpenStax treats them as a spark that set the Progressive movement in motion, with activists then carrying their causes into legislation.[^s1] The FDA's history calls *The Jungle* the final precipitating force behind the 1906 laws, placing it at the end of a longer push for regulation rather than at its start.[^s7] On the movement's decline, Steffens told Roosevelt his speech had "put an end" to the investigations, and historian Cornelius Regier judged it vague enough to indict all reform writers; a study of the *McClure's* split hosted by Allegheny College argues instead that the magazine broke apart over S. S. McClure's personal conduct and risky expansion plans, not the speech.[^s5] Accounts also differ over whom to include: the Roosevelt Center's list names Wells and Frances Kellor alongside Tarbell and Steffens, while the OpenStax account names magazine and book writers such as Riis, Tarbell, Steffens and Baker.[^s2][^s1]

## Connections

Muckraking took place during the Progressive Era and drew on grievances from the Gilded Age.[^s1] *The Jungle* led to the Meat Inspection Act, and the wider pure-food exposés to the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906.[^s3][^s7] Phillips's series contributed to the Seventeenth Amendment.[^s6] Theodore Roosevelt both named and acted on the muckrakers' work.[^s2][^s3] Wells's investigations link the movement to the history of lynching and Jim Crow, and Sinclair's Chicago stockyards link it to immigrant workers' lives.[^s8][^s3]
