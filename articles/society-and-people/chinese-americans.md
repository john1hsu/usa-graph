# Chinese Americans

Chinese Americans are Americans of Chinese descent — one of the oldest Asian American communities and, in the 2020 census, the largest Asian group counted "alone or in any combination," at 5,205,461 people identifying as Chinese (except Taiwanese), up 37.2 percent from 2010.[^s7] Their history runs from the miners and railroad builders of the 1850s and 1860s, through six decades of exclusion laws, to a large and varied community whose roots lie in Mainland China, Hong Kong, Taiwan and the Chinese diaspora of Southeast Asia.[^s6]

## History

**Gold, railroads and work.** The California gold discoveries and political upheaval in China drew some of the earliest large-scale Asian immigration to the United States; Chinese immigrants worked in mining, on railroads and in agriculture.[^s2] More than 11,000 Chinese immigrants worked for the Central Pacific Railroad on the first transcontinental line, built between 1863 and 1869.[^s2] They were paid on average about 30 percent less than white workers, housed in segregated camps, charged for their own food and lodging, and often given the most dangerous tunnel work through solid granite; no accurate death count exists, but the National Park Service estimates that more than a thousand died.[^s1] On June 24, 1867, Chinese crews stopped work along a 30-mile stretch between Cisco and Truckee, California, demanding higher pay, a ten-hour day and shorter tunnel shifts. The company broke the week-long strike by cutting off food and supplies, but it was then the largest organized labor strike in the country.[^s1]

**Exclusion and violence.** Laws aimed at Chinese immigrants followed. The 1875 Page Law restricted the immigration of Chinese women; the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 suspended the entry of laborers, and was renewed in 1892, made permanent in 1902 and lifted only in 1943.[^s1] The 1882 act also made Chinese immigrants already in the country ineligible for citizenship.[^s2] Racial violence peaked between 1885 and 1887, and the Chinese population fell from about 133,000 in 1882 to about 90,000 by 1900.[^s1] The worst single attack came at Rock Springs, Wyoming Territory, on September 2, 1885, when white coal miners killed 28 Chinese miners, wounded 15 and looted and burned all 79 homes in the town's Chinatown; federal troops were sent in, but a grand jury brought no charges and no one was ever convicted.[^s3] Because the Page Law kept most women out, the community long remained overwhelmingly male: census data show 1,887 Chinese men for every 100 women in 1900, falling to 135 by 1950.[^s6]

**Citizenship and the gate.** Chinese Americans fought exclusion in the courts. Wong Kim Ark, born in San Francisco in 1873 to Chinese parents, was detained for about six months when he returned to San Francisco in August 1895. In 1898 the Supreme Court ruled 6–2, in an opinion by Justice Horace Gray, that he was a citizen by birth under the Fourteenth Amendment.[^s4] From 1910 to 1940 the Angel Island Immigration Station in San Francisco Bay processed arrivals from across the Pacific, holding detainees for days, months and sometimes years during medical examinations and interrogations; the poems they carved into the barrack walls survive.[^s2] Despite exclusion, Chinatowns endured, with the largest in Los Angeles, New York City and San Francisco.[^s6]

**War and repeal.** At the start of World War II there were only about 78,000 Chinese Americans on the mainland and 29,000 in Hawaii, yet as many as 20,000 served in the U.S. armed forces — about 40 percent of them not citizens, since the law had barred their naturalization.[^s5] With China a wartime ally, Congress repealed exclusion in 1943 with the Magnuson Act, which set an annual quota of 105 and finally allowed Chinese immigrants to naturalize; the 1946 Chinese War Brides Act then admitted servicemen's wives outside the quota.[^s6]

## Today

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 made family reunification the main basis for admission, and arrivals from Mainland China, Hong Kong and Taiwan transformed and diversified the community.[^s6] Chinese students and professionals granted permanent residence after the 1949 revolution, ethnic Chinese refugees from Vietnam, and an estimated 45,000 Chinese students protected by a 1992 law after the Tiananmen Square crackdown added further layers.[^s6] New suburban centers have grown in California's San Gabriel Valley and Silicon Valley, while new immigrants have revitalized older urban Chinatowns.[^s6] Among people reporting a single Asian group in 2020, Chinese (except Taiwanese), at 4,128,718, ranked second after Asian Indian.[^s7] Public memory has also shifted: in 2018 Congress awarded a collective Congressional Gold Medal to Chinese American veterans of World War II.[^s5]

The Golden Spike ceremony of May 10, 1869, shows how memory and record can diverge. A Chinese contingent from the Central Pacific attended, and eight Chinese men moved the final rail into position.[^s1][^s8] Yet the best-known photograph of the day, A. J. Russell's "East and West Shaking Hands," omits them — an omission a Utah State University Libraries exhibit calls glaring, while noting there is no evidence it was deliberate and that other photographs taken that day record Chinese workers.[^s8] Company records and newspapers seldom named individual workers, and no letters or diaries from them are known to survive.[^s1]

## Connections

- Chinese Americans originated in **China**, and since 1965 increasingly also in Hong Kong, Taiwan and the Chinese diaspora of Southeast Asia.[^s6]
- They organized the **Chinese railroad workers' strike of 1867**, part of their central role in building the **transcontinental railroad**.[^s1]
- They took part in the **California Gold Rush**, the first large wave of Chinese immigration.[^s2]
- Up to 20,000 served in **World War II**, which led to the repeal of exclusion in the **Magnuson Act**.[^s5][^s6]
- The **Chinese Exclusion Act**, the **Page Act of 1875**, the **Rock Springs massacre** and **United States v. Wong Kim Ark** shaped their legal and physical security; the **Angel Island Immigration Station** and its **detention-barracks poetry** record how exclusion was enforced.[^s1][^s3][^s4][^s2]
- The **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965** reshaped the community, and the **2020 census** measures it today.[^s6][^s7]
