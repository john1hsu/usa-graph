# American Civil War

The American Civil War (1861–1865) was fought between the United States and eleven Southern states that left the Union to form the Confederate States of America in order to protect slavery. The Union's victory kept the nation whole and, through the actions of enslaved people, Black soldiers, Congress and President Abraham Lincoln, ended slavery in the United States.[^s1] It was the deadliest war in American history.[^s6]

## Background

By 1850 the North and South had grown apart economically, socially and ideologically, chiefly because slavery had faded in the North after the Revolution but remained central to Southern life. In the 1850s the party system broke down: the Whigs collapsed, the Democrats split, and the new Republican Party formed to stop slavery from spreading into the western territories. Violence in Kansas, the caning of Senator Charles Sumner and the *Dred Scott* decision deepened the divide.[^s7]

Lincoln won the 1860 election with under 40 percent of the popular vote, carrying every free state except New Jersey. The Deep South saw his victory as a threat to slavery. South Carolina's convention voted 169 to 0 to secede on December 20, 1860, and Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana and Texas followed by February 1, 1861. Their delegates met in Montgomery, Alabama, on February 4 and chose Jefferson Davis as president.[^s1] The seceding states stated their reasons plainly: Mississippi declared that its position was "thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery."[^s2] South Carolina's declaration centered on Northern refusal to enforce the Fugitive Slave Act, and Confederate Vice President Alexander Stephens said in March 1861 that the new government rested on the idea that Black people were not equal to white people.[^s3]

## What happened

On April 12, 1861, Confederate forces fired on Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor; it surrendered the next day, and Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers. That call pushed four more states of the Upper South into the Confederacy, while the slaveholding border states of Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri stayed in the Union.[^s1]

Enslaved people made freedom a war aim from the start. Thousands fled to Union lines; in May 1861 General Benjamin Butler began receiving them at Fort Monroe as "contraband of war." Congress followed with confiscation acts, abolished slavery in the District of Columbia in April 1862, and passed a Second Confiscation Act that July. After the Battle of Antietam on September 17, 1862, the bloodiest single day in American history, Lincoln announced emancipation, and the Emancipation Proclamation took effect on January 1, 1863, in areas under Confederate control.[^s1] About 179,000 Black men, roughly a tenth of the Union Army, served as soldiers and about 19,000 in the Navy. They were at first paid less than white soldiers until Congress granted equal pay in June 1864, and nearly 40,000 died, most of them from disease.[^s5]

Women served as nurses, raised money through the U.S. Sanitary Commission and worked as spies for both sides, and hungry Confederate women led bread riots in 1863.[^s1] In July 1863 the Union won at Gettysburg and took Vicksburg, splitting the Confederacy. Sherman captured Atlanta on September 2, 1864, and marched to the sea, and on April 9, 1865, Robert E. Lee surrendered to Ulysses S. Grant at Appomattox.[^s1]

## Aftermath and legacy

Fighting did not stop at once. Joseph E. Johnston surrendered in North Carolina on April 26, 1865, and Edmund Kirby Smith surrendered the Trans-Mississippi Department on May 26. Stand Watie, a Cherokee leader and Confederate brigadier general, surrendered his command at Doaksville in Indian Territory on June 23, the last Confederate general to do so, and the raider CSS *Shenandoah* surrendered in Liverpool, England, on November 6. President Andrew Johnson formally proclaimed the insurrection over on August 20, 1866.[^s4]

The war's cost was immense. The long-cited total of about 620,000 dead came from incomplete records; in 2011 historian J. David Hacker used census data to estimate about 750,000 deaths, within a range of 650,000 to 850,000.[^s6] Republicans drafted the Thirteenth Amendment during the war to end slavery permanently, but Reconstruction's promise of Black rights soon gave way to white violence, segregation and voting discrimination.[^s1] In memory, many white Southerners embraced the "Lost Cause," a story that denied slavery's central role; the National Park Service now uses the documentary record to confront that story at its battlefields.[^s3]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree that slavery caused secession, but they still debate why the Union won. Some stress Northern superiority in industry and manpower; others emphasize Lincoln's leadership, the Union's turn under Grant, Sherman and Sheridan to a harder war, the South's failed diplomacy, internal Confederate conflicts over states' rights, or the collapse of slavery, which wrecked the Southern economy and supplied Black troops that let Lincoln refuse to compromise on emancipation.[^s7] A related debate concerns who freed the enslaved: textbooks such as *The American Yawp* stress that Black Americans pressed emancipation from the start, while Lincoln's own approach began cautiously and evolved over the war.[^s1]

## Connections

The war grew out of slavery in the United States, background it **requires**, and it **led to** the Emancipation Proclamation, the Thirteenth Amendment and the Reconstruction era, whose amendments include the Fourteenth.[^s1] It **took place in** the United States, where Abraham Lincoln led the Union as president and Jefferson Davis led the Confederacy from Montgomery and then Richmond.[^s1] It also reached Indian Territory, where the Cherokee leader Stand Watie made the last surrender by a Confederate general.[^s4] Its memory produced the Lost Cause narrative that historians and the National Park Service have challenged.[^s3]
