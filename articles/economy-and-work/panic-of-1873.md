# Panic of 1873

The Panic of 1873 was a financial crisis that broke out in September 1873, when Jay Cooke and Company, one of the country's best-known banking houses, collapsed under the weight of its railroad investments.[^s1] Bank runs spread from New York to the Midwest and the South, the New York Stock Exchange shut its doors for ten days, and the crisis was followed by a depression that lasted until about 1879.[^s1][^s3] Until the 1890s, and in some accounts until the 1930s, Americans called it the "Great Depression"; economists now often call it the Long Depression.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

## Background

Railroads drove much of American growth after the Civil War, but they needed huge sums of money up front for risky projects, and they raised it largely by selling bonds.[^s2] By the early 1870s, railroad building had outrun the traffic to pay for it, and returns on railroad investment were falling.[^s1] The national banking system created in 1863–64 made matters more fragile: banks around the country kept their required reserves in New York and other reserve cities, and the supply of currency could not expand quickly when demand for cash rose, so strains at the center spread outward.[^s1]

Jay Cooke and Company had become famous for selling Union war bonds during the Civil War, and by 1869 it was financing the Northern Pacific Railway, planned to run from Duluth, Minnesota, to the Pacific Northwest.[^s2] The project suffered from mismanagement, construction problems and a widening funding gap, and the Crédit Mobilier scandal of 1872, over inflated Union Pacific construction costs, made both investors and Congress warier of new railroad ventures.[^s2] The Encyclopedia of Arkansas adds that speculation, distrust of banks, an 1869 gold-market panic and great fires in Chicago and Boston had already strained the economy.[^s3]

## What happened

The immediate trigger came from Europe. Stock market crashes in Vienna, Austria, in May and September 1873 led European investors to sell American securities, especially railroad bonds; prices fell, railroads could no longer borrow, and many defaulted on their debts to banks.[^s1] On September 18, 1873, Jay Cooke and Company suspended payments and went bankrupt.[^s1][^s2] Its failure changed what depositors expected of every bank: two days later the New York Stock Exchange closed for the first time in its history, and on September 24 the New York Clearing House suspended cash payments in the city.[^s1] Problems reached banks in Washington, D.C., Pennsylvania, New York, Virginia and Georgia and in Indiana, Illinois and Ohio, and at least one hundred banks failed nationwide.[^s1]

The acute panic eased by mid-October, but the damage to industry and commerce lasted for years.[^s1] About 18,000 businesses failed over the next two years, railroad construction fell sharply, and markets for timber, cattle and wheat faltered.[^s3] Between 1873 and 1877, tens of thousands of workers, many of them Civil War veterans, took to the roads looking for work, and unemployment in New York City reached about 25 percent, roughly 100,000 people, according to New York Fed economists.[^s2] In the South, credit dried up, many landowners defaulted and wages fell.[^s4]

Washington debated how to respond. In 1874 Congress passed a bill to add greenback paper currency, which President Ulysses S. Grant vetoed as an inflationary threat to long-term credit; in January 1875 the Specie Resumption Act instead committed the Treasury to retire greenbacks and return to gold-backed money within four years.[^s2] Farmers' opposition to that hard-money course helped give rise to the Greenback Party.[^s3]

## Aftermath and legacy

The panic destroyed the Freedman's Savings and Trust Company, chartered by Congress in 1865 for formerly enslaved people and their descendants.[^s5] After an 1870 charter amendment, its trustees had invested depositors' money in real estate and railroads and made risky loans to friends, some without collateral; the panic made most of those investments worthless, runs drained the branches' cash, and several trustees resigned.[^s6] Frederick Douglass was elected president in March 1874 to restore confidence, soon felt "married to a corpse," and within about six weeks urged Congress to close the bank.[^s5][^s6] It closed on June 29, 1874.[^s5] Its deposits had no federal protection, and of 61,131 depositors eligible for payouts, only 29,996 claimed them; about half of the depositors eventually recovered some three-fifths of their balances.[^s5]

The depression also reshaped politics. *The American Yawp* argues that economic issues replaced Reconstruction as the leading national question, that the downturn ate away at Northern idealism about Reconstruction, and that it helped Democrats win control of the House of Representatives in the 1874 elections.[^s4] Across the South, white Democrats calling themselves Redeemers promised local rule by white Democrats, and Black voters were disenfranchised and Republicans sometimes physically attacked as Reconstruction governments fell.[^s4][^s3]

For workers, years of layoffs and wage cuts set the stage for the Great Railroad Strike of July 1877, which President Rutherford B. Hayes ended with federal troops.[^s3] The depression began to lift early in 1879.[^s3]

## Debates and interpretations

Scholars broadly agree on the sequence of events but weigh the causes differently. Federal Reserve historians emphasize overbuilt railroads, falling returns and the sudden withdrawal of European capital after the Vienna crashes, magnified by a rigid national banking system.[^s1] The Encyclopedia of Arkansas gives weight as well to monetary policy, especially the Coinage Act of 1873, which ended the coinage of silver; critics later called it the "Crime of '73" because, they argued, the move toward a gold standard hurt miners and indebted farmers.[^s3] The New York Fed economists leave the question open: was the long slump mainly the end of an investment boom that government could do little about, or was it prolonged by the hard-money policies that followed?[^s2]

Historians also differ on how much the depression explains the end of Reconstruction. *The American Yawp* treats the economic crisis as a turning point that led both parties to retreat from Reconstruction and gave Democrats the House.[^s4] The same textbook and the Encyclopedia of Arkansas also stress developments in the South itself, where Redeemer campaigns, disenfranchisement and attacks on Republicans restored white Democratic rule, so that economic distraction in the North and political violence in the South worked together.[^s4][^s3]

## Connections

The panic grew out of the boom in railroads in the United States and the financing of the Northern Pacific Railway by Jay Cooke and Company, and it centered on New York City, the hub of the national banking system during the financial Gilded Age.[^s1][^s2] It led to the Great Railroad Strike of 1877 and to the labor movement that strike strengthened.[^s3] It destroyed the Freedman's Savings and Trust Company, drawing in Frederick Douglass as its last president.[^s6][^s5] It affected the course of the Reconstruction era by shifting national attention and power away from it.[^s4] President Ulysses S. Grant's veto of greenback expansion and the Specie Resumption Act defined the federal response, and the Coinage Act of 1873 remains part of the debate over its causes.[^s2][^s3]
