# Underground Railroad

The Underground Railroad was neither underground nor a railroad. The National Park Service defines it as resistance to enslavement through escape and flight, carried on by enslaved African Americans who sought their own freedom and lasting until the end of the Civil War.[^s1] Freedom seekers, a term the Park Service prefers to "fugitives" or "runaways", often fled entirely on their own; others were helped by free and enslaved Black people, American Indians and people of many religious and ethnic backgrounds who broke the law to aid them.[^s1] Their destinations included the free states, Canada, Mexico, Spanish Florida, Indian Territory, the Caribbean and, during the war, Union army lines.[^s1]

## Origins

Enslaved people tried to escape from the earliest days of slavery in the colonies, long before anyone spoke of a railroad.[^s1][^s2] The name itself is uncertain. A popular story holds that when Tice Davids crossed the Ohio River from Kentucky in 1831, his enslaver said he must have vanished on an underground road, but that account is folklore; the phrase appears in print in the *New York Spectator* in 1842.[^s2] A Pennsylvania memoir of 1883 dated slaveholders' use of the term near Columbia, Pennsylvania, to the late 1780s, while other accounts place it about 1804.[^s4]

Federal law shaped the movement from the start. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1793 let enslavers seize freedom seekers even in free states, and in *Prigg v. Pennsylvania* (1842) the Supreme Court upheld it while ruling that state officials did not have to help enforce it.[^s2] In response, Black and white activists in Northern cities formed vigilance committees; Robert Purvis brought one to Philadelphia in 1837, and William Still, who joined the Pennsylvania Anti-Slavery Society in 1847, later chaired its committee.[^s2][^s4]

## Goals and methods

The goal was freedom, and the methods were as varied as the people who sought it. In Virginia, many freedom seekers left from ports such as Norfolk, Portsmouth and Richmond aboard steamships and schooners whose captains or crews were sympathetic, landing in Boston or New Bedford; others crossed the Appalachians toward the Ohio River or Pennsylvania, aided by free Black communities along the way.[^s2] Henry "Box" Brown had himself shipped in a three-by-two-foot box from Richmond to Philadelphia on March 23, 1849, a journey of 27 hours.[^s4] Harriet Tubman, Thomas Garrett, Levi and Vestal Coffin, David Ruggles and Isaac T. Hopper were among the best-known helpers.[^s2]

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, approved September 18, 1850, raised the stakes. It commanded "all good citizens" to aid in enforcement, barred alleged fugitives from testifying, and set a fine of up to $1,000 and up to six months in prison for anyone who harbored or rescued them.[^s5] Commissioners received $10 when they sent a person south but only $5 when they did not.[^s5] After the act, the Park Service notes, aid became more deliberate and organized in some places.[^s1] Some communities resisted with force: at Christiana, Pennsylvania, on September 11, 1851, a Maryland slaveholder was killed while trying to recapture freedom seekers.[^s4] Because the law made the free states unsafe, many people pushed on to Canada, where St. Catharines in Canada West became a favored destination.[^s2]

## Outcomes

No one knows exactly how many people escaped. One estimate cited by Encyclopedia Virginia puts the number who fled the South at about 100,000, roughly a third of whom had reached Canada by 1861.[^s2] In Philadelphia, William Still's journal records 485 freedom seekers aided between 1852 and 1857, and the historian Nilgun Okur estimated that nearly 9,000 reached the city by the start of the Civil War.[^s4] Enslavers complained loudly of their losses, which kept escape at the centre of the national quarrel over slavery.[^s2] Still's records became the basis of his 1872 book *The Underground Railroad*, still a key source for historians.[^s4] In 1998 Congress called the Underground Railroad "one of the most significant expressions of the American civil rights movement" and created the National Underground Railroad Network to Freedom within the National Park Service to commemorate it.[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

For decades the standard account was Wilbur H. Siebert's *The Underground Railroad from Slavery to Freedom* (1898), built on interviews with aging participants and focused on white conductors and stationmasters.[^s3] Larry Gara's *The Liberty Line* (1961) argued that much of this was legend: most freedom seekers crossed the South on their own, organized help mattered mainly once they reached the North, and the real heroes were the people who fled and the free Black people who aided them.[^s3][^s2] John Hope Franklin and Loren Schweninger's *Runaway Slaves* (1999), drawn from runaway advertisements, concluded that only a small portion of runaways used the Underground Railroad; most hid nearby and returned within days or weeks.[^s3] Other historians have partly restored the picture of organization. Fergus Bordewich called it an interracial civil rights movement, and Eric Foner's *Gateway to Freedom* (2015) showed New York's interracial vigilance committees helping thousands, while describing the whole as "an interlocking series of local networks" rather than one organization.[^s3][^s2]

## Connections

The Underground Railroad is a form of resistance to slavery, the National Park Service's definition, and it began among enslaved African Americans seeking their own freedom.[^s1] It was shaped by the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, after which aid became more organized.[^s1][^s5] It affected Canada, which received about a third of the estimated 100,000 freedom seekers by 1861.[^s2] It overlaps with abolitionism but is not the same thing: many freedom seekers never met an abolitionist, and Harriet Tubman and Frederick Douglass were themselves freedom seekers before they were helpers and speakers.[^s1][^s2] The popular claim that quilt patterns carried coded directions is treated separately as a myth.
