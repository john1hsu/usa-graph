# Pledge of Allegiance

The Pledge of Allegiance is a short oath of loyalty to the flag of the United States and to the republic it represents. Federal law sets its wording, which opens "I pledge allegiance to the Flag of the United States of America," and says it should be recited standing at attention, facing the flag, with the right hand over the heart.[^s1] It was written in 1892, entered federal law in 1942 and took its present wording in 1954.[^s3][^s8]

## Origins

The pledge began as part of a magazine's school campaign. *The Youth's Companion*, a Boston periodical for young readers with about half a million subscribers, planned a national school program for the 400th anniversary of Columbus's arrival, tied to the World's Columbian Exposition. Francis Bellamy, a former minister who had joined the magazine's promotions department in 1891, organized it: he lobbied Congress to endorse the school ceremony and helped persuade President Benjamin Harrison to proclaim a Columbus Day holiday.[^s4] The magazine printed the pledge on September 8, 1892, without a byline.[^s5][^s3] Schoolchildren recited it at Columbus Day ceremonies that October; Bellamy recalled first hearing it on October 21 from 4,000 Boston high school boys.[^s4][^s3]

The original wording was shorter and named no country: it began "I pledge allegiance to my Flag" and went on to promise loyalty to the republic as one indivisible nation with liberty and justice for all.[^s3] National Flag Conferences led by the American Legion and the Daughters of the American Revolution changed "my Flag" to "the flag of the United States" in 1923 and added "of America" in 1924.[^s4] Congress gave the pledge legal standing for the first time on June 22, 1942, in a joint resolution codifying flag customs, and adopted its official name in 1945.[^s3][^s1]

Children first recited it with the "Bellamy salute," the right arm extended toward the flag. Because the gesture resembled the Nazi salute, Congress replaced it in 1942 with the right hand over the heart.[^s3]

## Meaning and practice

**Compulsion and the flag salute cases.** Many states required pupils to salute the flag and recite the pledge. Jehovah's Witnesses refused, reading Exodus 20:4–5 as forbidding them to bow to a graven image, which they considered the flag to be.[^s6] In November 1935 Lillian and William Gobitas were expelled from school in Minersville, Pennsylvania; on June 3, 1940, the Supreme Court ruled 8–1 in *Minersville School District v. Gobitis* (the caption misspelled the family's name) that the requirement was constitutional.[^s7] Mob violence against Witnesses followed: a Kingdom Hall was destroyed in Litchfield, Illinois, and a Witness in Nebraska was castrated.[^s7]

On January 9, 1942, West Virginia's Board of Education made the salute and pledge compulsory. Expelled children could be treated as delinquents, and their parents fined up to $50 and jailed for up to 30 days.[^s6] Walter Barnett's family sued, and on June 14, 1943, the Supreme Court overruled *Gobitis*.[^s7] Justice Robert Jackson wrote that no official may prescribe what is orthodox in "politics, nationalism, religion, or other matters of opinion."[^s6] Since then the pledge has been voluntary in public schools.[^s5]

**"Under God."** The Knights of Columbus, a Catholic fraternal organization, campaigned to add a reference to God.[^s4] Congress added "under God" after "one Nation" by an act signed on June 14, 1954; its House sponsor, Representative Rabaut, said the purpose was to set the United States apart from the officially atheist Soviet Union.[^s9][^s8] Signing it, President Dwight D. Eisenhower said schoolchildren would now daily proclaim "the dedication of our nation and our people to the Almighty."[^s10] Congress's 2002 findings date the change June 15, but the statute is dated June 14.[^s2][^s1]

Schools remain the pledge's main setting: California, for example, requires appropriate patriotic exercises in public elementary schools and counts reciting the pledge as meeting that requirement.[^s8] Since a 2013 amendment to the flag code, service members out of uniform and veterans may give the military salute in place of the hand over the heart.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

**Who wrote it.** Because the pledge appeared unsigned, both Bellamy and James B. Upham, a *Youth's Companion* editor, were later credited with it.[^s3] A committee of the U.S. Flag Association ruled for Bellamy in 1939, and a detailed Library of Congress report in 1957 supported that finding.[^s5]

**Whether "under God" is religious or patriotic.** In June 2002 a divided Ninth Circuit panel held, in a suit by Michael Newdow, an atheist father of a California second-grader, that the 1954 act and his daughter's school's recitation policy violated the Establishment Clause; the majority read the act's legislative history as having a religious purpose and found teacher-led recitation coercive.[^s8] Congress answered with near-unanimous resolutions and, on November 13, 2002, reenacted the pledge with findings stating that the court had "erroneously held" the phrase unconstitutional.[^s8][^s2] In 2004 the Supreme Court reversed on standing, holding that Newdow could not sue as his daughter's next friend under California custody law, and did not decide the question.[^s8] Concurring in the judgment, Chief Justice William Rehnquist, joined by Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, argued that the pledge is a patriotic exercise and "under God" a description rather than a prayer, Justice Clarence Thomas also concurred in the judgment, although, as Rehnquist noted, he regarded the recitation as coercive under the Court's precedents.[^s9] All three concluded that the policy did not violate the Establishment Clause.[^s8]

## Connections

The pledge was **introduced by** Francis Bellamy and first set out in *The Youth's Companion* in 1892, as the Flag Association and Library of Congress concluded.[^s5] It is **established by** the [[United States Code]], which fixes its wording and manner of delivery at 4 U.S.C. § 4, and it **symbolizes** the [[United States]], pledging loyalty to the republic the flag represents.[^s1] Its 1954 addition was **influenced by** the [[Cold War]], when Congress sought to contrast American religiosity with Soviet atheism.[^s9] Compulsory recitation **led to** [[West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette]], which protects the right not to salute or pledge.[^s7] It belongs with the other [[National symbols of the United States]], is addressed to the [[flag of the United States]], and was reaffirmed in the same 2002 law as the national motto [[In God We Trust]].[^s2]
