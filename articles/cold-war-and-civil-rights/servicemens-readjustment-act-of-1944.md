# Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944

The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, better known as the GI Bill or the GI Bill of Rights, was a federal law that helped World War II veterans go to school, train for jobs, buy homes, farms and businesses, and get by while they looked for work.[^s1][^s2] President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed it on June 22, 1944, two weeks after D-Day, saying it told the armed forces that "the American people do not intend to let them down."[^s3] It put higher education within reach of millions of veterans, but it is also much debated, because its benefits did not reach all veterans equally.[^s1][^s7]

## Background

Planning began well before the war's end. From 1942 the National Resources Planning Board studied postwar manpower, and in June 1943 it recommended education and training programs; the Labor Department estimated that as many as 15 million service members could be jobless after demobilization.[^s1] Memories of 1932, when World War I veterans of the Bonus Army marched on Washington demanding early payment of their bonuses, made leaders eager to avoid a repeat.[^s4]

The American Legion, a veterans' organization, designed the bill's main features and lobbied it through Congress.[^s1] In late 1943 the Legion drafted its own "GI Bill of Rights," with former national commander Harry Colmery sketching the ideas at the Mayflower Hotel in Washington, and a Legion publicist, Jack Cejnar, gave it the popular name.[^s4][^s1] The bill, S. 1767, passed both chambers unanimously in the spring of 1944.[^s1][^s2] Mississippi congressman John Rankin helped insert language letting state-level agencies administer benefits, which critics argue let Jim Crow states steer benefits away from Black veterans.[^s4]

## What it says

The act, Public Law 346 of the 78th Congress, applied to veterans who had served at least 90 days on or after September 16, 1940, and been discharged under conditions other than dishonorable.[^s2] Its main titles provided:[^s2][^s3]

- **Education and training:** a year of schooling, plus more up to the length of a veteran's service and a maximum of four years, with tuition and fees of up to $500 a school year and a living allowance of $50 a month, or $75 with dependents.[^s2]
- **Loans:** a Veterans Administration guarantee of up to half of a loan for a home, farm or business, capped at $2,000.[^s2]
- **Unemployment pay:** $20 a week for up to 52 weeks, the source of the shorthand "52–20."[^s2][^s4]
- **Hospitals and services:** new veterans' hospitals and job counseling, with the Veterans Administration declared an essential war agency.[^s2][^s3]

The law also said no federal agency could exercise "any supervision or control, whatsoever" over state educational agencies or schools, leaving much of its administration to states, colleges and private lenders.[^s2] Roosevelt noted that it left gaps, asking Congress to extend similar benefits to merchant seamen and Social Security credit to all service members.[^s3]

## Legacy

The scale was enormous. Within seven years about 8 million veterans had used its education benefits: about 2.3 million attended colleges and universities, and millions more took school-based or on-the-job training.[^s1] By 1947 veterans made up 49 percent of college students, and degrees awarded nationwide more than doubled between 1940 and 1950.[^s4][^s1] By 1955 the Veterans Administration had guaranteed 4.3 million home loans worth $33 billion, and veterans bought about a fifth of all new homes built after the war.[^s1] When the World War II education program ended in 1956 it had paid out $14.5 billion (National Archives).[^s1] Congress created successor programs for Korean War veterans in 1952, Vietnam-era veterans in 1966 and the all-volunteer force with the "Montgomery GI Bill" of 1984.[^s5]

The benefits were not shared equally. Under Jim Crow, most Black veterans in the South could use their education benefits only at about 100 segregated "Colleges for Negroes"; Black veterans nonetheless used education benefits at high rates, swelling enrollment at historically Black colleges.[^s4] Banks and local officials often refused them loans: in thirteen Mississippi cities, Black veterans received two of 3,200 VA-backed loans, and redlining and restrictive covenants kept many out of the new white suburbs.[^s4][^s1] Native veterans could not use VA home loans on reservation land, Mexican American veterans faced similar obstacles, about 50,000 service members, including many gay and lesbian service members, were barred by "blue" discharges, and merchant mariners were not recognized as veterans until 1988.[^s4] About 65,000 of roughly 332,000 eligible women veterans used the bill to attend college.[^s4]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians and social scientists agree on these facts but weigh them differently, and Ira Katznelson and Suzanne Mettler set out their disagreement in a joint 2008 dialogue.[^s7] Katznelson, in *When Affirmative Action Was White* (2005), argues that the bill's decentralized administration let the South discriminate against Black veterans, making it a channel of "white affirmative action" that widened racial gaps.[^s9] Lizabeth Cohen likewise argued that it did less to broaden higher education and homeownership than often believed, discriminated against African Americans, largely excluded women and favored those already advantaged.[^s8]

Mettler, drawing mainly on surveys and interviews with World War II veterans, sees a relatively inclusive program that told veterans government cared about people like them and made those who used it more civically and politically engaged.[^s8][^s7] Economists Sarah Turner and John Bound found a mixed picture: the war and the bill substantially raised college attainment for white men and for Black men born outside the South, but had little effect for Black veterans limited to southern schools, widening the Black–white gap among southerners.[^s6]

## Connections

Congress enacted the law and Franklin D. Roosevelt signed it in 1944.[^s2][^s3] The American Legion drafted and promoted it, and the memory of the Bonus Army shaped planners' fears.[^s1][^s4] It transformed American higher education, including historically Black colleges, where many Black veterans enrolled.[^s1][^s4] Its uneven administration under Jim Crow laws, segregation and redlining is central to debates over how federal policy shaped racial gaps in education and wealth.[^s4][^s9][^s6]
