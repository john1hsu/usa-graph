# McCarthyism

McCarthyism, also called the Second Red Scare, was the wave of domestic anticommunism that swept the United States in the first decade of the Cold War. Through federal loyalty reviews, congressional hearings, blacklists, loyalty oaths and new security laws, government and private employers pursued suspected Communists and "subversives," and thousands of people lost their jobs, often on anonymous evidence.[^s3][^s9] The era takes its name from Joseph McCarthy, a Republican senator from Wisconsin, whose sweeping and largely unsupported accusations from 1950 to 1954 made him its best-known figure.[^s6] This article dates the era from President Truman's loyalty order of March 1947 to McCarthy's death in 1957, but historians stress that the campaign started earlier and lasted longer than McCarthy's own career.[^s9][^s5]

## Background

Fear of radicals was not new: the House created a Special Committee on Un-American Activities, chaired by Martin Dies Jr. of Texas, in May 1938, and made the House Committee on Un-American Activities (HUAC) a permanent standing committee in January 1945.[^s4] After World War II, rivalry with the Soviet Union sharpened those fears. In 1949 the Soviets tested an atomic bomb and Communists won the Chinese civil war, and in 1950 Britain convicted the physicist Klaus Fuchs of passing Manhattan Project secrets to Moscow.[^s6] The Communist Party of the United States had only about 80,000 members at its peak, but it had real influence in some CIO unions and had been an early opponent of Jim Crow.[^s5]

## Key developments

**Loyalty programs.** In March 1947 President Harry S. Truman issued Executive Order 9835, which screened federal employees for loyalty and relied on an Attorney General's list of "subversive" organizations. Between 1947 and 1956 more than five million federal workers were screened, leading to about 2,700 dismissals and 12,000 resignations; Truman defended the program publicly while privately worrying that it could turn into a witch hunt.[^s3] State and local governments set up loyalty boards of their own, and in 1950 the University of California dismissed 31 professors who refused to sign a loyalty oath.[^s6][^s5]

**Congressional investigations.** HUAC's 1947 hearings on Hollywood led to contempt citations for the "Hollywood Ten," who refused to answer its questions, and to a blacklist that kept hundreds of film artists out of work for about a decade.[^s5] Witnesses who invoked the Fifth Amendment often lost their jobs.[^s6] In August 1948 Whittaker Chambers testified before HUAC, beginning the case against Alger Hiss, a former government official whom *The American Yawp* calls the highest-ranking official linked to Soviet espionage; Hiss was convicted of perjury after a first trial ended in a hung jury.[^s5]

**McCarthy's rise.** On February 9, 1950, McCarthy told a Republican women's club in Wheeling, West Virginia, "I have here in my hand a list of 205" State Department Communists.[^s13] He had no such list, and the figure soon shifted to 57 and then 81; a Senate subcommittee, the Tydings Committee, called his charges a "fraud and a hoax."[^s5] On June 1, 1950, Maine Republican Margaret Chase Smith and six Republican colleagues issued a Declaration of Conscience warning that the Senate had become "a forum of hate and character assassination."[^s11] From 1953 McCarthy chaired the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, which questioned 395 witnesses in 160 closed sessions during the 83rd Congress and targeted the Voice of America, State Department libraries and the Army.[^s1][^s6]

**Laws and purges.** The McCarran Internal Security Act of September 1950 required "communist organizations" to register, and the Communist Control Act of August 1954 effectively criminalized party membership.[^s5] Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, convicted in an atomic spy case, were executed on June 19, 1953.[^s5] The same fears drove the "Lavender Scare": anticommunists claimed gay employees were open to Soviet blackmail, and Eisenhower's Executive Order 10450 of April 27, 1953, barred gay men and lesbians from federal jobs.[^s6][^s8] On the West Coast waterfront nearly 3,800 seamen and dockworkers, many of them union activists and many African American, were screened out of work.[^s9]

**Downfall.** The televised Army-McCarthy hearings of 1954, watched by about 20 million people over 36 days, exposed McCarthy's tactics.[^s5][^s1] On June 9 Army counsel Joseph Welch, after McCarthy attacked a young lawyer on Welch's staff, asked him, "Have you no sense of decency?"[^s12] On December 2, 1954, the Senate voted 67 to 22 to censure him, after a bipartisan select committee chaired by Arthur Watkins of Utah reviewed the charges; McCarthy died in 1957, aged 48.[^s2][^s12]

## Legacy

The anticommunist campaign outlived McCarthy: civil rights activists, including Martin Luther King Jr., were attacked as communists by the John Birch Society into the late 1950s.[^s5] HUAC was renamed the Committee on Internal Security in 1969 and abolished in 1975.[^s4] The federal civil service ended its ban on gay employees in 1975, and Executive Order 10450 was formally repealed in 2017.[^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

**Was the threat real?** Starting in July 1995 the National Security Agency released about 3,000 Venona decrypts of Soviet intelligence cables, which confirmed that some Americans spied for Moscow and exposed Julius Rosenberg's spy ring.[^s7] Some historians, such as the *Journal of Cold War Studies* reviewer of Schrecker's documentary history, argue that this evidence, and the popularity of Truman's anticommunist measures, show that fears of Communist infiltration were not irrational, and they criticize accounts that focus only on the FBI and government.[^s10]

**Repression beyond the spies.** Ellen Schrecker and other critics of McCarthyism argue that it was a broad system of political repression, carried out by agencies, employers and unions together, that punished many people for their politics rather than for espionage, often without letting them see the evidence.[^s9] Even so, Schrecker accepts the case against Julius Rosenberg and considers it likely that Hiss was a Soviet agent.[^s10] Accounts still differ on Ethel Rosenberg: OpenStax concludes that Julius, but not Ethel, passed information, while the NSA's summary of Venona describes roles for both.[^s6][^s7]
