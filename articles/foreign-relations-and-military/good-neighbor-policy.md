# Good Neighbor Policy

The Good Neighbor policy was the approach to Latin America and the Caribbean that President Franklin D. Roosevelt adopted when he took office in 1933. It favored cooperation and trade over military force as the way to keep the hemisphere stable.[^s1] In his inaugural address of March 4, 1933, Roosevelt said that in world affairs he would dedicate the nation to "the policy of the good neighbor," one that respected its own obligations and the rights of others.[^s2] In practice the policy meant giving up the claimed right to intervene that the Roosevelt Corollary had asserted, and it became a pillar of hemispheric cooperation before and during World War II.[^s1][^s5]

## Origins

For three decades before 1933 the United States had repeatedly sent troops into Caribbean and Central American countries. Marines occupied Haiti from July 1915, after the assassination of its president, controlling its finances and its gendarmerie; the occupation provoked a peasant rebellion in 1919–1920 against policies that included racial segregation, press censorship and forced labor.[^s6] In Nicaragua, Marines were deployed from 1912 to 1925 and returned in 1926, and critics argued that the second intervention damaged U.S. standing across Latin America.[^s5] There the Marines fought an insurgency led by Augusto Sandino while training a new Guardia Nacional to take over security.[^s8]

Growing Latin American opposition to these interventions, and criticism inside the United States, pushed policy in a new direction during the Coolidge and Hoover administrations.[^s5] The last Marines left Nicaragua on January 2, 1933, before Roosevelt was inaugurated.[^s8] Historian William E. Leuchtenburg credits Herbert Hoover with starting the good neighbor initiative, which Roosevelt then continued and expanded.[^s4]

## How it works

The policy's core was a pledge of non-intervention. At the Pan-American conference at Montevideo in December 1933, Secretary of State Cordell Hull backed a declaration, favored by most governments in the hemisphere, that no state had a right to intervene in another's affairs.[^s1] The resulting Convention on Rights and Duties of States, signed on December 26, 1933, states in Article 8: "No state has the right to intervene in the internal or external affairs of another."[^s3] Hull, however, attached a reservation: the United States would follow its own policies and the generally accepted law of nations, while assuring other governments they need not fear U.S. intervention under Roosevelt.[^s3] Later that month Roosevelt declared U.S. policy to be "one opposed to armed intervention."[^s1] At the Buenos Aires conference of December 1936, which Roosevelt attended in person, the United States accepted an additional protocol on non-intervention without reservation, as Latin American states had sought.[^s9]

Concrete steps followed. In 1934, at Roosevelt's direction, the 1903 treaty with Cuba based on the Platt Amendment, which had given the United States the right to intervene there, was abrogated.[^s1] The same year U.S. forces formally withdrew from Haiti, though the United States kept certain economic ties.[^s6] The 1934 Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act let the president extend most-favored-nation trade terms to countries that signed agreements, and the Export-Import Bank, created that year, was financing projects across Latin America by the end of the decade.[^s4][^s5]

The hardest test came from Mexico. On March 18, 1938, President Lázaro Cárdenas expropriated the assets of nearly all foreign oil companies in Mexico.[^s7] Roosevelt rejected calls for intervention and had the State Department negotiate compensation.[^s4] Washington accepted Mexico's right to expropriate with compensation; an early note from Hull suspending U.S. silver purchases was treated as undelivered after the U.S. ambassador objected and the Treasury opposed it, and Roosevelt wanted good relations partly to keep Mexico from aligning with the Axis powers.[^s7] Under the Cooke-Zevada agreement of April 18, 1942, Mexico agreed to pay about $29 million to American firms.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what the policy did but weigh it differently. Leuchtenburg calls it Roosevelt's greatest foreign-policy success before the wars in Europe and Asia took center stage, citing the departure of the last U.S. troops from the Caribbean, the abrogation of the Platt Amendment and the restraint shown toward Mexico.[^s4] The Office of the Historian presents it as a deliberate break with the Roosevelt Corollary and the interventions of the 1910s and 1920s, though it notes that economic problems at home and World War II soon drew attention away from the hemisphere.[^s1]

Mark T. Berger stresses continuity of influence by other means. Marines and gunboats gave way to diplomats, economic and military advisers, loans and private capital; reciprocal trade treaties deepened Central America's dependence on U.S. markets; and new military ties linked Washington to Latin American armies.[^s5] He calls U.S. support for dictators a "major contradiction" of the policy, pointing to Anastasio Somoza in Nicaragua, Rafael Trujillo in the Dominican Republic and Fulgencio Batista in Cuba.[^s5] He adds that the Cold War sharpened the contradiction, with the CIA-backed overthrow of Guatemala's elected government in 1954, the Bay of Pigs invasion in 1961 and the U.S. landing in the Dominican Republic in 1965.[^s5] Historians also differ on where the policy began: Leuchtenburg gives Hoover the credit for starting it, while Berger describes it as growing out of both the Coolidge and Hoover years before Roosevelt formally adopted it.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

The Good Neighbor policy reversed the Roosevelt Corollary, Theodore Roosevelt's 1904 claim of a U.S. police power in the hemisphere, and with it the interventionist reading of the Monroe Doctrine.[^s1] Franklin D. Roosevelt introduced it in his first inaugural address and carried it out with Secretary of State Cordell Hull, building on the withdrawals begun under Herbert Hoover.[^s2][^s1][^s4] It brought the nineteen-year occupation of Haiti to a formal end in 1934, and it shaped Washington's negotiated response to Mexico's oil expropriation.[^s6][^s4] Its commitments were written into the Montevideo convention of 1933 and the Buenos Aires protocol of 1936, and it laid groundwork for hemispheric cooperation during World War II.[^s3][^s9][^s5]
