# Brown v. Board of Education

*Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka* is the U.S. Supreme Court decision of May 17, 1954, in which the Court ruled unanimously that segregating children in public schools solely on the basis of race violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, even where buildings and other tangible factors are equal. Writing for the Court, Chief Justice Earl Warren concluded that "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal," and the decision overruled *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) as applied to public education.[^s1] It is one of the most consequential rulings in American history and a turning point in the civil rights era, but it did not by itself desegregate the nation's schools.[^s3]

## Background

In *Plessy v. Ferguson*, a case about railroad cars, the Supreme Court had accepted state-mandated racial separation so long as facilities were nominally equal. Six later Supreme Court cases on public education turned on that "separate but equal" rule; the last two, the graduate-school cases *Sweatt v. Painter* and *McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents*, found specific programs unequal without questioning the doctrine itself.[^s1] The NAACP and its Legal Defense and Educational Fund, whose first director was Thurgood Marshall, began by attacking segregation in graduate and professional schools and around 1950 moved to a direct assault on segregation as such.[^s5] That strategy built on the work of Charles Hamilton Houston and relied on local attorneys, families, activists and researchers as well as Marshall.[^s3]

*Brown* was not the first challenge to segregated schools: *Roberts v. City of Boston* came in 1849, and Kansas courts heard eleven such cases between 1881 and 1949.[^s3] The cases that reached the Court in the early 1950s came from four states and the capital. In Prince Edward County, Virginia, 16-year-old Barbara Rose Johns led a student walkout on April 23, 1951, from Robert Russa Moton High School, which held 477 students in a building meant for 180; NAACP lawyers Oliver W. Hill and Spottswood Robinson III filed suit a month later.[^s6] *Briggs v. Elliott* in South Carolina exposed severe inequalities in buildings, buses and equipment; Delaware's *Gebhart v. Belton* produced the only lower-court order admitting Black students to white schools; and in Topeka, Kansas, where facilities were substantially comparable, the case squarely challenged segregation itself.[^s3] There the local NAACP organized a class action of 13 parents on behalf of 20 children.[^s3]

## The decision

The Court consolidated the Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia and Delaware cases, heard argument in December 1952, and then asked the lawyers to return with answers to specific questions; reargument began in December 1953.[^s4][^s5] Robert L. Carter argued the Kansas case, Marshall argued the South Carolina case, and the veteran lawyer John W. Davis defended segregation for South Carolina and Virginia.[^s4] Warren, newly appointed by President Dwight D. Eisenhower, worked deliberately for a single opinion with no dissents or separate concurrences, and proposed postponing the question of how to carry out the ruling.[^s5]

The opinion found that the history of the Fourteenth Amendment was inconclusive about its intended effect on public education, so the framers' intent could not settle the question.[^s2] Relying on a lower-court finding from Kansas, the Court held that segregation sanctioned by law harmed Black children's motivation and development, a finding it said modern authority amply supported.[^s1] Among the studies it cited was a 1950 paper by psychologist Kenneth Clark, whose doll tests with Mamie Phipps Clark found that most Black children aged three to seven preferred a white doll to an otherwise identical Black one.[^s5] Because the Fourteenth Amendment binds the states, not the federal government, the District of Columbia case, *Bolling v. Sharpe*, was decided separately the same day under the Fifth Amendment.[^s3]

## Legacy

The 1954 ruling set no timetable, and the Court took up remedies separately in *Brown II*, decided on May 31, 1955.[^s1] That decision directed desegregation "with all deliberate speed" under federal district courts; Justice Felix Frankfurter supplied the phrase in place of "forthwith," which Marshall had urged.[^s5] Opponents used that vague standard to delay. On March 12, 1956, Representative Howard Smith of Virginia introduced the Southern Manifesto, signed by 19 senators and 82 representatives from former Confederate states, which called the ruling an abuse of judicial power and urged resistance by lawful means.[^s7] Virginia's leaders, led by Senator Harry F. Byrd Sr., adopted "massive resistance," and Prince Edward County stopped funding its public schools in June 1959 while a new private academy, aided by state tuition grants, served white students; the schools reopened only on September 8, 1964, after the Supreme Court's *Griffin* decision.[^s6] Elsewhere, families who joined the suits faced job loss, eviction, threats and violence, and tens of thousands of Black teachers and principals lost positions or leadership roles as schools merged.[^s3]

The decision also drew criticism on legal grounds: some constitutional scholars objected that it leaned on social science rather than precedent, and advocates of judicial restraint argued that the Court had in effect made new law.[^s1] Civil rights supporters were encouraged, and the Warren Court went on to decide major cases on race, criminal justice and voting.[^s1] Litigation over Topeka itself resumed in 1979 (*Brown III*), a reminder that ending legally required separation did not automatically bring integration or equal educational opportunity.[^s3]

## Connections

The decision **interprets** the Fourteenth Amendment, holding that its Equal Protection Clause forbids state-imposed school segregation, and so **protects** the right to equal protection of the laws.[^s1] It **overturns** *Plessy v. Ferguson*'s "separate but equal" doctrine in public education.[^s1] The opinion was **authored by** Chief Justice Earl Warren for a unanimous Court.[^s4] It **led to** the Southern Manifesto of 1956, in which Southern members of Congress denounced it,[^s7] and to Virginia's policy of massive resistance and the closing of Prince Edward County's schools.[^s6] It belongs to the wider history of school desegregation and the civil rights movement, was argued by lawyers of the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund including Thurgood Marshall, and was decided alongside *Bolling v. Sharpe*.[^s3][^s5]
