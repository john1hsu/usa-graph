# Roosevelt Corollary

The Roosevelt Corollary was President Theodore Roosevelt's extension of the Monroe Doctrine, announced in his annual message to Congress on December 6, 1904.[^s3] It held that the United States might intervene, as a last resort, in Western Hemisphere nations that failed to meet their obligations to foreign creditors or fell into disorder, so that European powers would have no excuse to intervene themselves.[^s1] A corollary is an extension of an earlier idea, but this one reversed much of the original: a doctrine meant to keep Europeans out became a justification for American intervention across the Caribbean and Central America.[^s2]

## Origins

In 1823 James Monroe had warned European powers against new colonization or interference in the Americas, a largely passive policy.[^s1] By the early 1900s the danger looked different. Latin American governments owed large sums to European bondholders, and European states were willing to use force to collect. In 1902, after Venezuela defaulted, British, German and Italian gunboats blockaded its ports, and many Americans feared such actions would undermine U.S. dominance in the region.[^s2] Roosevelt worried that a debt dispute like Venezuela's could turn into a European invasion.[^s1] The United States was also beginning to build an isthmian canal in Panama, raising the stakes of stability in the region.[^s3]

The Dominican Republic posed the test case. It owed about $32 million that its revenues could not service, to creditors in France, Belgium, Italy and Germany and to a U.S. firm, the San Domingo Improvement Company; competing pledges of its customs revenues raised the prospect of a foreign scramble for its customhouses.[^s5] Roosevelt developed the corollary amid these pressures.[^s5]

## How it works

In the December 1904 message Roosevelt argued that "chronic wrongdoing," or an impotence that loosened the ties of civilized society, might require intervention by a civilized nation, and that in the Western Hemisphere the United States might have to act.[^s3] In flagrant cases, he said, the country might be forced to exercise "an international police power."[^s2] He returned to the theme in his 1905 message; the 1904 message also denied any U.S. desire for territory in the hemisphere and pointed to Cuba, Venezuela and Panama as examples of U.S. policy.[^s2] The corollary was thus a statement in a presidential message to Congress, not a statute or treaty.[^s3][^s2]

The Dominican Republic became the first application. On January 20, 1905, President Carlos Morales, encouraged by U.S. minister Thomas C. Dawson, signed an executive agreement giving the United States control of the customhouses. Critics objected that it bypassed the Senate, and when Democratic opposition blocked action on a treaty, Roosevelt put the plan into effect on April 1, 1905, through a new executive agreement, a modus vivendi.[^s5] A receiver nominated by the U.S. president collected the duties; 45 percent went to the Dominican government and the rest, minus costs, was held in a New York bank for creditors.[^s5] Creditors accepted a reduction of the debt from more than $30 million to about $17 million, refinanced by a $20 million bond issue.[^s5]

A convention signed at Santo Domingo on February 8, 1907, made the arrangement formal. The U.S. Senate advised ratification on February 25 and Roosevelt proclaimed it on July 25, 1907.[^s4] Under its terms the U.S. president appointed a general receiver of Dominican customs, revenues went first to the receivership and bondholders and only then to the Dominican government, and the republic could neither increase its public debt nor change its import duties without U.S. agreement.[^s4] One scholarly account credits the receivership with about four years of what it calls "the unaccustomed blessings of financial solvency and political stability," before renewed revolts brought a more drastic U.S. intervention.[^s5]

Over the following decades the corollary justified U.S. intervention in Cuba, Nicaragua, Haiti and the Dominican Republic, as the United States increasingly used military force to restore internal stability in the region.[^s1] Undersecretary of State J. Reuben Clark's memorandum of December 17, 1928, challenged the corollary on technical legal grounds; at Montevideo in 1933 and Buenos Aires in 1936 the United States pledged non-intervention.[^s6] Franklin D. Roosevelt, the elder Roosevelt's distant cousin, officially abandoned the corollary in 1934 as part of the Good Neighbor policy.[^s3]

## Debates and interpretations

The corollary was controversial from the start. Anti-imperialists in the United States argued that it turned the country from an opponent of foreign intervention into an intervening power, and Latin American leaders, notably Argentine Foreign Minister Luis María Drago, objected.[^s3] Roosevelt's supporters believed it would foster prosperous, democratic governments in the hemisphere.[^s3]

Historians still weigh it differently. Frederick W. Marks III argues that Roosevelt's own use of the police power in Santo Domingo did little harm to U.S. standing, that it was Woodrow Wilson's later, democracy-promoting interventions that left lasting bitterness, and that the corollary faded chiefly for geopolitical reasons: new machinery for settling debt defaults existed, Germany had been defeated and Britain was friendly.[^s6] Gene A. Sessions, by contrast, describes three decades in which repeated interventions under the corollary taught Latin Americans to equate the Monroe Doctrine with imperialism, and concludes that by 1930 the policy had failed: opinion at home and abroad had turned against it, and the interventions rarely achieved their vague goals.[^s7] The State Department's Office of the Historian notes that, in the long run, the corollary said little about relations with Europe and served mainly to justify U.S. intervention.[^s1]

## Connections

The corollary grew directly out of the Monroe Doctrine, which Roosevelt claimed to be extending, and out of the 1902 Venezuelan crisis that convinced him European debt collection could become invasion.[^s1][^s2] Its first application was the Dominican customs receivership, formalized by the 1907 convention.[^s5][^s4] Its logic later framed interventions in Cuba, Nicaragua and the occupation of Haiti.[^s1][^s2] It was criticized by Latin American leaders such as Drago, and it was renounced by the Good Neighbor policy, which pledged the opposite course.[^s3][^s6]
