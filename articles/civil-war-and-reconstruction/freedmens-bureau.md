# Freedmen's Bureau

The Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, known as the Freedmen's Bureau, was a federal agency created in the War Department by an act of Congress on March 3, 1865.[^s1] Its job was to help formerly enslaved people move from slavery to self-sufficiency, and to aid white refugees, in a South devastated by the Civil War. New Georgia Encyclopedia author Edward Hatfield calls it the first organization of its kind.[^s5] It ran for seven years, did most of its work between June 1865 and December 1868, and was discontinued in June 1872.[^s1][^s3]

## Origins

Congress began debating a federal agency for freedpeople after the Emancipation Proclamation. Representative Thomas D. Elliot of Massachusetts introduced a bill, and the House passed it by just 69 to 67 on March 1, 1864.[^s3][^s2] The Senate debated for months; a conference bill was finally adopted, and President Abraham Lincoln signed the act the day it passed, March 3, 1865. The bureau was to last for the rest of the war and one year after.[^s2] Before then, relief for freedpeople and control of seized land had been split among army commanders and Treasury officials.[^s1]

The act also made land central. In January 1865 General William T. Sherman's Special Field Order No. 15 had set aside abandoned coastal land in Georgia and South Carolina for freedpeople.[^s5] The bureau's act authorized it to assign loyal refugees and freedmen no more than forty acres of abandoned or confiscated land each, protected for three years at a rent of no more than six percent of the land's value, with the right to buy it.[^s3]

## Powers and structure

President Andrew Johnson named Major General Oliver Otis Howard commissioner in May 1865. From Washington, Howard oversaw assistant commissioners in each state and sub-assistants and agents in the districts of the former Confederacy, the border states and the District of Columbia.[^s1][^s3] The bureau was chronically short of money and staff: no more than about 900 agents served at any one time, leaving large parts of the South unsupervised.[^s4]

Its work was broad. It issued rations and clothing, ran hospitals and refugee camps, supervised labor contracts between planters and freedpeople, settled disputes, legalized marriages made during slavery, and helped families reunite.[^s1] It sent officers into state courts to watch for discriminatory justice and set up tribunals of its own.[^s4][^s5] Later it helped Black soldiers and sailors claim back pay, bounties and pensions.[^s1] The National Park Service counts more than fifteen million rations given to destitute white and Black Southerners.[^s3]

Education was its best-remembered work. The bureau mostly paid for buildings and logistics while Northern missionary societies such as the American Missionary Association supplied teachers.[^s4][^s5] By 1870 it supported more than 1,500 schools with over 100,000 pupils, after spending over six million dollars on freedmen's education in five years.[^s3]

The bureau's limits showed fastest on land and labor. In the fall of 1865 Johnson overturned Sherman's order, and his pardons of former Confederates meant the bureau returned abandoned and confiscated land to its former owners; in Georgia, restoration was nearly complete by summer 1866, though freedpeople led by Tunis Campbell resisted.[^s5][^s4] The Park Service estimates that only about one-sixth of the 400,000-plus acres settled by some 10,000 freed families stayed in Black hands.[^s3] Instead, bureau policy steered freedpeople into contracts as farm workers for white landowners.[^s4] In Georgia, assistant commissioner Davis Tillson denied rations to able-bodied men, enforced vagrancy laws and allowed planters to pay with a share of the crop, a practice that foreshadowed sharecropping.[^s5]

Politics set the bureau's lifespan. Johnson vetoed an open-ended extension on February 19, 1866, calling it unnecessary, costly and an intrusion on the states; the Senate failed to override.[^s2] Congress passed a narrower bill over a second veto, and it became law on July 16, 1866, extending the bureau for two years — a clash that widened the split between Johnson and congressional Republicans.[^s2][^s4] White opponents petitioned against the bureau, ostracized its agents and burned schools, while Black Southerners generally trusted and used it.[^s4] By 1869 Congress had cut most of its funding.[^s3]

## Debates and interpretations

Judgments of the bureau have always diverged. Johnson and many white Southerners saw it as unneeded federal interference that favored one group.[^s2][^s4] W. E. B. Du Bois, writing in 1901–1903, gave a mixed but largely favorable verdict: he credited it with free labor, legal recognition for Black Southerners and, above all, the free common school, but called the land outcome a bitter disappointment and argued a permanent, better-funded agency could have done far more.[^s6] Later historians, summarized by Roberta Sue Alexander, call its grant of power revolutionary for its time while faulting it for not securing freedpeople's independence and land ownership.[^s4] State studies stress how much depended on individual officers, some egalitarian and some, like Tillson, more concerned with order than with freedpeople's interests.[^s5]

## Connections

The bureau was established by the Freedmen's Bureau Act of 1865 and extended by the Act of 1866 (`establishedBy`).[^s2] It sat within the War Department (`partOf`) and was led by Oliver Otis Howard (`ledBy`).[^s1] It shaped the lives of African Americans leaving slavery (`affected`), and its support for Black education helped open higher education to Black Americans in the years when Fisk, Howard, Hampton and Atlanta were founded, a root of historically Black colleges and universities (`ledTo`).[^s3][^s6] It belongs to the Reconstruction era and requires the Civil War and slavery as background. Its land policy began with Sherman's field order and ended with Johnson's restorations, pushing many freedpeople toward crop-share labor that foreshadowed sharecropping, and its fortunes were tied to the Freedman's Savings Bank, whose 1874 collapse left Black Southerners with even less economic security.[^s3][^s5][^s6]
