# Chicago

Chicago is Illinois's largest city and, at the 2020 census, the nation's third-largest, with 2,746,388 residents.[^s7] It grew from a few hundred settlers when it received its first town charter in 1833 to more than a million people by 1890, becoming the country's great junction of water and rail, a center of grain, meatpacking and steel, and a magnet for migrants.[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s7]

## Geography

The city lies along Lake Michigan, around the mouth of the Chicago River.[^s1][^s3][^s15] Its importance comes from a low divide: only a short portage separates the Great Lakes drainage from the rivers that flow to the Mississippi, a link the French explorer Louis Jolliet recognized in 1673.[^s1] Much of the area was marshland. The city's name is associated with Shikaakwa, the Myaamia (Miami) name for a wetland where wild leeks, garlic and onions gave the land a strong smell, and the Bodéwadmi (Potawatomi) called it the place of onions.[^s12] Beginning in 1832, U.S. officials drained wetlands across the region to attract settlers, eventually destroying 367,485 acres.[^s10]

## History

The Chicago portage was Indigenous land long before it was a city. Neshnabé people (Potawatomi, Odawa and Ojibwe) lived here until their removal in the 1830s, and Myaamia and other nations also have ties to the area.[^s10][^s11][^s12] The first non-Native person to establish a permanent post was Jean Baptiste Point DuSable, a trader generally described as born about 1745 in Saint-Domingue (now Haiti) to a French father and an enslaved African mother, though much of his early life is unknown. He married Kitihawa, a Potawatomi woman, built a large homestead near the river's mouth, and sold it in May 1800.[^s13] Accounts of his arrival range from 1778 to the late 1780s.[^s1][^s13]

The United States acquired a six-mile-square tract at the river's mouth by treaty in 1795 and built Fort Dearborn there in 1803.[^s1] The 1816 Treaty of St. Louis ceded land around the portage and drew an Indian Boundary Line limiting where Neshnabé people could live, and treaties in 1829 and 1832 ceded the remaining land under the present city.[^s10] At the 1833 Treaty of Chicago the federal government pressed the Potawatomi to give up their last lands in Illinois and Wisconsin and to move west of the Mississippi; some fled to Wisconsin and Canada instead.[^s1][^s9] The Newberry Library's Indigenous Chicago project describes such treaties as coercive and unequal, and stresses that they remain binding agreements.[^s10]

Chicago received its first city charter in 1837, with six wards and a mayor.[^s3] In 1848 the Illinois & Michigan Canal was completed, the first railroad opened and the Board of Trade was founded; by 1856 ten trunk railroads ended in the city, and the Union Stock Yard opened in 1865.[^s1][^s2] The population rose from 4,470 in 1840 to 298,977 in 1870.[^s7]

On the evening of October 8, 1871, fire broke out in a barn on DeKoven Street owned by Catherine and Patrick O'Leary. Driven by gale-force winds, the fire burned about 3.5 square miles of the city center, destroyed more than 18,000 structures, left a third of the city's 300,000 residents homeless and killed at least 300 people.[^s4] Who or what started it was never established: the police and fire board's nine-day inquiry said it was unable to determine the cause and found the O'Leary household asleep, and in 1884 the historian A. T. Andreas wrote that the cause "is now a mystery, and must ever remain so."[^s5][^s6] The story that Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lantern is a legend; the city formally exonerated her and the cow in 1997.[^s5][^s6] The stockyards, grain elevators and factory districts survived, and with worldwide charity the city rebuilt quickly.[^s1][^s4] By 1890 it was the nation's second-largest city, and in 1893 it hosted the World's Columbian Exposition.[^s1][^s7]

Industrial growth brought labor conflict. When workers at George Pullman's company town struck on May 11, 1894, the American Railway Union under Eugene V. Debs launched a national boycott of Pullman cars; a federal injunction and thousands of marshals and Army troops, deployed over the objections of Illinois Governor John Peter Altgeld, defeated it by mid-July, and clashes in the Chicago area left dozens dead.[^s14] To protect its drinking water, Chicago turned to the Sanitary and Ship Canal, built in the 1890s, which in 1900 permanently reversed the Chicago River to carry sewage away from Lake Michigan.[^s1][^s15]

Black Chicago numbered about 40,000 people in 1910. At least 50,000 Black southerners arrived between 1916 and 1920, drawn by wartime industrial jobs and encouraged by the *Chicago Defender*, and a second wave during and after World War II brought the Black population to 813,000 by 1960.[^s8] Migrants met hard limits: by 1910, 78 percent of Black Chicagoans lived in a narrow, overcrowded South Side "Black Belt," and they faced job discrimination and exclusion from unions. In 1919, five days of racial violence left 23 Black Chicagoans dead and about 300 wounded, and in 1946 violent white opposition blocked public housing outside crowded areas, contributing to segregated high-rise projects.[^s8]

## Today

The city's population peaked at 3,620,962 in 1950.[^s7] The region lost about 188,000 heavy-industry jobs in the 1980s alone, and services and tourism now drive its economy.[^s1][^s2] In 1983 Harold Washington won the mayoralty with 51.8 percent of the vote, becoming Chicago's first Black mayor.[^s16] Native people also returned: more than 5,000 moved to Chicago through a Bureau of Indian Affairs relocation program, and the American Indian Center, founded in 1953, still holds its annual powwow.[^s11] In June 2021 the City Council voted to rename Lake Shore Drive for DuSable, whom the city recognizes as its first permanent non-Indigenous settler.[^s13]

## Connections

Chicago lies in Illinois (`locatedIn`), which chartered it and later granted it home rule, and it borders Lake Michigan (`borders`), while the Chicago River runs through it.[^s3][^s15] Its rise followed the Erie Canal and the Illinois & Michigan Canal, which tied the Great Lakes to the Atlantic and the Mississippi River system.[^s1] Its related nodes include the Treaty of Chicago, the Great Fire and the cow legend, the Pullman Strike, the Great Migration and Harold Washington.[^s4][^s5][^s8][^s9][^s14][^s16]
