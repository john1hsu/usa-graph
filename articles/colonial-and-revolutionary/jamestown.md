# Jamestown Colony

Jamestown was the English fort and town founded in May 1607 on the James River in Virginia, which the National Park Service describes as the first permanent English settlement in North America.[^s1] It was built inside Tsenacomoco, the paramount chiefdom of the Powhatan people, and for most of the seventeenth century it served as the capital of the colony of Virginia.[^s2][^s3] Many of the patterns that shaped English America — a commercial charter colony, representative assembly, tobacco agriculture, war over Native land and the forced labor of Africans — first took hold there.[^s1][^s2]

## Geography

The settlers chose a marshy peninsula, now Jamestown Island, about fifty miles up the James River from the Chesapeake Bay.[^s2] The Virginia Company had told them to look for a place surrounded by water on three sides, far enough inland to defend against a Spanish attack, deep enough for ships to anchor close by, and not occupied by Native people.[^s1] The low, swampy ground met those military tests but was unhealthy: brackish river water and disease killed many colonists in the first years.[^s1] The land was not empty in any wider sense. Coastal Virginia was the realm of Wahunsenacawh, known to the English as Powhatan, who ruled more than 150 towns that paid him tribute from his capital at Werowocomoco on the York River.[^s3]

## History

King James I chartered the Virginia Company of London on April 10, 1606, and three ships — the *Susan Constant*, *Godspeed* and *Discovery* — sailed that December.[^s2] On May 13, 1607, 104 English men and boys selected the site, named it for the king, and by June 15 had finished a triangular fort with a bulwark at each corner.[^s1] Powhatan sent gifts of food to the struggling colony, and the Park Service judges that without Powhatan help it would most likely have failed.[^s1] Relations were tense from the start. Captain John Smith was captured in the winter of 1607–08 and taken to Werowocomoco; the familiar story that Pocahontas saved his life comes from Smith's later writings, and Encyclopedia Virginia considers it unlikely.[^s2][^s3]

When drought struck in 1609 and the English demanded more food than the Powhatans would give, war broke out and the Powhatans blockaded the fort.[^s1][^s2] During the winter of 1609–10, the "Starving Time," the colony fell from a peak of about 400 people in August 1609 to roughly 90 survivors; William Strachey put the losses at 80 to 90 percent.[^s1][^s2] In May 1610 Governor Thomas Gates decided to abandon Jamestown, but he turned back on learning that Lord De La Warr's relief fleet was arriving.[^s1] Archaeology has since confirmed the written accounts of desperation: in 2013 researchers announced that the remains of a 14-year-old English girl, called "Jane," showed cut marks that a Smithsonian study identified as evidence of survival cannibalism.[^s5]

Tobacco saved the colony financially. John Rolfe introduced a new strain in 1612, and it quickly became the company's cash crop.[^s1] The English kidnapped Pocahontas in 1613; her marriage to Rolfe in April 1614 ended the First Anglo-Powhatan War.[^s2][^s3] In 1619 three developments came together. On July 30, Governor George Yeardley convened the first representative legislative assembly in what is now the United States; the Virginia Company sent about 90 women to start families; and the first documented Africans were forcibly brought to Virginia to work the tobacco fields.[^s1] Whether those Africans were treated as indentured servants or as slaves is debated, but the Park Service notes that the evidence points to treatment closer to enslavement.[^s1]

Tobacco's hunger for land drove the colony deeper into Powhatan territory. On March 22, 1622, Opechancanough led a coordinated attack that killed 350 to 400 of roughly 1,200 colonists, though a warning from a Powhatan boy spared Jamestown itself.[^s1][^s2] The Crown revoked the Virginia Company's charter on May 24, 1624, and Virginia became a royal colony until the Revolution.[^s1] A second Powhatan attack in 1644 killed 350 to 400 of about 8,000 colonists; afterwards Opechancanough was captured and killed at Jamestown, and the Powhatan chiefdom was reduced to tributary status under treaty.[^s1] In September 1676 followers of Nathaniel Bacon burned the town during Bacon's Rebellion, and after a 1698 fire destroyed the statehouse the capital moved in 1699 to Middle Plantation, renamed Williamsburg, and Jamestown ceased to be a town.[^s1]

## Today

Jamestown Island is preserved by the National Park Service and Preservation Virginia as Historic Jamestowne.[^s1] For generations people believed the 1607 fort had washed into the James River, a story fed by erosion and by a seawall built in 1902. William Kelso's Jamestown Rediscovery project began digging on April 4, 1994, found within three seasons that the fort's remains survived on dry land near the old church tower, and has since recovered more than 4 million artifacts.[^s4] The site is now a place to study not only English beginnings but also the Powhatan world the colonists entered and the Africans forced into it.[^s1][^s3]
