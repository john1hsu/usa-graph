# Fifteenth Amendment

The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified on February 3, 1870, says that the right of U.S. citizens to vote may not be denied or abridged by the United States or any state "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude," and its second section gives Congress power to enforce that guarantee by appropriate legislation.[^s1] The last of the three Reconstruction Amendments, it was meant to secure the ballot for Black men after emancipation, but its narrow wording and weak enforcement allowed most Southern states to disfranchise Black voters for nearly a century.[^s1][^s2]

## Background

Before 1867 only five Northern states let Black men vote on the same terms as white men.[^s7] In 1867 Congress extended the vote to Black men in the District of Columbia and the territories and required the seceded states to guarantee Black male suffrage before they could be readmitted.[^s3] Black men then voted in large numbers in the South and helped write new state constitutions; by 1877 more than 2,000 had held office, including Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce, U.S. senators from Mississippi.[^s7] Statutes and readmission terms could be reversed, however, and the Fourteenth Amendment had only penalized states that denied the vote rather than forbidding it outright.[^s3][^s8]

After Ulysses S. Grant's election, the lame-duck session of the Fortieth Congress took up the question.[^s3] Representative George Boutwell proposed an amendment in the House on January 11, 1869, and rival versions followed, some of them broader.[^s9] Congress split three ways: members who opposed any federal guarantee of Black suffrage, members who wanted universal male suffrage that would also abolish property and education tests, and members willing to accept a narrower ban on racial qualifications.[^s3] The narrower group prevailed. A conference committee dropped broader language, including protection for officeholding, and Congress gave final approval on February 26, 1869, by votes of 144 to 44 in the House and 39 to 13 in the Senate.[^s9] Senator William Stewart of Nevada guided the measure through the Senate.[^s2] The framers also debated whether the amendment should cover women, Irish Americans and Chinese immigrants, and whether neutral-sounding tests could be used to get around it.[^s8]

## What it says

Section 1 forbids the federal government and the states from denying or abridging citizens' right to vote because of race, color or previous condition of servitude; Section 2 authorizes Congress to enforce the ban.[^s1] It does not say who may vote. States kept the power to set qualifications that applied equally to all races, and the amendment said nothing about sex.[^s2][^s7] In United States v. Reese (1876) the Supreme Court held that the amendment "does not confer the right of suffrage upon any one," but only forbids preferring one citizen over another on those grounds.[^s4] Later decisions read it more broadly: in 2000 the Court said it protects all persons and that ancestry can be a proxy for race.[^s4]

## Legacy

Black Americans celebrated ratification with events such as a Baltimore parade on May 19, 1870, and Congress passed Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871 that made it a crime to deprive freedpeople of their rights and allowed federal troops to protect them.[^s7] Ku Klux Klan terror and the waning of federal commitment ended Reconstruction by 1877, and the Supreme Court's 1876 decisions in Reese and United States v. Cruikshank limited federal prosecutions.[^s7][^s4] Southern states then wrote poll taxes, literacy tests and grandfather clauses into law to disfranchise Black voters while sparing many white voters.[^s1][^s2] In Guinn v. United States (1915) the Court unanimously struck down Oklahoma's grandfather clause, and in Lane v. Wilson (1939) it held that the amendment reaches subtle as well as overt discrimination.[^s5]

The amendment also divided the women's rights movement. Lucy Stone and Frederick Douglass supported it as an urgent protection for Black men, while Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony opposed any amendment that left out women, sometimes with openly racist arguments.[^s6] Frances Ellen Watkins Harper and Sojourner Truth pointed out that both sides overlooked Black women.[^s6] The American Equal Rights Association dissolved after its May 1869 meeting, and rival suffrage organizations took its place.[^s6]

Effective enforcement came only in 1965, when President Lyndon B. Johnson asked Congress for a law that would make it impossible to thwart the amendment; the Voting Rights Act that followed abolished remaining barriers and authorized federal oversight of registration.[^s1] In 2013 the Supreme Court struck down a key provision of that oversight.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

The standard account treats the amendment as a narrow compromise whose silence on poll taxes, literacy tests and officeholding invited evasion under Jim Crow.[^s8][^s2] Legal historian Travis Crum argues that its original understanding was broader: its framers meant it to forbid racial proxies such as ancestry and discriminatory application of neutral laws, to protect all races, and to give Congress a novel and independent enforcement power, making it in his view a firmer basis for voting-rights law than the Equal Protection Clause.[^s8] Historians also weigh the motives of its Republican sponsors; The American Yawp notes that many Republicans acted chiefly from party interest even as some, like Thaddeus Stevens, believed in racial equality.[^s7]

## Connections

The amendment amends the United States Constitution and was proposed by the United States Congress, which passed it in February 1869.[^s1][^s3] It protects the right to vote against racial discrimination and builds on the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantees.[^s1][^s3] Its enforcement clause led to the Voting Rights Act of 1965.[^s1] Its omission of sex split the women's suffrage movement, whose federal goal was reached only with the Nineteenth Amendment.[^s6][^s7]
