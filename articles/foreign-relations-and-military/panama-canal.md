  - question: Why did Panama, rather than Colombia, sign the 1903 canal treaty with the United States?

  - question: How did the gold and silver rolls shape life for the canal's workers?
    answer: Skilled white workers, mostly from the United States, were paid in gold on the gold roll and got better housing, dining and clubs; the mostly Black West Indian laborers on the silver roll were paid in local silver at about half the wage, lived in crowded camps, did the most dangerous work such as dynamiting, and died at far higher rates.
  - question: Why did the United States succeed in building a canal where the French effort had failed?
    answer: The French were defeated by disease and bankruptcy after about 20,000 deaths; the Americans first ran a large sanitation campaign under William Gorgas that ended yellow fever in Panama City by 1905 and cut malaria deaths sharply, and they switched to a lock design, which Stevens argued could be finished about a decade sooner than a sea-level cut.

# Panama Canal

The Panama Canal is a waterway across the Isthmus of Panama that joins the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. The United States built it between 1904 and 1914 on a strip of land it controlled under a 1903 treaty, and the Office of the Historian describes it as a symbol of American technological and economic power that later became an irritant in relations with Panama.[^s1] Ships are lifted by locks to Gatun Lake, about 85 feet above sea level, and lowered again on the far side.[^s7] The canal opened on August 15, 1914, and passed to Panama on December 31, 1999.[^s6][^s2]

## History

Americans had long eyed a canal across Central America. In 1850 the United States and Britain signed the Clayton-Bulwer Treaty to limit their rivalry over a planned Nicaraguan route, and in 1901 the Hay-Pauncefote Treaty freed the United States to build and run a canal alone.[^s1] Meanwhile a French company led by Ferdinand de Lesseps, builder of the Suez Canal, had started digging in Panama, then a province of Colombia, in 1880. Malaria, yellow fever and bankruptcy ended that effort after about nine years and roughly 20,000 deaths.[^s1]

On June 19, 1902, the Senate chose the Panama route. Secretary of State John Hay then signed a treaty with Colombian diplomat Tomás Herrán, but Colombia's congress rejected it over the money.[^s1] President Theodore Roosevelt sent warships to Colón and Panama City, and on November 3, 1903, Panama declared independence; the United States recognized the new republic on November 6.[^s1][^s9] Panama's envoy was Philippe Bunau-Varilla, a French engineer from the old canal company who had not lived in Panama for seventeen years and who signed the treaty on November 18 without formal consent from Panama's government.[^s2] The Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty guaranteed Panama's independence and granted the United States, in perpetuity, a zone ten miles wide, five miles on each side of the canal line.[^s3] Within it the United States could act as it would "if it were the sovereign of the territory," excluding Panama's authority, in return for $10 million and an annual payment of $250,000.[^s3]

Construction began on May 4, 1904. Dr. William Gorgas arrived with the first staff, and his sanitation campaign of draining, oiling and screening ended yellow fever in Panama City by November 1905; the malaria death rate among employees fell from 7.45 per 1,000 in 1906 to 0.30 per 1,000 in 1913.[^s5][^s10] Chief engineer John F. Stevens persuaded Roosevelt and Congress to build locks rather than a sea-level cut, and the House approved that design on June 27, 1906; Gatun Lake's filling displaced many residents of the Chagres valley.[^s5] Army engineer George Washington Goethals took over in April 1907, and the workforce peaked at 44,733 in March 1913.[^s5] The cement boat *Ancon* made the first official transit on August 15, 1914, in a modest ceremony scaled back because of World War I.[^s6] The Panama Canal Authority estimates the U.S. cost at about $375 million, the costliest American construction project to that time.[^s6]

The canal was built mainly by Black workers from the Caribbean. By 1907, more than three quarters of the 24,000 laborers came from the West Indies, especially Barbados.[^s11][^s8] The Isthmian Canal Commission divided employees into a "gold roll" of skilled, mostly white Americans paid in gold and a "silver roll" of mostly Black laborers paid in local silver currency.[^s11] The University of Florida's centennial exhibit describes this as a system of segregation built to control a diverse workforce, and silver-roll workers lived in crowded camps while West Indian women ran businesses that fed and served them.[^s8] Silver-roll wages were about half of gold-roll pay, many facilities had separate entrances, and in early 1906 the death rate for non-white workers was 59 per 1,000 against 17 for white employees.[^s10] Hospital records list 5,609 deaths during the American construction era.[^s6]

## Significance

At its opening the canal was hailed as a major foreign-policy achievement and a symbol of American technological and economic power.[^s1] Roosevelt's presidential library calls it both his most audacious foreign-policy achievement and his most controversial.[^s9] It also bred lasting resentment in Panama. Many Panamanians questioned the 1903 treaty, and in 1964 a riot over the right to fly Panama's flag in the Canal Zone briefly broke off diplomatic relations.[^s2] Talks led to two treaties signed by President Jimmy Carter and Panama's leader Omar Torrijos on September 7, 1977: a Neutrality Treaty letting the United States defend the canal's neutrality permanently, and the Panama Canal Treaty, which ended the Canal Zone on October 1, 1979, and transferred the canal on December 31, 1999.[^s2] The Senate approved them on March 16 and April 18, 1978, each by 68 to 32, one vote more than two-thirds, with support from Majority Leader Robert Byrd and Minority Leader Howard Baker, while opponents such as Strom Thurmond warned against giving up the canal.[^s2][^s4] Panama has administered the canal since 1999.[^s2]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians and commentators still disagree about how to judge the founding of the canal. Roosevelt boasted at Berkeley in 1911, "I took the Isthmus," but his own presidential library calls that something of an exaggeration and lists open questions: how directly he orchestrated the revolution, whether he acted from sympathy or strategy, and whether he broke international or constitutional limits.[^s9] Defenders argue the canal was strategically essential and that Panama would have won independence anyway; critics see a violation of Colombian sovereignty and a precedent for U.S. intervention in Latin America.[^s9] The Office of the Historian stresses that Bunau-Varilla signed without his government's formal approval, a fact behind Panama's decades of protest.[^s2] Other scholars put the workers at the centre, presenting the canal as a feat of West Indian labour carried out under racial segregation and heavy loss of life.[^s8][^s10]

## Connections

- **Panama:** the canal lies in the Republic of Panama, whose independence from Colombia in 1903 was bound up with the canal deal.[^s1]
- **Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty:** the 1903 treaty established the Canal Zone and the U.S. right to build and run the canal.[^s3]
- **Torrijos-Carter Treaties:** decades of Panamanian objection to U.S. control led to the 1977 treaties that ended the Canal Zone and returned the canal.[^s2]
- **Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute:** the Smithsonian's research station in Panama grew out of canal construction and the fight against yellow fever and malaria.[^s7]
- **Theodore Roosevelt:** he backed Panama's independence, pushed the project through and, in 1906, made the first foreign trip by a sitting president to inspect it.[^s9]
