# Thomas Jefferson

Thomas Jefferson was a Virginia planter, lawyer and revolutionary who drafted the Declaration of Independence and served as the third president of the United States from 1801 to 1809.[^s1] He is remembered both for some of the most influential statements of liberty and equality in American history and for holding hundreds of people in slavery throughout his adult life, a contradiction that historians, his descendants and the public continue to weigh.[^s1][^s8]

## Life and work

Jefferson was born on April 13, 1743, at Shadwell, a plantation in western Virginia, the son of Peter Jefferson and Jane Randolph Jefferson.[^s2] He entered the College of William and Mary in 1760, studied law, and was admitted to the bar in April 1767.[^s2][^s1] He sat in the Virginia House of Burgesses from 1769 to 1774, and in 1774 wrote *A Summary View of the Rights of British America*.[^s2] He married Martha Wayles Skelton on January 1, 1772; she died after childbirth on November 6, 1782.[^s2]

Sent to the Second Continental Congress, Jefferson drafted the Declaration of Independence in 1776, including its claim that all men are created equal.[^s1] He later served two years as Virginia's governor during the war.[^s1] His bill for religious freedom, drafted in 1777, stalled until James Madison revived it; the General Assembly adopted it on January 16, 1786. It barred compelled support for any church, separated church and state in Virginia, and is generally credited with shaping the religion clauses of the First Amendment.[^s9] After about four years as minister to France from 1785, he became secretary of state under George Washington in 1790, resigning in 1793 after bitter disputes with Alexander Hamilton over the power of the national government.[^s2][^s1]

Jefferson finished second in the 1796 electoral vote and so became John Adams's vice president.[^s1] In 1800 he and his running mate, Aaron Burr, each received 73 electoral votes, because electors did not then distinguish between candidates for president and vice president. The Federalist-controlled House of Representatives cast 35 deadlocked ballots before electing Jefferson on the 36th, on February 17, 1801.[^s10]

As president, Jefferson sought a frugal federal government.[^s1] In 1803 the United States bought Louisiana from France for $15 million, roughly doubling the nation's size. Jefferson doubted that the Constitution allowed the purchase and considered an amendment, but set aside his strict reading of federal power; the Office of the Historian notes that the decision helped build the principle of implied powers.[^s3][^s1] He sent Meriwether Lewis to explore the West from 1804 to 1806.[^s1] His plans for the West also reached into Native homelands. In a private letter of February 1803 to William Henry Harrison, governor of Indiana Territory, Jefferson proposed government trading houses that would lead Native leaders into debt they would pay off by ceding land, and he expected Native nations either to become citizens or to "remove beyond the Missisipi."[^s4]

On March 2, 1807, Jefferson signed the act banning the importation of enslaved people from January 1, 1808, the earliest date Article I of the Constitution allowed; the law still regulated the shipment of enslaved people between American ports.[^s12] Facing British and French seizures of American ships and the British impressment of sailors, he answered with the Embargo Act of December 1807, which kept American ships in port. It hurt the American economy, encouraged smuggling and grew unpopular, and he signed its replacement, the Non-Intercourse Act, on March 1, 1809, three days before leaving office.[^s11]

In retirement at Monticello he founded the University of Virginia, and he died there on July 4, 1826, fifty years after the Declaration was adopted.[^s1] Over his lifetime he owned more than 600 people; about 200 at a time lived on his plantations, up to 140 of them at Monticello. Despite writing of his wish to ease slavery's conditions, he sold 70 people in 1790 to pay debts.[^s8] Sally Hemings, an enslaved woman who lived in Paris with Jefferson's household from 1787 to 1789, had at least six children; Jefferson freed two of her sons in his 1826 will but never freed Hemings herself.[^s5]

## Legacy

Jefferson chose his own epitaph: author of the Declaration, author of the Virginia statute for religious freedom, and father of the University of Virginia, "and not a word more", so the stone does not mention the presidency.[^s7] His name is also attached to sayings he never wrote: the Thomas Jefferson Foundation finds no evidence for the slogan about the government that governs least, which first appeared in print in 1837 and was first credited to him in 1853.[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

Readers of the record weigh Jefferson's ideals and expansion of the nation against his enslavement of hundreds of people and his plans for Native land.[^s1][^s4][^s8] The question of his relationship with Sally Hemings shows how these readings have shifted. Her son Madison Hemings stated in 1873 that Jefferson was his father. A 1998 DNA study linked male-line Jefferson descendants with a descendant of Eston Hemings, and in 2000 a Thomas Jefferson Foundation research committee concluded that Jefferson was very probably Eston's father and likely the father of all six children.[^s5] A 2001 report by the Scholars Commission of the Thomas Jefferson Heritage Society dissented, judging his paternity very unlikely and pointing to his brother Randolph; the foundation's account presents both conclusions.[^s5]

## Connections

Jefferson **held the office** of president from 1801 to 1809, and before that **held the offices** of secretary of state under Washington and vice president under Adams.[^s1] He was a **member of** the Second Continental Congress, which appointed him in 1776 and for which he drafted the Declaration of Independence.[^s1] He **participated in** the Louisiana Purchase, setting aside his doubts about its constitutionality to complete it.[^s3] His administration also produced the 1807 ban on importing enslaved people and the Embargo Act, and his ideas tie him to the Virginia statute for religious freedom and the University of Virginia.[^s12][^s11][^s9][^s7]
