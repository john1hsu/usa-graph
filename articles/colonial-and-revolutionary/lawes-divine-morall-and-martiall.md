# Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall

The *Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall* were the orders that governed the English colony in Virginia from 1610 to 1619. Issued by the Virginia Company of London's military governors after the Starving Time had nearly destroyed Jamestown, they regulated worship, work, food, livestock, sanitation and trade with Native people, and they punished many offenses with whipping, mutilation or death.[^s1][^s2] Encyclopedia Virginia calls them the earliest surviving body of English-language laws in the Western Hemisphere, but stresses that they were not a legal code in the modern sense: no legislature made them, no court applied them, and they ignored English common-law procedures such as jury trial even though the company's charters promised colonists the rights of Englishmen.[^s1]

## Background

The winter of 1609–1610 killed most of Jamestown's colonists. When Sir Thomas Gates arrived from Bermuda on May 24, 1610, after the wreck of the *Sea Venture*, he found about sixty malnourished survivors.[^s4] The National Park Service describes a colony that had "collapsed into chaos" through disease, drought, hunger and infighting after John Smith's departure in 1609.[^s2] Leaders and promoters blamed the famine largely on the colonists' supposed idleness, and the survivors who were turned back to Jamestown in June 1610 found themselves under a new, stricter regime.[^s6]

The company's 1609 charter allowed it to send a military governor, and Gates, Lord De La Warr and Sir Thomas Dale were all career army officers.[^s1] Gates set out the first orders on the day he landed; De La Warr, the new governor, confirmed them and added his own on June 12, 1610; and Dale, who reached Virginia in May 1611 with about 300 armed soldiers, reissued and enlarged them on June 22, 1611.[^s3][^s5] The colony's secretary, William Strachey, compiled the orders and published them in London in 1612.[^s1]

## What it says

The civil orders that applied to all colonists run to thirty-seven numbered articles, which Strachey said were published to take away the plea of "I did not know."[^s2][^s3] They begin with religion: officers were to see that God was served daily, everyone had to attend services twice a day when the bell rang, and blasphemy was punishable by death.[^s3] Speaking traitorous words against the king, or speaking against or disobeying the colony's governors and officers, also carried severe or capital penalties.[^s3]

Many articles protected the colony's scarce food and goods. Killing a bull, cow, calf, horse or other livestock without the general's permission was a capital crime, stealing from gardens and cornfields was severely punished, and selling company goods to sailors for private profit was forbidden.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Tradesmen had to attend their work daily, soldiers and workers had to report at the beat of the drum, and the code set rules for keeping houses clean and for not washing clothes or relieving oneself near the new well.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

The orders also reflected the war with the Powhatan paramount chiefdom. Article 15 declared that "no man of what condition soever shall barter, trucke, or trade with the Indians" without authority, on pain of death.[^s3] Taking goods by force from Native people who came to trade was a capital crime, and running away to live among Native communities was forbidden, as hungry colonists had done during the siege of 1609–1610.[^s2]

Punishments escalated with repeat offenses: loss of rations, whipping, a bodkin driven through the tongue, service in the galleys, and finally death.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Dale's law martial, which fills most of the printed book, was a standard military code setting out the duties of officers and soldiers.[^s1]

## Legacy

Dale enforced the code harshly. Encyclopedia Virginia records that a man who stole food was tied to a tree and left to starve as a warning, and that Dale was sharply criticized, then and later, for governing by brutal martial law.[^s5] The same years saw Dale wage war on the Nansemond and Appamattuck, found Henricus and Bermuda Hundred, the latter on Appamattuck land, and hold Pocahontas hostage from 1613.[^s4][^s5] By the time he left in 1616, the colony had become largely self-sufficient; Dale wrote that he had returned from the hardest task he ever undertook.[^s5]

Government under the *Lawes* ended on April 18, 1619, when George Yeardley arrived as governor under the company's reformed charter and told colonists they would have the same common law as in England.[^s1][^s4] From July 30 to August 4, 1619, the first General Assembly met at Jamestown; the Park Service calls the change the forerunner of the government Americans still have.[^s2][^s4] Legal historians still study the code as an early, non-common-law strand in Virginia's criminal justice; Encyclopedia Virginia's reading list includes David Thomas Konig's 1982 article on that theme.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

**Martial law for everyone?** The National Park Service describes the period from 1609 to 1619 as one of martial law at Jamestown, with laws aimed at officers, laborers and farmers alike.[^s2] Brent Tarter argues that this is a common misunderstanding: most of the printed text was a code for soldiers that Dale probably did not apply to civilians, and the familiar name "Dale's Laws" is misleading because Dale was not the main author.[^s1]

**Exceptional cruelty or standard practice?** The punishments look extreme today, and contemporaries criticized Dale's rule.[^s5] Tarter notes that the codes were not notably harsher than orders English officers used in Ireland and elsewhere.[^s5]

**Did discipline save the colony?** The Park Service concludes that the code held the colony together and had served its function by 1619, and Encyclopedia Virginia credits strict enforcement with restoring order.[^s1][^s2] Historian Rachel B. Herrmann, writing about the famine, cautions that leaders used the memory of starvation to justify laws controlling the production and distribution of food, and McCartney adds that immigration, military success and tobacco also helped Virginia survive.[^s6]

## Connections

- It was **authored by** Sir Thomas Gates, who issued the first orders on May 24, 1610, and by Sir Thomas Dale, who enlarged them in 1611; Lord De La Warr added orders of his own, and William Strachey compiled and published them.[^s3][^s1]
- It **affected** the Colony of Virginia, which the Park Service says the code held together until representative government began in 1619.[^s2]
- It followed directly from the Starving Time at Jamestown and was replaced in 1619 when the Virginia Company created the General Assembly.[^s6][^s4]
- Related: the Virginia Company of London, the Powhatan paramount chiefdom and the Anglo-Powhatan Wars, which shaped its rules on trade and desertion.[^s1][^s2]
