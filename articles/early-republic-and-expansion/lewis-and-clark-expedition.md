# Lewis and Clark Expedition

The Lewis and Clark Expedition, whose members called themselves the Corps of Discovery, was a U.S. Army journey of about 8,000 miles that left the Mississippi River in May 1804, reached the Pacific Ocean in November 1805 and returned to St. Louis in September 1806.[^s2][^s3] Commissioned by President Thomas Jefferson and led by Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, it produced the most accurate maps of western North America then available and recorded hundreds of plants and animals new to U.S. science.[^s2] It also carried the claims of a new nation into the homelands of more than fifty Native nations, whose help kept it alive.[^s2][^s7]

## Background

Jefferson had planned a western expedition before the United States bought Louisiana from France. On January 18, 1803, he sent Congress a confidential message asking for $2,500 to explore the Missouri River and the nations living along it, framed as a way to extend U.S. commerce. He argued that secrecy would keep rivals from obstructing the plan. The same message proposed government trading houses and urged that Native nations be led toward farming so they would need less land and sell the rest.[^s5]

Lewis trained with Philadelphia scientists and in June 1803 asked Clark to share command.[^s3] Jefferson's instructions of June 20, 1803 told Lewis to find the most direct and practicable water route across the continent for commerce, to fix latitude and longitude at key points, and to describe soils, plants, animals, minerals and climate. Lewis was to treat Native peoples in a friendly and conciliatory way, record their languages, customs and trade, and turn back rather than fight a superior force.[^s1]

## What happened

After wintering at Camp Wood, near where the Missouri meets the Mississippi, the Corps started upriver on May 14, 1804. About 45 people set out, including soldiers, a French-Indian interpreter, Lewis's Newfoundland dog Seaman, and York, a man enslaved by Clark.[^s3] Sergeant Charles Floyd died in August, probably of a ruptured appendix; he was the only member lost.[^s3] Councils with the Otoe, Missouria and Yankton Sioux went smoothly, but a September meeting with the Lakota, called Teton Sioux in the journals, nearly ended in bloodshed.[^s3]

The party wintered in 1804–1805 at Fort Mandan, beside the Mandan and Hidatsa villages in present-day North Dakota. There it hired the trader Toussaint Charbonneau, whose Lemhi Shoshone wife, Sacagawea, had been captured by a Hidatsa raiding party about four years earlier; she joined with their infant son, Jean Baptiste.[^s3][^s4] In April 1805 a keelboat took specimens, letters and maps back to St. Louis, and a smaller permanent party of about 33 continued west.[^s2][^s3]

In August 1805 the Corps reached the navigable limit of the Missouri, met the Shoshone, bought horses and crossed the Continental Divide at Lemhi Pass.[^s3] The Rockies proved to be a tangle of ranges rather than the short portage Jefferson had hoped for.[^s7] Crossing the Bitterroots on the Lolo Trail, the men were starving when they came out onto Weippe Prairie in September 1805. There the Nez Perce fed them dried salmon, camas and berries; Nez Perce tradition credits Watkuweis, a woman who had once been captured and treated kindly by white people, with persuading her family to welcome them.[^s10] The park that preserves these sites says the Nez Perce saved the expedition from starvation.[^s9]

Descending the Columbia, the Corps reached the Pacific in November 1805. The members voted on where to winter, and York and Sacagawea both cast votes, unusual for the time; they chose a site where they built Fort Clatsop, near present-day Astoria, Oregon.[^s2][^s3][^s6] On the way home in 1806 the captains split up to explore more country. Lewis's detachment fought Piegan Blackfeet on the Marias River, killing two men, the only deadly violence of the journey. The Corps reunited near the mouth of the Yellowstone and reached St. Louis on September 23, 1806, having spent about $40,000.[^s3]

## Aftermath and legacy

Clark's track map, published in 1814 with Nicholas Biddle's *History of the Expedition*, was the first published map to show the geography of the trans-Mississippi West with reasonable accuracy, and it drew on maps sketched for Clark by Shoshone, Nez Perce and Clatsop informants.[^s7] Jefferson's instructions became the model for federal exploration for the rest of the century, and later expeditions and surveys filled in the map until the boundary and railroad surveys of the 1850s.[^s7][^s11] Clark became superintendent of Indian affairs at St. Louis in 1807.[^s7]

The rewards were unequal. The other men received double pay and 320 acres of land, but York returned to slavery. Clark refused his requests for freedom, writing in 1808 that York's service did not warrant it, and freed him only sometime after 1815.[^s6] Sacagawea is generally believed to have died at Fort Manuel, in present-day South Dakota, in December 1812, though some writers later argued she lived until 1884.[^s4][^s8] For the Nez Perce, the friendship of 1805–1806 did not lead to the relationship promised; within a generation the flight of 1877 had ended hopes of lasting peace.[^s9] Since 1978 the route has been preserved as the Lewis and Clark National Historic Trail, extended in 2019 to include Lewis's preparatory journey from Pittsburgh.[^s2]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on the route and the dates but differ on what the expedition meant. The National Park Service stresses its maps, its scientific record and the democratic vote at the Pacific, presenting it as a landmark of exploration.[^s2] The Library of Congress shows it as an instrument of empire: the captains handed out at least 89 peace medals and told nations that the United States was now their "father," part of a strategy of designating chiefs with medals, certificates and uniforms that eased U.S. negotiations and weakened traditional leadership.[^s7] Jefferson's own message ties the trip to acquiring Native land.[^s5] James P. Ronda, writing from the Native side of the encounter, argues that inflating Sacagawea into a lone guide obscures the many Native men and women whose contributions were more fundamental.[^s8]
