# E pluribus unum

*E pluribus unum* is Latin for "Out of many, one." It is the motto on the front (obverse) of the Great Seal of the United States, where it appears on a scroll held in the eagle's beak, and it was meant to express the union of the thirteen states.[^s3] Congress adopted it with the seal on June 20, 1782, and federal law still requires it on the reverse of every U.S. coin.[^s3][^s4] Despite its age and familiarity, it has never been the country's official national motto: that title belongs by statute to "In God we trust," declared in 1956.[^s5]

## Origins

On July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress named Benjamin Franklin, John Adams and Thomas Jefferson to design a seal for the new nation. Their report of August 20, 1776, was ordered to "lie on the table" and never approved.[^s3] The design they recommended was largely the work of their consultant, the portrait artist Pierre Eugène Du Simitière. His manuscript proposal, dated August 20 and now in the Library of Congress, describes a shield quartered with emblems of six European nations from which, he wrote, Americans descended, surrounded by a chain of thirteen small shields lettered with the initials of the states, and topped by the Eye of Providence. Its motto was "E PLURIBUS UNUM."[^s1] Every nation on that list was European; the design gave no place to the African and Native peoples who also lived in the states.[^s1]

The phrase itself came from print culture, not ancient Rome. The editors of the Jefferson Papers judge that its direct source was most likely the title page of London's *Gentleman's Magazine*, familiar to literate colonists.[^s1] In a 1923 study, the classicist Monroe E. Deutsch traced the motto back to Pierre Motteux's *Gentleman's Journal*, where it appeared in January 1692, and argued that Motteux adapted it from a line of Horace. Deutsch rejected the popular derivation from the Latin poem *Moretum* (attributed to Virgil), whose wording differs and which describes the blending of a salad.[^s2]

Two later committees, in 1780 and 1782, failed to produce an acceptable seal, though the second, advised by Francis Hopkinson, contributed the thirteen stripes, thirteen stars and olive branch.[^s3] On June 13, 1782, Congress gave all their material to its secretary, Charles Thomson. He kept the first committee's motto, placed it on the scroll in the eagle's beak, and Congress adopted his design on June 20, 1782. Thomson's official explanation says the motto "alludes to this union" of the states under Congress.[^s3]

## Meaning and practice

As adopted, the "many" were the states and the "one" was the union they formed. Historian Andreas Etges reads the motto as a task — making one common polity out of many states — that he argues is still not fully accomplished.[^s6] Over time the phrase has also been applied to the many peoples who make up the American population, and it has become a touchstone in arguments about how national unity and the country's diversity fit together.[^s6]

The motto reaches Americans mostly through objects. The Great Seal is impressed on treaty instruments, proclamations and certain commissions some 2,000 to 3,000 times a year, and since 1935, when Franklin D. Roosevelt approved a new design, both sides of the seal have appeared on the back of the one-dollar bill.[^s3] Federal law requires that the reverse of each U.S. coin carry the inscriptions "United States of America" and "E Pluribus Unum," alongside "Liberty" on the obverse and "In God We Trust."[^s4]

The 1956 statute naming "In God we trust" the national motto did not remove E pluribus unum from the seal or the coinage; the two now appear side by side. Congress recodified that statute in 1998 and reenacted it unchanged in 2002.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

**Who proposed the motto.** No surviving record says who first suggested the phrase. The State Department's history of the seal credits Du Simitière, in whose proposal it first appears.[^s3] Deutsch, weighing Franklin's long association with the *Gentleman's Magazine*, concluded that "the evidence is overwhelming that the suggestion came from Benjamin Franklin."[^s2] The editors of the Jefferson Papers note that it has been attributed to Jefferson, Franklin and Du Simitière and leave the question open.[^s1]

**What "many" and "one" should mean.** The founding-era meaning — states forming a union — is clear from Thomson's remarks.[^s3] Later uses that apply the motto to a people of many origins are a matter of interpretation. Etges uses the phrase to frame a debate over national history museums in Washington: some scholars, such as Benjamin Filene, welcome museums devoted to particular groups, while Etges asks whether a single museum can still tell one comprehensive national story. Etges himself concludes that the multiple narratives are historically necessary because women and minorities have not been treated as equals, and that the museums complement one another.[^s6]

## Connections

E pluribus unum is **part of** the [[Great Seal of the United States]], whose obverse carries it on the eagle's scroll, and it **symbolizes** the [[United States]] as a union of states.[^s3] It was **introduced by** Pierre Eugène Du Simitière's 1776 proposal for the first seal committee of Franklin, Adams and Jefferson.[^s1] Its wording was **influenced by** the *Gentleman's Magazine* and its predecessor, Motteux's *Gentleman's Journal*.[^s2][^s1] It is often **confused with** [[In God We Trust]], the motto Congress designated in 1956; the belief that the one replaced the other is a separate myth node.[^s5] It belongs with the other [[national symbols of the United States]], including the [[flag of the United States]], whose stars and stripes also count thirteen states and entered the seal through Francis Hopkinson's committee.[^s3]
