# Starving Time of 1609–1610

The Starving Time was the famine that struck the English colony at Jamestown, Virginia, in the winter of 1609–1610. Caught between a severe drought, a siege by the Powhatan paramount chiefdom and the loss of most of their expected supplies, roughly three-quarters of the colonists died of hunger and related disease, and some survivors resorted to eating the dead.[^s1] By the time relief arrived in May 1610 only about sixty people were left alive in the fort, and the survivors had already set out to abandon the colony when a new governor's fleet turned them back.[^s2][^s3]

## Background

Illness killed more than half of Jamestown's first 104 colonists in 1607, and the settlement depended heavily on corn obtained, by trade or by force, from the Powhatan towns of Tsenacomoco.[^s1] Under John Smith, who became president in 1608, the colony got through its second winter, but Smith's forceful demands for corn angered Native communities.[^s2] The National Park Service notes that by late 1609 relations had broken down because the English were demanding too much food during a drought.[^s3]

That drought was exceptional. Baldcypress tree rings from southeastern Virginia show that Jamestown was founded during the driest seven-year episode in about 770 years, from 1606 to 1612, which the researchers link to the colony's high death rate.[^s6] George Percy also blamed brackish, contaminated river water and poor sanitation for many deaths.[^s1]

In 1609 the Virginia Company of London issued a second charter and sent nine ships with new colonists and supplies to last the winter. A hurricane scattered the fleet, and the flagship *Sea Venture*, carrying much of the cargo and the new leaders Thomas Gates, George Somers and Christopher Newport, wrecked on Bermuda. The ships that reached Jamestown in August brought about 300 colonists and little food.[^s2] The newcomers raised the population to nearly 400 and quickly ate a corn crop meant to last the winter.[^s1] In October Smith, badly injured in a gunpowder explosion, sailed for England, and George Percy, elected president of the council in September, was left in charge.[^s1][^s2]

## What happened

In November 1609 Powhatan's warriors ambushed a trading party led by John Ratcliffe, who was tortured to death, and Powhatan ordered a siege of Jamestown. Rather than storm the fort, the Powhatans used hunger as a weapon: they killed the colonists' hogs and anyone who ventured out to hunt, fish, trade or forage.[^s1] Percy later wrote that the Indians killed "as fast without as Famine and Pestilence did within."[^s2] Colonists who fled to Native towns for food were also killed.[^s4]

Inside the palisade, Percy calculated that the rations would last only halfway through the winter. People ate the seven horses brought the previous summer, then dogs, cats, rats, mice and snakes, and finally shoe leather.[^s2] Dysentery and typhoid followed.[^s2] A 1624 report by Virginia's Ancient Planters, early settlers commissioned by the General Assembly, recorded that the starving dug up corpses to eat, and that one man killed his sleeping wife and ate her and was executed for it.[^s4] Governor Gates later doubted that story, suggesting the man had murdered his wife and invented the cannibalism when caught.[^s1]

The siege lifted in early May 1610 when the Powhatans returned to spring planting. Of the roughly 240 people who had stayed in the fort in November, about 60 were still alive.[^s1] Estimates of the toll range from about two-thirds to William Strachey's 80 to 90 percent.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

## Aftermath and legacy

On May 24, 1610, Gates and the *Sea Venture* survivors arrived from Bermuda in two boats they had built there.[^s1][^s3] They found the palisade torn down and the gates off their hinges, and with little food on hand Gates decided to abandon the colony.[^s3] He announced the departure on June 7; the next day the retreating colonists met the fleet of the new governor, Lord De La Warr, who ordered them back to rebuild.[^s1][^s2] The war with the Powhatans continued until 1614, and stricter discipline, new immigration, military successes and, later, tobacco helped the colony survive.[^s1]

In 2012 Jamestown Rediscovery archaeologists recovered a teenage girl's skull and shinbone from a trash deposit dating to that winter.[^s2][^s5] Smithsonian forensic anthropologist Douglas Owsley found chop marks on the skull, apparently made to open it, and knife cuts on the jaw and cheek suggesting flesh was removed; on May 1, 2013, the team announced the first scientifically proven case of survival cannibalism in colonial America. The girl, about fourteen and probably arrived in August 1609, was nicknamed "Jane" because her name is unknown.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

**Why so many died.** Smith, Ralph Hamor and Strachey blamed idleness and bad government, a judgment long repeated. Karen Ordahl Kupperman argued instead that malnutrition, together with scurvy, dysentery, malaria and other diseases, produced apathy and despair that observers mistook for laziness, and many historians now favour her view.[^s1] The tree-ring evidence adds drought as an environmental cause.[^s6]

**The Powhatan side.** English accounts describe the siege as treachery,[^s4] while the National Park Service and Encyclopedia Virginia present it as a deliberate strategy of starvation, adopted after relations collapsed over English demands for food during a drought.[^s1][^s3]

**How to read the cannibalism accounts.** The forensic study of Jane confirms that survival cannibalism happened.[^s5] Historian Rachel B. Herrmann cautions that no document describes this particular girl, that the written accounts' authors had motives of their own, and that leaders later used tales of famine to justify tight control over food and settlers.[^s1]

## Connections

- It **occurred in** Jamestown Colony, the fort and town on the James River, and nearly ended it in 1610.[^s1][^s3]
- It was **part of** the opening phase of the Anglo-Powhatan Wars: the siege was Powhatan's strategy in the First Anglo-Powhatan War (1609–1614).[^s1]
- It **affected** the Colony of Virginia by killing most of its English population and nearly causing its abandonment.[^s3]
- Related: Powhatan, John Smith, George Percy, Thomas Gates and the Virginia Company of London.[^s2]
