# Fourteenth Amendment

The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, passed by Congress in June 1866 and ratified on July 9, 1868, opens by declaring that "All persons born or naturalized in the United States" are citizens of the nation and of the state where they live. It forbids any state to abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens, to deprive any person of life, liberty or property without due process of law, or to deny any person the equal protection of the laws.[^s1] The second of the three Reconstruction amendments, its enforcement power underpins statutes such as the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.[^s2]

## Background

The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery in 1865 but did not define the legal status of the people it freed. Southern legislatures quickly passed Black Codes limiting the rights of African Americans, and white southerners threatened, terrorized and murdered freed people. In April 1866 Congress passed the Civil Rights Act, extending to people born in the United States rights such as owning property and using the courts.[^s4] The Supreme Court's *Dred Scott* decision, however, still stood for the rule that Black Americans could not be citizens, a rule the new amendment's first sentence would overturn.[^s7]

The 39th Congress created the Joint Committee of Fifteen on Reconstruction on December 13, 1865, with nine representatives and six senators under Senator William Pitt Fessenden of Maine. After hearing testimony from generals, Southern politicians and formerly enslaved people, it drafted the amendment.[^s3] Representative John A. Bingham of Ohio was the primary author of Section 1.[^s1] With Fessenden ill, Senator Jacob Howard of Michigan managed the measure, and the Senate passed it on June 8, 1866, by 33 to 11.[^s3] The House approved it on June 13 by 120 to 32, with Radical Republican Thaddeus Stevens supporting it while wanting a stronger version.[^s4]

## Ratification

President Andrew Johnson opposed the amendment, as did Northern Democrats, and white southerners rejected it almost unanimously.[^s7] After Northern voters backed Republicans in the 1866 elections, Congress made ratification a condition for restoring a Southern state's representation; the Reconstruction Act of March 2, 1867, placed the former Confederate states except Tennessee, an early ratifier, under military districts.[^s7][^s3] North Carolina, Louisiana and South Carolina, which had first rejected the amendment, ratified in July 1868, completing the required three-fourths on July 9.[^s6]

Because Ohio and New Jersey had voted to rescind their ratifications, Secretary of State William H. Seward first proclaimed the amendment valid only if those withdrawals were ineffective; Congress declared it adopted on July 21, and Seward certified ratification by 28 states on July 28, 1868.[^s5]

## What it says

Section 1 holds the Citizenship, Privileges or Immunities, Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses. Section 2 replaced the Constitution's three-fifths rule by counting the whole number of persons in each state, excluding Indians not taxed, and reduced a state's representation if it denied the vote to male citizens aged 21 or older.[^s3][^s1] Section 3 barred from office anyone who had sworn to support the Constitution and then engaged in insurrection or rebellion, unless two-thirds of each house of Congress removed the disability. Section 4 voided Confederate debts and any claim for compensation for emancipated people. Section 5 gave Congress power to enforce the article.[^s1]

## Legacy

The Supreme Court soon narrowed the amendment. In the *Slaughter-House Cases* of 1873 it held that the Privileges or Immunities Clause protected only rights owed to the national government, leaving most rights to the states and rendering the clause what the Constitution Annotated calls a practical nullity.[^s8] For decades the Court also held that it did not apply the Bill of Rights to the states, and it failed to protect Black citizens in practice, though petitions, lawsuits and dissents later fed twentieth-century change.[^s1] In cases such as *Gitlow v. New York* (1925), on free speech, the Court applied most Bill of Rights guarantees to the states one by one through the Due Process Clause.[^s9][^s7] *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) marked a turning point against government-sponsored racial discrimination, and courts later extended equal protection to sex discrimination.[^s7]

Native people under tribal authority were excluded, and in *Elk v. Wilkins* (1884) the Court held that Native Americans were not citizens even after leaving their nations; Congress made all Native Americans citizens in the Indian Citizenship Act of June 24, 1924, though some states kept them from voting.[^s11][^s7] In *United States v. Wong Kim Ark* (1898), decided 6 to 2 while exclusion laws barred Chinese laborers, the Court held that a San Francisco-born son of Chinese parents was a citizen.[^s10]

Section 2's use of the word "male" seemed to reserve voting to men, and disputes over the Reconstruction amendments, above all the Fifteenth, split the American Equal Rights Association in 1869; Frances Ellen Watkins Harper and Sojourner Truth pressed both sides to remember Black women.[^s12] When Virginia Minor claimed the vote as a citizen, the Court held in *Minor v. Happersett* (March 29, 1875) that the Constitution "does not confer the right of suffrage upon any one."[^s11][^s13]

## Debates and interpretations

Senator Howard told the Senate that the Privileges or Immunities Clause would extend the personal rights in the first eight amendments to the states, but historians disagree about how widely that view was shared in Congress and the ratifying states.[^s1] Historian Michael Les Benedict argues that the framers expected Congress, under Section 5, to protect rights at least as actively as the courts, a role later constrained by the state-action doctrine.[^s7]

## Connections

The amendment **amends** the United States Constitution and **protects** due process and the equal protection of the laws.[^s1] It was **enacted by** the United States Congress as House Joint Resolution 127.[^s4] Section 1 was **authored by** John A. Bingham.[^s1] Its citizenship clause **overturns** the citizenship holding of *Dred Scott v. Sandford*.[^s7] The Joint Committee on Reconstruction drafted it, and the Court interpreted it in the *Slaughter-House Cases*, *Minor v. Happersett*, *Elk v. Wilkins*, *Wong Kim Ark* and *Brown*.[^s3][^s7][^s8][^s10][^s11]
