# Jazz

Jazz is a style of music that took shape in New Orleans between about 1895 and 1917, created above all by African American and Creole musicians who mixed the blues, ragtime, brass-band marches and dance music with improvisation.[^s1] From there it spread through recordings, touring and migration to Chicago, New York and the rest of the country, and it changed again and again — into swing, bebop and many later forms.[^s1][^s4] The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture describes it as an original American art form, closely tied to African American history and culture.[^s5]

## Origins

New Orleans was founded in 1718 as part of French Louisiana, passed to Spain in 1763, returned briefly to France and became American through the Louisiana Purchase of 1803.[^s1] Slavery shaped the city from the start: by 1721 enslaved West Africans were about 30 percent of its population, and by the end of the 1700s people of African descent, enslaved and free, made up more than half.[^s1] By the mid-eighteenth century enslaved people gathered on Sundays at a market outside the city walls that became known as Congo Square, famous for African dance and music; the city sanctioned these weekly ring shouts from 1817 to 1856.[^s1][^s2] Those dances ended before the Civil War, but related musical traditions lived on in Black neighborhoods, including the Mardi Gras Indian processions documented by the 1880s, whose drumming and call-and-response chants influenced Louis Armstrong and Jelly Roll Morton.[^s1]

In the 1880s and 1890s brass bands such as the Excelsior and the Onward played for parades and funerals organized by mutual-aid and benevolent societies; neighbors who danced along behind them became known as the "second line."[^s1] As ragtime became a national craze in the 1890s, bands added ragtime pieces to their marches, and social aid and pleasure clubs gave young players steady work and training.[^s1] Between 1895 and 1900 the uptown cornetist Charles "Buddy" Bolden began improvising on the blues and speeding up familiar dance tunes; many early jazz players credited him as the first to play the new style, though contemporaries also praised rival bands as even "hotter."[^s1][^s2]

Segregation helped forge the music. Creoles of color — people of mixed African and European descent, many of them trained musicians in the city's best orchestras — lost their separate social standing under the segregation laws of the 1890s, which pushed them together with uptown Black musicians and combined Creole discipline with uptown improvisation.[^s1] Bands settled on a front line of cornet, clarinet and trombone weaving separate melodies together, the polyphonic sound of New Orleans jazz.[^s1] White ragtime bands such as "Papa" Jack Laine's Reliance bands also played in the city from about 1890 to 1913.[^s1]

## Meaning and practice

Jazz rests on improvisation within a shared framework: players vary a melody, trade phrases and build solos over a tune's chord structure, and early New Orleans bands did this collectively, with every horn improvising at once.[^s1] Musicians were not untrained, as an old assumption held; many read music fluently while others played by ear.[^s2] The music was first functional — for parades, funerals, dances and social rituals — and for much of its history it was dance music.[^s2][^s4]

Musicians carried the style out of New Orleans early. Jelly Roll Morton began touring in 1907, Freddie Keppard's Original Creole Orchestra moved to Los Angeles in 1912, and Tom Brown's band and the Original Dixieland Jazz Band went to Chicago in 1915 and 1916.[^s1] In February 1917 the Original Dixieland Jazz Band made the first jazz recordings in New York; the first recording by a Black New Orleans jazz band, Kid Ory's, came only in 1922.[^s2] Joe "King" Oliver, Sidney Bechet and Louis Armstrong moved to Chicago, and Armstrong's Hot Five and Hot Seven records of 1925–1928 shifted jazz from group playing toward the virtuoso soloist.[^s1] Recordings made in Chicago between 1923 and 1928 led to jazz being widely recognized as an American art form.[^s2]

In the swing era of the 1930s and 1940s, big bands led by Duke Ellington, Count Basie, Benny Goodman, Chick Webb and Artie Shaw made jazz the country's popular dance music.[^s4] Ellington's band played Harlem's Cotton Club from 1927; the club admitted only white patrons, and Black performers had to use back entrances.[^s5] Segregation followed jazz everywhere: the musicians' union stayed racially divided and Black musicians met Jim Crow treatment on tour.[^s4] In the early 1940s, after-hours sessions in Harlem clubs such as Minton's Playhouse produced bebop, led by Dizzy Gillespie, Charlie Parker and Thelonious Monk — small-group music with fast tempos and complex solos, meant for listening rather than dancing.[^s4] In September 1947 Gillespie's band played Afro-Cuban jazz pieces such as "Manteca" with the Cuban percussionist Chano Pozo at Carnegie Hall.[^s4]

During the Cold War the federal government used jazz abroad. In March 1956 the State Department sent Gillespie on the first jazz-ambassador tour, to southern Europe, the Middle East and South Asia, and over the next two decades Armstrong, Dave Brubeck, Ellington, Goodman and Sarah Vaughan also toured for the department; the department presented the music as an expression of American values.[^s6]

## Connections

Jazz **originated in** New Orleans[^s1] and among African Americans, whose history and culture the music carries.[^s5] It was **influenced by** the blues and ragtime[^s1] and by the African-rooted music and dance of Congo Square, which grew out of the city's history of slavery.[^s1][^s2] The 1890s segregation laws that reshaped Creole and Black musical life were part of the Jim Crow order in Louisiana, the same setting as the Plessy v. Ferguson case.[^s1] Storyville, the red-light district closed in November 1917, is often wrongly treated as jazz's birthplace.[^s3] Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington carried it to national audiences,[^s1][^s5] and the State Department's jazz ambassadors made it part of U.S. diplomacy in the Cold War.[^s6]

The Storyville legend is the most persistent myth about jazz. Bruce Raeburn, curator of Tulane University's Hogan Jazz Archive, called the idea that jazz was born there and fled when it closed "a romantic idea," and his research found that musicians left New Orleans for pay, working conditions, racial equality and safety — King Oliver, for instance, had been repeatedly arrested for disturbing the peace while playing.[^s3] In his account, Storyville was only one of many settings where music was required.[^s2]
