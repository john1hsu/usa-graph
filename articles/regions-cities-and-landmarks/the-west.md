# The West

"The West" names both a part of the United States and an idea that has moved across the map. For statistical purposes the Census Bureau defines a West region of 13 states in two divisions: the Mountain division (Arizona, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming) and the Pacific division (Alaska, California, Hawaii, Oregon and Washington).[^s1] In history and popular culture, though, "the West" has also meant whatever lay beyond the line of American settlement, and arguments over how to tell its story have shaped how Americans understand their country.[^s4][^s5]

## Geography

Much of the region is dry. In his 1878 *Report on the Lands of the Arid Region*, the government surveyor John Wesley Powell used Smithsonian data showing that beyond the 100th meridian average rainfall falls below twenty inches a year, too little for most crops; he warned that the 160-acre homestead suited to the East would fail there and urged irrigation-based farming and ranching instead.[^s8] The region also contains most of the nation's public land: as of 2024 the federal government owned about 45.9 percent of the 11 contiguous western states and 60.8 percent of Alaska, compared with roughly 28 percent of all U.S. land (CRS, 2026). The Congressional Research Service attributes the contrast partly to early treaties, land settlement laws and settlement patterns, and to the reservation of parks and other reserves in the West beginning with Yellowstone in 1872.[^s7]

## History

Native nations controlled most of the continent west of the Mississippi deep into the nineteenth century.[^s4] Much of the Southwest and California was Mexican territory until the 1840s. After the U.S.–Mexican War, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed February 2, 1848, transferred 55 percent of Mexico's territory, including present-day California, Nevada, Utah, New Mexico, most of Arizona and Colorado, for $15 million. Article VIII promised that Mexicans' property would be "inviolably respected," but the Senate struck Article X, which protected Mexican land grants, before ratifying.[^s3]

Federal policy then pushed land into private hands. The Homestead Act of 1862 offered 160 acres to settlers who lived on and improved a claim for five years, though married women were excluded, and railroads received more than 175 million acres of public land between 1850 and 1871; the Central Pacific and Union Pacific met in Utah in 1869, built in part by Chinese workers.[^s4] Roughly 1.29 billion acres of public domain left federal ownership between 1781 and 2024, 97 percent of private transfers before 1940.[^s7]

Settlement came through conquest. California's Native population fell from about 150,000 before the gold rush to under 20,000 by 1880; the Army forced Navajo people on the Long Walk to Bosque Redondo; Colorado militia massacred about 200 Cheyenne at Sand Creek in 1864; and at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890, U.S. troops killed between 150 and 300 Lakota men, women and children. Bison, the basis of Plains life, fell from over ten million to a few hundred by the early 1880s, and the Dawes Act of 1887 broke reservations into allotments and sold the "surplus" to settlers.[^s4] The West was also diverse: perhaps a fourth of cowboys were African American, and Mexican *vaqueros* gave ranching words such as *rodeo* and *lasso*.[^s4]

## Today

The 2020 census counted 78,588,572 people in the West region, up 9.2 percent from 2010 and up from about 7.1 million in 1910 (Census Bureau, 2020).[^s2] That is about 24 percent of the nation's 331.4 million residents, a share computed from the same Census table.[^s2] Since the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976, remaining public-domain land has generally been kept in federal ownership, and homestead entries ended entirely in 1986.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

In 1893, at the American Historical Association's meeting in Chicago, Frederick Jackson Turner cited an 1890 census bulletin saying there "can hardly be said to be a frontier line" any longer. He argued that an area of free land and the westward advance of settlement explained American development, fostering individualism and democracy.[^s5] *The American Yawp* faults the thesis for Anglo-Saxon chauvinism and for ignoring Chinese and Mexican immigrants and the roles of federal subsidies, technology and corporations.[^s4] In *The Legacy of Conquest* (1987), Patricia Nelson Limerick rejected the "closing frontier" model altogether, treating the West as a place shaped by an ongoing conquest of land, resources and peoples whose consequences continue today, and she wrote women, Native Americans, Spanish and Mexican settlers, and Chinese, Japanese, Black and Mormon westerners into the story. One reviewer in a Native American studies journal judged her framework more useful than Turner's for understanding Native issues past and present, including tribal sovereignty.[^s6]

## Connections

The West **lies in** the United States as one of the four Census regions.[^s1] It was **shaped by** the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which brought the Mexican Cession under U.S. rule,[^s3] by the Homestead Act's land transfers,[^s7] and by the Dawes Act and the transcontinental railroad, which opened Native land and the interior to settlers.[^s4] The idea of the West as a place **contrasts with** the idea of the American frontier as a moving line, the distinction at the heart of Limerick's critique of Turner.[^s6] Yellowstone, established in 1872, began the era of reserving western land as parks.[^s7]
