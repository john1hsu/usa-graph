# Morrill Land-Grant College Act

The Morrill Land-Grant College Act, signed by President Abraham Lincoln on July 2, 1862, offered every loyal state federal land to endow at least one college teaching agriculture and the mechanic arts — engineering and applied science — alongside other studies.[^s1][^s2] It is the founding law of the American land-grant university system, which by the Congressional Research Service's count included 111 institutions in fall 2020, among them Cornell, Ohio State, Texas A&M and the University of California.[^s2] It also turned roughly 11 million acres of the public domain, most of it recently taken from Native nations, into college endowments.[^s2][^s6]

## Background

The idea of public colleges for farmers and mechanics was, as one historian of the movement put it, "in the air" by mid-century, and no single originator can be named.[^s7] In Illinois, the college professor Jonathan Baldwin Turner proposed publicly supported colleges for farmers and mechanics at a convention in Putnam County in November 1851, later added federal land grants as the way to pay for them, and lobbied Stephen Douglas and Abraham Lincoln.[^s7][^s5] The model also drew on an older federal habit of using public land for schooling, which the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 had written into national policy.[^s1]

Representative Justin Smith Morrill of Vermont introduced a national bill on December 14, 1857.[^s7] Congress passed it, but President James Buchanan vetoed it on February 24, 1859, arguing that it would drain land-sale revenue the Treasury needed and that the Constitution did not let Congress fund education in the states.[^s4] After Southern states seceded, Morrill reintroduced the bill in 1861, now stressing military instruction for a nation at war, and it became law the next year.[^s7][^s5]

## What it says

Each state received 30,000 acres for each of its senators and representatives under the 1860 census, and mineral lands were excluded.[^s1] States without enough federal land inside their borders received land scrip — certificates they could sell to buyers who then claimed unappropriated federal land elsewhere, up to one million acres per state.[^s1][^s2] To qualify, a state had to accept the grant by law and open at least one college within five years; the proceeds formed a perpetual endowment that could not be spent on buildings, and states in rebellion were barred from taking part.[^s1] The colleges were to teach agriculture and the mechanic arts without excluding classical studies, and to "promote the liberal and practical education of the industrial classes."[^s1][^s5]

## Legacy

States used the grants in different ways. New York sold its 990,000 acres of scrip to fund Cornell University, while Idaho kept land inside its borders and sold part of it for the University of Idaho.[^s2] North Carolina first gave its proceeds to the University of North Carolina; after pressure from the Farmers' Alliance it founded a separate agricultural and mechanical college in 1887, later NC State University.[^s5]

The land itself came from Indigenous peoples. A 2020 *High Country News* investigation by Robert Lee and Tristan Ahtone traced about 10.7 million acres to cessions from nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities, obtained through more than 160 land cessions the authors describe as backed by violence, some through treaties the Senate never ratified.[^s6] The United States paid less than $400,000 to extinguish Native title to those parcels, and nothing at all for more than a quarter of them, while the grants raised $17.7 million in endowments for 52 institutions by the early 20th century.[^s6] Dakota land ceded in Minnesota in 1851, for example, helped endow 35 universities, and the authors found more than 500,000 acres still held in trust for universities in fiscal year 2019.[^s6] The National Archives likewise notes that more than 10 million acres of the grant came from tribal lands.[^s1]

Congress built on the act. The Hatch Act of 1887 funded agricultural experiment stations at land-grant colleges, and the Smith-Lever Act of 1914 created the Cooperative Extension System that brings their research to farms and communities.[^s2] The Morrill Act of 1890 added annual appropriations and barred racial discrimination in admissions, but allowed states to comply by funding separate institutions "of like character," which produced the 19 historically Black 1890 Institutions in 18 states.[^s3][^s1] Unequal funding persisted: in 2020, 1862 Institutions received 82% of federal capacity funding for agricultural research and extension and 1890 Institutions 18%.[^s3] In 1994 Congress made tribal colleges and universities land-grant institutions, funded by appropriations and an endowment rather than land; 35 were active as of the CRS count.[^s2]

## Debates and interpretations

Accounts of the act's origins differ in emphasis. NCpedia credits Turner with the idea and Morrill with bringing it to Congress, while David Wrone stresses a broad popular movement and argues that no single person can be shown to have first expressed it.[^s5][^s7] The law itself framed the colleges as serving the industrial classes, and that purpose has long anchored accounts of the system as a widening of access to higher education.[^s1] The "land-grab universities" research reframes the story around the land: its authors argue that the system's wealth rests on the dispossession of Native nations, a point the Congressional Research Service notes in its overview of the system.[^s6][^s2]
