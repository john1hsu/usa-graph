  myth: The United States only began to industrialize after the Civil War, in the Gilded Age.
  fact: Industrialization began much earlier; by the 1830s the Northeast had a thriving industrial and commercial sector, and economic historians date an American industrial revolution to roughly 1790–1870, built on textile mills, steam engines and precision manufacturing.
  why: The dramatic railroads, steel and corporations of the late 1800s overshadow the earlier mills and workshops, which hides how much the antebellum North had already changed, and how closely its factories were tied to slave-grown cotton.

# Economic history of the United States

The economic history of the United States is the story of how its people made a living, traded, borrowed and shared — or were denied — the wealth they produced. It runs from a colonial economy of staple crops, Atlantic trade and enslaved labor, through early industrialization and the cotton boom, to recurring financial panics, the creation of federal economic institutions, the Great Depression, postwar affluence and a modern economy in which farming employs only a sliver of workers.[^s1][^s9] This article is a map of that story; its periods and turning points have their own entries.

## Origins

Before independence, the colonies were part of a British Atlantic economy. Indigenous nations traded furs, deer hides, food and other goods for European manufactures, and colonial expansion into their lands led Britain in 1763 to draw a boundary line along the Appalachians.[^s1] In the Chesapeake, tobacco dominated; in South Carolina, rice planters relied on the skills of enslaved West Africans, and by 1750 the colony had an enslaved majority. Enslaved people also worked in northern ports, and New England merchants, though they held few enslaved workers, were deeply involved in the slave trade and supplied Caribbean plantations. Parliament's Navigation Acts tried to channel colonial trade to Britain, though smuggling was widespread before 1763.[^s1]

After independence, Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton proposed a national bank modelled on the Bank of England. President Washington signed the charter in February 1791, and the First Bank of the United States opened in Philadelphia that December as the government's fiscal agent and a commercial bank. Jefferson opposed it as a monopoly the Constitution did not authorize, and Congress let its twenty-year charter lapse in 1811.[^s2]

## Growth and transformation

By the 1830s the Northeast had a thriving industrial and commercial sector: farmers sold to distant markets, factory work divided labor into steps, wage work spread and new class divisions hardened.[^s3] Economist Joshua Rosenbloom argues that Americans adapted British technologies rather than copying them, substituting machines and organization for scarce skilled labor in textile mills, high-pressure steam engines and precision manufacturing.[^s4]

The South's growth rested on slavery. After Eli Whitney's cotton gin of 1793, enslaved people were forced to clear and cultivate new lands; by 1860 the South produced about two-thirds of the world's cotton, and nearly four million people were held in bondage. Most of the crop went to British mills, northern mills depended on it, and planters borrowed from northern banks.[^s5]

Farming's share of work fell steeply over the next century. In 1900 about 41 percent of the workforce was employed in agriculture; by 2000 the figure was about 1.9 percent, as farms became fewer and larger (USDA Economic Research Service, 2005).[^s9] Financial instability recurred: after the Panic of 1907, when the country, alone among major financial powers without a central bank, depended on J. P. Morgan's private rescue, Congress studied reform, and President Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act on December 23, 1913.[^s8] The Great Depression was far worse; when Franklin D. Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, unemployment had reached about 25 percent and banks were failing at an alarming rate.[^s10]

After World War II, federal programs underwrote mass prosperity. The 1944 G.I. Bill, federally insured mortgages and the interstate highways helped raise homeownership from 44 percent in 1940 to almost 62 percent in 1960, but redlining and whites-only developments such as Levittown shut many Black families out of that wealth.[^s11] Between 1965 and 1982 the "Great Inflation" brought the end of the dollar's link to gold in 1971, two oil shocks and inflation near 15 percent in 1980, before the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker tightened money at the cost of a severe 1981–82 recession.[^s12]

## Significance

Economic change shaped nearly every other part of American history: where people lived, who could own land and homes, how regions related to one another and what the federal government was asked to do. It also shows how wealth was built through the coerced labor of enslaved people and the taking of Native land, and how later federal programs widened prosperity for some Americans while excluding others.[^s1][^s5][^s11] Recurring crises — 1907, 1929–33, the 1970s — repeatedly drove the creation or reform of national institutions.[^s8][^s10][^s12]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians and economists disagree about how central slavery was to American economic growth. Scholars associated with the "new history of capitalism," in the volume edited by Sven Beckert and Seth Rockman, argue that slavery was central to the emergence of American capitalism between the Revolution and the Civil War and must be placed at the heart of the nation's economic development.[^s7] Economic historian Gavin Wright accepts that many northern businesses were complicit in slavery but argues that the slave South discouraged immigration, underinvested in transportation and left most people uneducated, so national prosperity did not rest on slavery.[^s6] Both sides agree that slave-grown cotton tied the North, the South and Britain together.[^s5]
