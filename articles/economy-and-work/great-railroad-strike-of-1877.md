# Great Railroad Strike of 1877

The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 was, in the Miller Center's judgment, the most widespread general strike in American history. Over a few weeks in July, a walkout against a wage cut on the Baltimore and Ohio (B&O) Railroad spread along the trunk lines linking the Northeast seaboard and the Midwest, halting rail traffic and drawing in unemployed people and workers from other trades.[^s1][^s2] State militias and, for the first time in a labor dispute, federal troops were sent into the states to restore order, and nearly a hundred people died in what became known as the Great Upheaval.[^s1][^s2]

## Background

After the railroads' financial bubble burst in 1873, the country sank into years of depression. Railroad companies cut wages again and again, even while, workers complained, they enjoyed large government subsidies and paid shareholders generous dividends.[^s2] In 1877 the B&O announced another ten percent cut for all employees, on top of earlier reductions.[^s4] Almost none of the men who would strike belonged to a union.[^s1] The U.S. Army was small, about 25,000 men, and mostly stationed in the West.[^s1]

## What happened

On July 16, 1877, railroad workers at Martinsburg, West Virginia, walked off the job and stopped trains.[^s5] According to the *e-WV* encyclopedia, a railroad firemen's organization called the strike there.[^s4] Governor Henry M. Mathews sent the local militia company to move a train, but its volunteers were largely railroad workers or their relatives; after a striker fired on them and was mortally wounded by return fire, the company withdrew and refused to act again, and a second militia unit from Wheeling fared no better.[^s4] Mathews then asked President Rutherford B. Hayes for federal troops.[^s4]

On July 18 Hayes issued a proclamation citing the Constitution's guarantee of protection against domestic violence and ordering everyone obstructing the railroad to "disperse and retire peaceably to their respective abodes" by noon the next day.[^s3] Federal soldiers reached Martinsburg on July 19 and ended the violence there.[^s4] By then the strike was spreading along the lines. In Baltimore, Maryland militia fired on a crowd, killing ten or eleven people, and a crowd estimated at 15,000 burned passenger cars and part of the B&O depot.[^s1][^s2]

The worst violence came in Pittsburgh, where anger at a Pennsylvania Railroad wage cut ran high and local sympathy lay with the strikers, so state officials sent militia from Philadelphia.[^s6] On July 21, near the 28th Street crossing, shots rang out — no one knows who fired first — and the troops fired into the crowd, killing about twenty people.[^s6] The militiamen retreated into a railroad roundhouse, which the crowd set on fire; five guardsmen were killed as they fled.[^s1] Over two days, fires destroyed more than thirty buildings, including the Union Depot, and the crowd wrecked 104 locomotives and 2,152 railroad cars.[^s1][^s6] Allegheny County eventually paid about $2.6 million in damages.[^s6]

Strikes and clashes followed in Reading, Pennsylvania, where militia killed ten people, and in St. Louis, where federal troops and vigilantes broke a citywide general strike.[^s2] In Chicago, unrest peaked from July 24 to 28 as railroad workers walked out and meatpacking, lumber and other wage earners — many of them immigrants living along South Halsted Street — struck in sympathy, bringing the city close to a general strike; special police, the Illinois National Guard and six companies of U.S. Army infantry restored order, and at least 18 people died.[^s5] Most of the strike was over by the end of July, and it had been crushed within about six weeks of its start.[^s1][^s2]

## Aftermath and legacy

Nationwide, nearly 100 people died and workers destroyed nearly $40 million worth of property, according to *The American Yawp*.[^s2] On the federal side, Hayes rejected the Pennsylvania Railroad president Thomas Scott's request that he act directly for the companies, sent soldiers only when governors formally said they could no longer keep order, and refused to use the Army to run trains.[^s1] Even so, his deployment set a precedent: for the next two decades Gilded Age presidents used federal soldiers to back business during mass strikes.[^s1] In the same year Hayes withdrew the last federal troops protecting Reconstruction governments in Louisiana and South Carolina.[^s1]

The railroads won, but the Miller Center calls the victory "Pyrrhic": the public largely blamed the companies, and by early 1880 they had restored the cut wages.[^s1] Many workers concluded that they needed lasting, organized unions, while employers concluded that they needed greater political influence and government aid.[^s2] In West Virginia the strike exposed how unprepared the state was, prompting mostly unheeded calls for a professional national guard to handle labor disturbances.[^s4] In Chicago, fear of uncontrollable class conflict lingered for years.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what happened but differ on what it meant. *The American Yawp* treats the strike as the opening of a new era, describing protests that came close to class war and foreshadowed about fifty years of labor conflict.[^s2] The Miller Center's account stresses that the strike was spontaneous and leaderless, describes Hayes's aim as preserving order rather than breaking the strike, and calls his role minor — while still noting the precedent it set.[^s1] Local histories emphasize different things: the divided loyalties of militiamen who were railroad workers' own neighbours in West Virginia, the immigrant workers and socialists of Chicago, and the photographs that framed Pittsburgh's ruins for the public.[^s4][^s5][^s6]

## Connections

The strike grew out of the depression that followed the Panic of 1873, and it took place during the Gilded Age.[^s2] It occurred in West Virginia, where it began at Martinsburg; in Baltimore; in Pittsburgh, the scene of the worst violence; and in Chicago.[^s4][^s1][^s6][^s5] President Rutherford B. Hayes's proclamations and troop deployments tie it to the federal government and to the end of Reconstruction the same year.[^s3][^s1] Its main consequence was to push workers toward the organized labor movement and employers toward greater political influence.[^s2]
