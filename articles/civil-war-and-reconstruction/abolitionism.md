# Abolitionism

Abolitionism was the movement to end slavery in the United States. It began in revolutionary-era antislavery societies and gradual-emancipation laws in the North, became a radical, interracial crusade for immediate emancipation in the 1830s, and ended in triumph with the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865.[^s2][^s8] For most of its history abolitionists were, in the words of *The American Yawp*, "a small, marginalized group detested by most white Americans."[^s2]

## Origins

American Quakers began to question slavery as early as the late seventeenth century.[^s2] Before independence, Quakers, most Black Christians and other religious groups argued that slavery conflicted with Christ's teaching.[^s3] In 1775 Philadelphians founded the Society for the Relief of Free Negroes Unlawfully Held in Bondage; sixteen of its twenty-four original members were Quakers. Revived in 1784, given a new constitution in 1787 and led by Benjamin Franklin, the Pennsylvania Abolition Society found lawyers for kidnapped free Black people, opened a school for Black boys in 1794 and petitioned legislatures.[^s4]

Early antislavery aimed at gradual change. Pennsylvania's Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery, signed on March 1, 1780, was the first of several state laws passed between 1780 and 1804, but it freed no one immediately: children born afterward served their mothers' owners until age twenty-eight.[^s5] In February 1790 Franklin's society petitioned the First Congress against slavery; a House committee replied that the Constitution barred Congress from banning the slave trade before 1808 or interfering with emancipation, and the House tabled the petitions.[^s6] Delegates from abolition societies in six states met in Philadelphia on January 1, 1794, and agreed to keep petitioning.[^s3]

Many white opponents of slavery also backed colonization. The American Colonization Society, founded in 1816, sought to move free Black Americans to Africa and helped create Liberia.[^s1] Free Black Americans resisted it. Samuel E. Cornish and John B. Russwurm founded *Freedom's Journal* in New York in 1827, which the Library of Congress describes as the first African American newspaper in the United States.[^s9] David Walker's 1829 *Appeal to the Colored Citizens of the World* denounced colonization and called for resistance, by force if necessary,[^s1] and an 1831 convention of free Black people in New York declared, "This is our home, and this is our country."[^s3]

## Goals and methods

In the 1830s the movement radicalized. Anticolonization arguments by free Black activists, together with the evangelical fervor of the Second Great Awakening, which cast slavery as a supreme sin, produced immediatism: the demand for immediate emancipation and Black citizenship.[^s2] Garrison, who had supported colonization in the 1820s, was persuaded by Walker and James Forten that it was inherently racist, and founded *The Liberator* in 1831.[^s2] In 1833 reformers from ten states created the American Anti-Slavery Society, which rejected violence and relied on moral suasion; it claimed 250,000 members by 1838.[^s1]

Abolitionists printed newspapers, pamphlets and children's tracts, lectured, mailed antislavery literature into the South in 1835 and flooded Congress with petitions.[^s2][^s3] Women were central: Lydia Maria Child's 1833 *Appeal* won converts, and Sarah and Angelina Grimké, daughters of a South Carolina slaveholding family, lectured to mixed audiences from 1837.[^s3][^s1] Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in 1838, published his *Narrative* in 1845 and later founded the *North Star*.[^s1]

White Southerners blamed abolitionists for Nat Turner's 1831 rebellion and silenced antislavery dissent, and in 1836 the House adopted a gag rule against abolitionist petitions.[^s2] On October 21, 1835, a Boston mob broke into a meeting of the Boston Female Anti-Slavery Society and threatened Garrison's life.[^s3] A mob killed the editor Elijah Lovejoy in Illinois in 1837, and in 1838 about 10,000 people burned Pennsylvania Hall in Philadelphia.[^s1]

Disagreement split the movement. Garrisonians held that the Constitution was proslavery; political abolitionists argued it could be used against slavery and formed the Liberty Party, which ran James G. Birney for president in 1840.[^s2][^s1] That year Abby Kelley's election to the society's business committee led Arthur Tappan to take conservatives out to form the American and Foreign Anti-Slavery Society.[^s2] In the 1840s and 1850s the emphasis shifted toward resistance: aiding freedom seekers, building transatlantic networks and defying the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850.[^s2]

## Outcomes

Abolitionism transformed American politics. Its supporters fed the Free-Soil movement and the Republican Party, and the *Yawp* judges that Lincoln's election in 1860 is hard to imagine without it.[^s1][^s2] It also launched the women's rights movement: after the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London refused to seat women, Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton organized the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848.[^s2]

During the Civil War the Emancipation Proclamation freed people only in areas in rebellion.[^s8] Stanton and Susan B. Anthony's Women's Loyal National League gathered nearly half a million signatures for full emancipation, and Senator Charles Sumner, who presented the first 100,000 on February 9, 1864, credited it as the principal force behind the Thirteenth Amendment.[^s7][^s6] The Senate passed the amendment in April 1864, the House on January 31, 1865, by 119 to 56, and it was ratified on December 6, 1865.[^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians' judgments of the abolitionists have changed sharply. An older view, described by Robert H. Abzug, cast them as "meddlesome fanatics" who helped bring on a needless war; by the 1960s, when Martin Duberman's *The Antislavery Vanguard* appeared, few scholars defended that stereotype.[^s11] Many accounts, including the *Yawp*'s, stress the evangelical revivalism of the Second Great Awakening as the movement's engine.[^s2] Manisha Sinha instead treats abolition as an interracial movement stretching back to the Revolution and argues that "slave resistance rather than bourgeois liberalism lay at the heart" of it.[^s10] Historians also weigh how far abolitionists' divisiveness deepened the sectional crisis.[^s2]

## Connections

Abolitionism arose in response to slavery in the United States and drew on the Second Great Awakening's revivalism.[^s2] It led to the Liberty Party, helped pave the way for the Republican Party, and, through Mott and Stanton, to the women's suffrage movement.[^s2] Its petition campaigns, above all the Women's Loyal National League's, helped produce the Thirteenth Amendment.[^s7][^s8] Frederick Douglass was a leading voice, and Pennsylvania's 1780 act marks its gradualist phase.[^s1][^s5]
