# Nineteenth Amendment

The Nineteenth Amendment, ratified in August 1920, bars the United States and every state from denying or abridging a citizen's right to vote "on account of sex," and its second sentence gives Congress power to enforce that rule by appropriate legislation.[^s1] It is often described as the amendment that gave women the vote, but its text grants no one a ballot; it forbids sex as a reason for withholding one, so women in several western states were already voting, and many women of color stayed disfranchised long after 1920.[^s2][^s10]

## Background

The Constitution left voting qualifications to the states.[^s2] Kentucky let women vote in school elections from 1838, and Wyoming Territory gave women equal suffrage in 1869, keeping it when it became a state.[^s2] Suffragists also argued that the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause protected women's voting, but the Supreme Court rejected that claim in Minor v. Happersett (1874), closing the courtroom route.[^s2] By 1914 only about ten states had followed Wyoming, and suffragists had largely despaired of winning state by state.[^s2]

Senator Aaron Sargent of California introduced the federal amendment on January 10, 1878; the Senate first defeated it in January 1887, 16 to 34.[^s4] On January 10, 1918, the House passed it with barely the required two-thirds.[^s5] In September 1918 President Woodrow Wilson told the Senate the amendment was needed for the war effort and for democracy at home.[^s5] The Senate still rejected it on October 1, 1918, by two votes, and on February 10, 1919, by one.[^s4] In the new Congress the House passed it on May 21, 1919, and the Senate approved it 56 to 25 on June 4.[^s1][^s4]

## What it says

The amendment has two short sentences. The first protects citizens against denial or abridgment of the vote, by the federal government or any state, on account of sex; the second is an enforcement clause.[^s1] Its wording follows the Fifteenth Amendment of 1870, which bars racial discrimination in voting, and the Supreme Court later relied on that match in upholding it.[^s7]

Ratification took fourteen months. Wisconsin, Illinois and Michigan ratified on June 10, 1919, with Wisconsin counted first.[^s8] Alabama, Georgia, Maryland, Virginia, South Carolina, Mississippi, Louisiana and Delaware voted it down.[^s8] The decision fell to Tennessee in August 1920. Its senate approved, 25 to 4, but in the house a motion to table ratification deadlocked at 48 to 48. Harry Burn, at 24 the chamber's youngest member and an earlier opponent, had read a letter from his mother, Febb Burn, urging him: "Hurrah! and vote for suffrage and don't keep them in doubt." He voted yes, and ratification passed 49 to 47 on August 18.[^s9] Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified the amendment on August 26, 1920.[^s1] States that had rejected it ratified symbolically over the following decades, Mississippi last, in 1984.[^s8]

## Legacy

Historian Nancy Hewitt notes that the amendment added more voters to the electorate than any other measure in American history and inspired millions of women to vote.[^s11] Its reach was uneven. Because state courts held there was too little time to impose poll taxes and literacy tests before the November 1920 election, Black women registered and voted that fall in Richmond, Norfolk, Houston, Memphis and other southern cities; afterward they met the same disfranchising laws and violence used against Black men.[^s11][^s10] Many Mexican American women in the West were also turned away, while most American Indian and Chinese immigrant women, and women in Puerto Rico and the Philippines, were excluded by citizenship rules.[^s11] Black women nonetheless expanded their electoral work, and Oscar De Priest credited them with his 1928 election to Congress.[^s10] Poll taxes and literacy tests kept many minority women from voting until the Twenty-fourth Amendment (1964) and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which provides enforcement under the Fifteenth and Nineteenth Amendments.[^s5][^s4]

In Leser v. Garnett (February 27, 1922), Maryland voters argued that so large an addition to a state's electorate exceeded the amending power and that Tennessee's and West Virginia's ratifications were irregular. Justice Louis Brandeis, writing for the Court, answered that the amendment matched the Fifteenth in form and adoption: "One cannot be valid and the other invalid."[^s7] He held that ratification is a federal function that state constitutions cannot limit and that the Secretary of State's certification bound the courts.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

The Supreme Court has decided few cases under the amendment and has mostly treated it as a voting rule.[^s3] In Breedlove v. Suttles (1937) it upheld a Georgia poll tax charged to men aged 21 to 60 but not to women who did not register, finding no sex-based denial of the vote while acknowledging the amendment protects men too; Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections (1966) later struck down state poll taxes under the Equal Protection Clause without revisiting that holding.[^s6] Whether a claim requires proof of discriminatory intent remains open.[^s6]

Some scholars read it more broadly. In Adkins v. Children's Hospital (1923) the Court cited women's changed political status, culminating in the amendment, to strike down a minimum wage for women, and in Gray v. Sanders (1963) it pointed to the amendment as a mark of political equality.[^s3] Legal scholar Reva Siegel argues that courts have narrowed the amendment to a ban on sex discrimination at the polls, and that it should be read with the Reconstruction amendments as rejecting a husband's claim to represent his wife, informing sex-equality law generally.[^s12] Hewitt, for her part, stresses that its promise of full citizenship was realized only after decades of further struggle.[^s11]

## Connections

The amendment amends the United States Constitution and was enacted by the United States Congress, which proposed it in June 1919 after decades of Senate defeats.[^s1][^s4] It protects the right to vote against sex discrimination.[^s3] It was introduced by Senator Aaron Sargent in 1878.[^s4] It was influenced by the Fifteenth Amendment, whose structure it copies.[^s7] It was the culmination of the women's suffrage movement's federal strategy, and its enforcement now runs partly through the Voting Rights Act of 1965.[^s5][^s4]
