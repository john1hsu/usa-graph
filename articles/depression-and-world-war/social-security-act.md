# Social Security Act

The Social Security Act is the 1935 federal law that built the core of the American social insurance system. Its preamble describes a "system of Federal old-age benefits" alongside grants that let the states better provide for older people, blind people, dependent and disabled children, maternal and child health, public health and unemployment compensation; it also set up a three-member Social Security Board and new taxes to pay for the system.[^s1] President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed it on August 14, 1935, and said it would give some protection to about thirty million people.[^s2][^s3]

## Background

Before the 1930s, caring for older people was mostly left to families, localities and states; apart from veterans' pensions, Washington played almost no part.[^s1] The Great Depression broke that arrangement: by 1932 about 34 percent of nonfarm workers were unemployed.[^s5] Mass movements demanded bolder answers. Dr. Francis Townsend's plan would have paid $200 a month to every citizen aged 60 or older, funded by a 2 percent sales tax, and Senator Huey Long's "Share Our Wealth" clubs spread rapidly by 1935; SSA historian Abe Bortz called the Townsend plan the bill's most important rival.[^s9]

Roosevelt created the Committee on Economic Security by Executive Order 6757 on June 29, 1934. Labor Secretary Frances Perkins chaired it, and Edwin Witte served as its executive director.[^s9] Its report went to Congress on January 17, 1935, the day Senator Robert Wagner of New York and Representatives Robert Doughton of North Carolina and David Lewis of Maryland introduced the bill.[^s2] The bill passed the House 372 to 33 on April 19 and the Senate 77 to 6 on June 19, with majorities of both Democrats and Republicans voting yes.[^s2]

## What it says

The act combined two kinds of programs.[^s1][^s5]

- **Federal old-age insurance (Title II).** Covered workers would receive monthly benefits from age 65, starting no earlier than 1942, capped at $85 a month and based on lifetime covered wages. Titles VIII and IX levied the payroll and unemployment taxes. Beginning in 1937, workers and employers each paid 1 percent on the first $3,000 of yearly wages, scheduled to rise to 3 percent each by 1949.[^s1][^s5]
- **Federal–state programs.** Grants helped states run unemployment insurance and pay means-tested aid to needy older people, to children under 16 deprived of a parent's support (the forerunner of welfare programs for families), and to blind people, along with maternal and child health and public health services.[^s1][^s5]

Section 210 left out agricultural labor, domestic service in private homes, casual labor, government employment and work for many nonprofit groups. In the early years only about half of all jobs were covered.[^s1][^s5] Using the 1930 census, DeWitt calculates that about 65 percent of Black workers and about 27 percent of white workers held jobs in the excluded categories, though about three-quarters of all excluded workers were white.[^s4] Because the state programs depended on state action, local politics also shaped what people received. Georgia's governor Eugene Talmadge refused to set up the state agency the act required, a stand that helped New Deal supporter E. D. Rivers win the governorship in 1936. NAACP official Charles Houston likened the law to a sieve that let most Black workers fall through.[^s8]

Roosevelt admitted the law's limits. No law could protect everyone against every hazard, he said, but this one was "a cornerstone in a structure which is being built."[^s3]

## Legacy

Congress kept building on it. The 1939 amendments added benefits for wives, widows and children and moved the first monthly checks up to 1940.[^s5] By then the payroll tax had survived two Supreme Court challenges in 1937, and Ida May Fuller of Ludlow, Vermont, received the first monthly check in January 1940.[^s8] The 1950 amendments covered about 9 million more workers, including regularly employed farm and domestic workers; disability benefits followed in 1957, Medicare in 1965, and Supplemental Security Income and automatic cost-of-living increases in 1972. By the early 2000s about 96 percent of jobs were covered.[^s5] The 1983 amendments, drawn from the Greenspan Commission's recommendations, taxed part of benefits, sped up payroll tax increases and phased in a later retirement age.[^s5]

Popular memory of the original law is often garbled. A widely forwarded list says Roosevelt promised a voluntary, tax-deductible program that would never be taxed. The SSA historian's office points out that the 1935 law made the tax mandatory for covered jobs, explicitly barred deducting it, and never set a $1,400 wage base.[^s7]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what the act excluded but disagree about why. **Racial-motive interpretations**, by scholars such as Robert Lieberman and Linda Gordon, hold that the exclusion was deliberate and mainly racial, a concession to Southern Democrats in Congress who wanted Black workers kept in low-wage farm and household jobs.[^s4] **Larry DeWitt**, writing in the SSA's research journal, argues instead that the exclusion came from Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau's warning that collecting payroll taxes from such workers was not administratively feasible. He notes that it passed with near-unanimous support across parties and regions and says the racial thesis is unsupported by the evidence.[^s4] **Richard Rodems and H. Luke Shaefer** treat the exclusion as policy diffusion: no compulsory unemployment insurance program abroad covered these groups at the outset, and the drafters closely studied those models. Even so, they conclude that by fitting a cautious design onto a labor market already segregated by race, the drafters helped reproduce racial inequality.[^s6]
