# National symbols of the United States

The national symbols of the United States are the emblems, mottoes, songs, plants, animals and figures used to stand for the country as a whole. Some have a precise legal status: Congress has written the flag's design, the Pledge of Allegiance, the national anthem, motto, floral emblem, march, tree, mammal and bird into federal law, most of them in titles 4 and 36 of the U.S. Code.[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4] Others became symbols through use rather than statute; personified figures such as Columbia and Uncle Sam served as a visual shorthand for the nation long before Congress took notice of them.[^s9]

## Origins

The earliest symbols came out of the Revolution. On July 4, 1776, the Second Continental Congress appointed Benjamin Franklin, John Adams and Thomas Jefferson to design a seal. Franklin proposed Moses stretching his hand over the Red Sea to overwhelm Pharaoh, Adams suggested Hercules, and the committee's August 20 report was tabled. Two further committees followed in 1780 and 1782 before Congress's secretary, Charles Thomson, working with William Barton, produced the design that was adopted.[^s6] The Confederation Congress approved the Great Seal on June 20, 1782. Its front shows a bald eagle holding 13 arrows and an olive branch, standing for war and peace, under a banner reading E pluribus unum; its reverse shows a 13-step pyramid beneath the Eye of Providence, dated 1776 in Roman numerals.[^s7]

The flag was defined even earlier. On June 14, 1777, the Continental Congress resolved that it would have 13 alternating red and white stripes and 13 white stars on a blue field, representing a new constellation; the Library of Congress notes that scholars credit the design to Francis Hopkinson rather than Betsy Ross.[^s8] Star arrangements were left to flag-makers until President Taft standardized the 48-star flag in 1912. There have been 27 official versions, and the present 50-star flag dates from July 4, 1960, after Hawaii became a state.[^s8] Federal law keeps the 13 stripes and adds a star on the July 4 after each new state's admission.[^s2]

Not every symbol came from a legislature. Columbia, a female personification of British origin, represented the country until Lady Liberty largely displaced her in the late 1800s. Uncle Sam emerged from the War of 1812; one account traces the name to Samuel Wilson, a Troy, New York, meat-packer whose army beef barrels were stamped "U.S.," though the National Park Service calls the origin disputed. Thomas Nast's cartoons and James Montgomery Flagg's World War I recruiting poster fixed his appearance, and in September 1961 Congress honored Wilson as the figure's progenitor.[^s9]

Most statutory designations came in the twentieth century. President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed a national Flag Day in 1916, and in 1949 President Harry Truman signed legislation fixing it on June 14.[^s8] Congress made "The Star-Spangled Banner" the national anthem on March 3, 1931. It declared "In God we trust" the national motto on July 30, 1956, and reaffirmed that wording in 2002.[^s1] The Pledge of Allegiance entered federal law through a 1942 joint resolution; "under God" was added in 1954, and Congress reaffirmed the text in 2002.[^s2] Later laws named the rose the national floral emblem (1986), John Philip Sousa's "The Stars and Stripes Forever" the national march (1987) and the oak the national tree (2004).[^s1] The National Bison Legacy Act, approved May 9, 2016, made the North American bison the national mammal.[^s4]

The bald eagle shows how custom and law can diverge. It had been on the Great Seal since 1782, but no statute named it the national bird until Public Law 118-206, which the Senate passed in July 2024, the House on December 16, and which was approved on December 23, 2024.[^s3]

## Significance

National symbols are meant to unite, but their laws also record who the nation's story includes. The bison act's findings say bison were tied to the economic and spiritual lives of many tribes through trade and sacred ceremonies, and that more than 60 tribes take part in the Intertribal Buffalo Council, restoring herds on tribal lands of over a million acres. They also credit ranchers and the American Bison Society, founded in 1905, with saving the species after its herds were decimated.[^s4] The national-bird law likewise states that bald eagles are integral to the spiritual lives of most Indigenous peoples and Tribal communities.[^s3] The bison act adds that its designation cannot be used to change any federal plan, policy or regulation.[^s4]

Designation has never meant compulsion. In 1943, after West Virginia schools expelled Jehovah's Witness children who would not salute the flag or recite the pledge, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3, in an opinion by Justice Robert H. Jackson, that compelling the salute exceeded constitutional limits.[^s10] On June 21, 1989, in Texas v. Johnson, a 5–4 Court held that burning a flag at a protest during the 1984 Republican National Convention in Dallas was expressive conduct, and that a state could not punish it to preserve the flag as a symbol of national unity.[^s11] The Flag Code itself is mostly etiquette: its rules on half-staff display, keeping the flag off the ground and retiring worn flags, preferably by burning, carry no penalties, and the President may modify them by proclamation.[^s2]

Popular stories about symbols are not always accurate. Franklin did call the bald eagle "a Bird of bad moral Character,"[^s6] and he described the turkey as "a much more respectable Bird," but he wrote this in a January 26, 1784, letter to his daughter about the eagle on the Society of the Cincinnati's badge, not about the seal. The letter was never sent, and its full English text was not published until 1817.[^s5] His 1776 proposal for the seal contained no bird at all, and no evidence shows that Jefferson strongly preferred either bird.[^s6]

## Connections

The Great Seal carries the motto E pluribus unum, while the 1956 statute made In God we trust the national motto.[^s7][^s1] The symbols collectively stand for the United States as a nation, which is why artists and governments use them as shorthand for it.[^s9] Most designations are established by statutes codified in the United States Code, titles 4 and 36, which Congress has amended as it added the tree, mammal and bird.[^s1][^s2][^s3] The Supreme Court's decisions in Barnette and Texas v. Johnson set the constitutional limits on how far government may require respect for the flag and pledge.[^s10][^s11] The Betsy Ross story and the Franklin turkey story are both popular myths about the origins of these emblems.[^s8][^s5]
