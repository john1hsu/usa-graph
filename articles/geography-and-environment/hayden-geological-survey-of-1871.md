# Hayden Geological Survey of 1871

The Hayden Geological Survey of 1871 was the first federally funded scientific expedition into the Yellowstone region of what were then Wyoming and Montana Territories.[^s1] Led by Ferdinand V. Hayden, head of the U.S. Geological and Geographical Survey of the Territories, it spent the summer of 1871 mapping, measuring, collecting, sketching and photographing the plateau's geysers, hot springs, canyons and lake.[^s3][^s2] Its findings reached Congress just as a bill to protect the area was moving, and the Yellowstone National Park Act followed on March 1, 1872.[^s1][^s4]

## Background

People had known the Yellowstone country for at least 11,000 years, and an 1805 map drawn on a bison hide already marked a "volcano" on the Yellowstone River.[^s2] Euro-American accounts were patchier. The Folsom–Cook–Peterson party of 1869 and the Washburn–Langford–Doane expedition of 1870, which named Old Faithful, published descriptions that excited scientists, though the *New York Times* still wanted scientific confirmation.[^s3][^s4] Nathaniel Langford of the 1870 party lectured on the region at New York's Cooper Institute in January 1871 and on tour.[^s4]

Hayden, born in Massachusetts in 1829, trained as a physician, served as an Army surgeon in the Civil War and in 1867 took charge of a federal geological survey that by 1870 had become the Geological and Geographical Survey of the Territories under the Interior Department, one of the four great federal surveys of the West.[^s2][^s6] A sundry civil act of March 3, 1871, gave him $40,000 to continue work on the sources of the Missouri and Yellowstone rivers.[^s9][^s7] His instructions called for gathering full material to illustrate his report, including sketches and photographs.[^s7]

## What happened

Hayden and his managing assistant James Stevenson enlisted 32 men: two botanists, a meteorologist, a zoologist, an ornithologist, a mineralogist, a topographer and an agricultural statistician, along with photographer William Henry Jackson, artists Thomas Moran and Henry W. Elliott, and support staff.[^s2][^s3][^s9] Accounts differ slightly on the start: the National Archives says the team left Fort D. A. Russell in Wyoming Territory on June 1, while the USGS dates the departure from Ogden, Utah, to June 8.[^s1][^s2] An Army Corps of Engineers reconnaissance under Captains J. W. Barlow and D. P. Heap traveled alongside; its photographer's negatives were later destroyed in the Chicago fire of October 1871.[^s3][^s9]

After reaching Fort Ellis near Bozeman on July 10, the party left its wagons in Paradise Valley and entered the Yellowstone region on July 21 at Mammoth Hot Springs, then moved on to Tower Fall.[^s2] It reached Yellowstone Lake on July 28, where members built a boat, the *Annie*, to explore the islands and sound the lake's depths; mineralogist Albert Peale recorded several earthquakes at Steamboat Point on August 20.[^s2] Between those dates the survey worked through the Lower, Midway and Upper Geyser Basins, and in late August it crossed the Lamar Valley before heading south to Fort Bridger, reached on September 29; Hayden ended the expedition on October 2.[^s2] Jackson photographed rock formations, canyons, hot springs and waterfalls while Moran sketched and painted the same scenes.[^s1]

## Aftermath and legacy

Hayden brought back geological, botanical and zoological specimens, sketches and photographs and displayed some for members of Congress.[^s5] He called the region an "ideal open-air laboratory" and urged Congress and the Interior Department to protect it.[^s1] In *Scribner's Monthly* in early 1872 he asked why Congress should not "at once pass a law setting it apart as a great public park."[^s9] Senator Pomeroy introduced the park bill on December 18, 1871; the Senate passed it on January 30, 1872, the House approved it 115 to 65 with 60 not voting, and it became law on March 1.[^s4]

The survey corroborated earlier reports of thermal activity, produced an improved map and drew national scientific interest; Hayden argued that Yellowstone's hot springs outweighed Iceland's geysers in scientific value.[^s3] Moran's large *Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone* (1872), drawn from sketches made on government expeditions, was bought by Congress and hung in the Capitol for more than 50 years; it has been on loan from the Interior Department to the Smithsonian American Art Museum since 2000.[^s8]

The survey's legacy is also one of exclusion. Hayden made what the USGS calls shameful statements about Native peoples, urging that they be made farmers or exterminated if they resisted federal demands, and the park his survey helped create relocated Indigenous people from their homelands.[^s2] Many Yellowstone landmarks bear the names of white explorers and scientists, and tribal leaders backed by more than two dozen tribes have sought to rename some to honor the Indigenous people who lived there.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

How much the survey's images mattered is debated. The National Archives calls Jackson's photographs and Moran's art powerful testimonials that carried Yellowstone to Washington, and the Interior Department and USGS write that they helped persuade lawmakers.[^s1][^s8][^s2] Photographic histories went further, crediting a handful of Jackson's prints with saving Yellowstone.[^s4]

Journalism scholar Howard Bossen checked that claim against the record. The Congressional Globe mentions photographs once, when Pomeroy told the Senate "there are photographs of the valley and the curiosities, which Senators can see"; the House record never mentions them.[^s4] He found the story of prints placed on members' desks first in a 1947 book by Jackson's son, uncorroborated by contemporary sources, and judged the photographs one part of the evidence, not shown to be more persuasive than reports, specimens, lectures and lobbying.[^s4] He also noted family ties: Representative Henry Dawes's son served on the 1871 survey and Senator Trumbull's son on the 1870 expedition.[^s4][^s9] WyoHistory.org's account accepts Bossen's view while noting that the photographs, as part of Hayden's exhibit, added to the case.[^s5]

## Connections
