# Gatun Locks

The Gatun Locks are the gateway between the Caribbean Sea and the Panama Canal. They stand on the canal's Atlantic side near the city of Colón, and the United States built them between 1909 and 1913 as part of its canal project.[^s9][^s2] In three steps, the locks raise ships about 85 feet from sea level to the surface of Gatun Lake, the artificial lake that carries vessels across most of the isthmus, and lower them again on the return trip.[^s1] Their successful test on September 26, 1913, was one of the last milestones before the canal opened.[^s2]

## History

The choice of locks over a sea-level canal was made only after digging had begun. In 1906 the Senate narrowly backed a lock design that chief engineer John F. Stevens favoured after seeing the Chagres River in flood.[^s2] Damming the Chagres at Gatun created Gatun Lake, at the time the largest artificial lake in the world, and the Gatun Locks were built beside the dam to step ships up into it.[^s2] At Gatun, crews removed more than 4.5 million cubic yards of rock and earth by drilling and blasting, plus 186,000 cubic yards dug by hand at the level of the chamber floors.[^s2] The first concrete was poured on August 24, 1909, with cement shipped from New York on the steamers *Ancon* and *Cristobal*, and the three Gatun lock flights took more than 2 million cubic yards of concrete.[^s1][^s2]

The design team included Lieutenant Colonel Harry Hodges, who led the gate work, Henry Goldmark, who designed the gates, and the electrical engineer Edward Schildhauer, who designed the gate machinery, towing system and controls.[^s1] Each chamber is 110 feet wide and 1,000 feet long, built in side-by-side pairs; Gatun has three pairs, Pedro Miguel one and Miraflores two.[^s1] The steel miter gates, whose leaves stand between 47 and 82 feet tall and about 7 feet thick, close in a V and are hollow so that they float partly when the chambers fill.[^s1][^s2] Work on the upper lock gates at Gatun was under way by July 1911.[^s2]

Building those gates was among the most dangerous jobs in the Canal Zone. As many as 165 rivet gangs, mostly West Indian workers, drove more than 6 million rivets into the canal's gates, at the peak 660,000 in a single month.[^s2] Skilled workers came mainly from the United States and unskilled labour mostly from the Caribbean, and the canal's gold and silver payrolls formed what the University of Florida's centennial exhibit calls a system of segregation to control the workforce.[^s8]

On September 26, 1913, the seagoing tug *Gatun* made the first trial lockage, with the valves worked by hand because the central control board was not yet ready.[^s1][^s9] On October 10 the Gamboa dike was destroyed to join Gatun Lake with the Culebra Cut, and the *Ancon* made the first official transit on August 15, 1914.[^s2]

## How the locks work

The locks run on gravity. The Panama Canal Authority states plainly that "no pumps are used at the Panama Canal."[^s1] To fill a chamber, valves at its lower end close and those at its upper end open; lake water then flows through 18-foot culverts in the walls and rises through 100 openings in each chamber's floor, which spreads the flow and limits turbulence.[^s1] A chamber fills in about eight minutes and takes some 26.7 million gallons, and each ship's transit uses about 52 million gallons of fresh water.[^s2] Gatun Dam also generated the electricity that powered the gates. Electric towing locomotives, nicknamed mules, which General Electric delivered in 1914, guide ships along the lock walls at about 2 miles per hour.[^s2][^s1]

## Significance

The Gatun Locks fixed the size of ships that could use the canal. The original chambers take vessels up to 106 feet wide, about the size of the *Titanic*.[^s9] By 1939, as warships grew wider and officials feared that sabotage could close the canal, Congress authorized a $277 million third set of larger locks. Excavation began on July 1, 1940, and a June 1942 National Archives photograph records dry digging at Gatun for the "Third Locks Program."[^s3][^s5] Secretary of War Henry Stimson ended the project on May 23, 1942, after nearly 13 million cubic yards had been removed, as wartime funds went elsewhere.[^s3]

Those Atlantic-side cuts were later reused. When Panama began a new set of locks in 2007, the original Atlantic-side excavations helped speed the work, and the inaugural transit of the expanded canal began at the new Agua Clara Locks on June 26, 2016.[^s7][^s6] The new locks are 70 feet wider and 18 feet deeper than the 1914 chambers and recycle 60 percent of their water in side basins.[^s6] The old Gatun Locks remain in service; the Panama Canal Authority reports their concrete in near-perfect condition after more than 80 years.[^s1] National Archives photographs from the U.S. agencies that ran the Canal Zone document the locks across decades of American operation.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Panama Canal:** the Gatun Locks are the Atlantic-side lock flight of the canal, one of three lock sites with Pedro Miguel and Miraflores.[^s1]
- **Gatun Lake and Gatun Dam:** the lake provides the water that fills the chambers, and the dam once supplied their power.[^s2]
- **Third Locks Project:** the limits of the 110-foot chambers led Congress in 1939 to authorize larger locks, abandoned in 1942.[^s3]
- **Agua Clara Locks:** the expanded canal's Atlantic-side locks, built with help from the old third-locks excavations, opened on the Atlantic side in 2016.[^s7][^s6]
