# Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, often called the Hart-Celler Act, is the federal law that ended the national-origins quota system and rebuilt American immigration around family ties and skills. President Lyndon B. Johnson signed it on October 3, 1965, at the base of the Statue of Liberty, as Public Law 89-236.[^s1][^s2] It removed the formally racial basis of U.S. immigration law, yet it also set new limits — above all the first cap on immigration from the Americas — and its effects on who became American were far larger than its authors publicly predicted.[^s3][^s4]

## Background

From 1924 to 1965, immigration was governed by national-origins quotas that barred Asian immigrants, limited Latin American immigration and fixed rigid ceilings for each European country. Germany's quota was 51,227 a year while Italy's was 3,845, and applicants had to apply under their country of birth, so some waited about a dozen years while other quotas went unfilled.[^s3] The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 kept the quotas — about 85 percent of 154,277 annual visas went to people of northern and western European lineage — and, while it repealed the last measures excluding Asian immigration and the bar on Asian naturalization, it assigned Asian quotas of about 100 visas by race rather than nationality. Congress passed it over President Harry Truman's veto, which had condemned the quotas as discriminatory.[^s8]

At the signing, Johnson condemned the quota system as contrary to the nation's founding principles and framed its repeal as correcting a cruel and long-standing wrong.[^s2] According to Congressional Quarterly's reporting at the time, the bill passed because a large Democratic majority backed the administration, public attention was focused more on civil rights and health care, and interest groups were willing to compromise.[^s3] The House approved it 318 to 95 on August 25, 1965, and the Senate 76 to 18 on September 22.[^s3]

## What it says

The act rewrote sections of the 1952 law. Section 201 capped Eastern Hemisphere immigration at 170,000 visas a year, and section 202 limited natives of any one country to 20,000 a year and declared that no one could receive a preference or priority because of race, sex, nationality, place of birth or place of residence.[^s1] Section 203 divided the visas among seven preferences: 74 percent for relatives of citizens and permanent residents (including 24 percent for brothers and sisters of citizens), 10 percent for professionals and people of exceptional ability, 10 percent for workers in short supply, and 6 percent for refugees from Communist countries or the Middle East and people displaced by natural disasters.[^s1][^s4] Spouses, minor children and parents of U.S. citizens entered outside the numerical limits.[^s4]

The old quotas and a transitional pool ended on June 30, 1968, and from July 1, 1968, immigration from the Western Hemisphere was capped at 120,000 a year — a region that had never before faced a numerical ceiling. The act also created a Select Commission on Western Hemisphere Immigration.[^s1][^s4] At the signing, Johnson said immigrants would now be chosen for their skills and close relationships to people already here, and he announced that Cubans seeking refuge would be admitted in an orderly program.[^s2]

## Legacy

The law opened the door to large-scale immigration from outside Europe. Employment preferences drew many highly educated Asian professionals, shaping the Asian American population and feeding the "model minority" stereotype, and the law created new opportunities for migration from Africa.[^s4] The Census Bureau reports that the foreign-born share of the population had fallen to 4.7 percent in 1970; Asia's share of the foreign-born rose from 8.9 percent in 1970 to 19.3 percent in 1980, and by 2024 the foreign-born numbered 50.2 million (14.8 percent of the population), of whom 51.4 percent were born in Latin America, 30.8 percent in Asia and 9.7 percent in Europe.[^s6]

For Mexico and Latin America the story ran the other way. Before 1965, Mexican migration included roughly 200,000 braceros and 35,000 permanent admissions a year; historian Mae Ngai argues that the new caps recast much of that movement as "illegal."[^s4] In 1976, Congress extended the 20,000 per-country limit and the preference system to the Western Hemisphere; President Gerald Ford, signing the amendments, said they would roughly halve the 40,000 Mexican nationals who immigrated legally each year.[^s7] Because the system offered few channels for unskilled workers in farming, construction and domestic service, an unauthorized population grew that is employed but has no lawful way to immigrate.[^s4]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree on what the law said but disagree about what Congress intended. At the signing Johnson described it as "not a revolutionary bill" whose numbers would change little.[^s2] Many scholars, including David Reimers and Roger Daniels, have argued that the rise in non-European immigration was an unintended consequence — that the family preferences were expected to favor Europeans and that Congress would not have passed the bill had it foreseen the results.[^s5] Legal scholar Gabriel J. Chin, drawing on the congressional record and interviews with participants, argues instead that the law was a deliberate civil rights measure and that Congress likely understood more Asian immigrants would follow.[^s5] Ngai offers a third emphasis: the law combined inclusion and exclusion, and its celebrated abolition of national origins has obscured the new restrictions it placed on the Americas.[^s4]

## Connections

The act was enacted by the **United States Congress** and signed by **Lyndon B. Johnson** on Liberty Island.[^s2][^s3] It **amends** the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, rewriting its quota sections, and **supersedes** the national-origins system created by the **Immigration Act of 1924**.[^s1][^s3][^s8] It sits alongside the earlier **Chinese Exclusion Act** and **Angel Island** in the history of Asian immigration, and it **affected** **Asian Americans** and **Mexican Americans** in opposite ways.[^s4] Chin links it to the same **civil rights movement** that produced the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.[^s5] Its Western Hemisphere limits **led to** the **Immigration and Nationality Act Amendments of 1976**.[^s7]
