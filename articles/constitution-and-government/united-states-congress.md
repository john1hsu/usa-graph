# United States Congress

The United States Congress is the lawmaking branch of the federal government. Article I of the Constitution opens by vesting "All legislative Powers herein granted" in a Congress made up of a Senate and a House of Representatives.[^s1] Its two chambers, its limited list of powers and its checks on the president and the courts make it the first of the three branches described in the Constitution; the House, with its frequent elections, was meant to stay closest to voters.[^s1][^s6]

## Origins

Congress replaced the single-chamber Congress of the Articles of Confederation, whose weaknesses led to the 1787 convention that drafted a stronger constitution.[^s6] On July 16, 1787, the framers settled on a bicameral legislature with equal representation of the states in the Senate, while House seats would follow population.[^s2][^s6] The apportionment formula was entangled with slavery: Article I counted the whole number of free persons, excluded "Indians not taxed," and added "three fifths of all other Persons"—enslaved people, who had no vote but still enlarged their enslavers' states' share of seats. The Fourteenth Amendment later replaced this clause.[^s1]

The new Congress was due to convene at Federal Hall in New York City on March 4, 1789, but only 8 of 22 senators and 13 of 59 representatives had arrived. The House reached a quorum on April 1 and the Senate on April 6, when it could finally count the electoral votes for president.[^s3] The First Congress quickly took up a promise made during ratification: on June 8, 1789, Representative James Madison proposed amendments, and in September Congress sent 12 to the states. Ten were ratified by December 15, 1791, becoming the Bill of Rights; one of the others, on congressional pay, was ratified only in 1992 as the Twenty-seventh Amendment.[^s7] Congress sat in Philadelphia from December 1790 and moved to the unfinished Capitol in Washington, D.C., in November 1800; British troops burned that building on August 24, 1814.[^s2]

## Powers and structure

**Two chambers.** Representatives serve two-year terms and must be at least 25 and citizens for seven years; senators serve six-year terms and must be at least 30 and citizens for nine years.[^s1] The Senate has 100 members, two per state.[^s6] House seats are divided among the states after each census, with every state guaranteed at least one.[^s1][^s5] The House began with 65 members, grew to 105 after the 1790 census, and kept growing until a 1911 law fixed it at 433 with two seats added for Arizona and New Mexico; no reapportionment followed the 1920 census, and the House has been capped at 435 since 1929.[^s5][^s6] Alaska and Hawaii briefly raised the total to 437 until the apportionment after the 1960 census.[^s5]

**Choosing senators.** The Constitution originally had state legislatures choose senators.[^s1][^s4] That system produced 45 deadlocks in 20 states between 1891 and 1905 and nine bribery cases before the Senate between 1866 and 1906; Delaware went four years without sending a senator.[^s4] Oregon adopted popular choice in 1907, and by 1912 as many as 29 states used primaries or general elections. The Seventeenth Amendment, ratified April 8, 1913, made senators "elected by the people," and in 1914 every Senate race was decided by popular vote for the first time.[^s4]

**Powers.** Congress holds enumerated powers written in Article I, Section 8—among them taxing and borrowing, regulating commerce, coining money, raising armies and the power "To declare War"—plus implied powers drawn from the clause allowing laws "necessary and proper" to carry them out.[^s1][^s6] Each chamber has distinct roles: revenue bills must start in the House, which alone can impeach; the Senate tries impeachments, needs two-thirds to convict, and gives "Advice and Consent" to treaties and appointments.[^s1] A presidential veto can be overridden by two-thirds of each chamber, and two-thirds of both may propose constitutional amendments.[^s1]

**Organization.** The House is led by the Speaker and the Senate by the majority leader, each normally from the majority party, while committees do most of the detailed work of writing and amending bills.[^s6] The Senate adopted its first cloture rule to limit filibusters in 1917, and the Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946 overhauled the committee system.[^s2]

**Who has served.** Congress long reflected the exclusions of American society. Hiram Revels of Mississippi became the first African American senator on February 25, 1870, during Reconstruction. Rebecca Felton was appointed as the first woman senator in October 1922, and Hattie Caraway became the first woman elected to the Senate on January 12, 1932.[^s2] Political scientists call the question of how closely Congress mirrors the nation's population descriptive representation.[^s6]

## Connections

Congress is established by the United States Constitution and is one of the three branches in its separation of powers.[^s1] It grew out of dissatisfaction with the Confederation Congress under the Articles of Confederation.[^s6] Its first session produced the amendments that became the Bill of Rights.[^s7] It has met in Washington, D.C., since 1800.[^s2] The Seventeenth Amendment remade how its Senate is chosen, and its House seats are reallocated after every census.[^s4][^s5]
