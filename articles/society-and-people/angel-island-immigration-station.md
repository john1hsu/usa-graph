# Angel Island Immigration Station

The Angel Island Immigration Station was the federal government's main port of entry for immigrants crossing the Pacific to San Francisco from 1910 to 1940. Unlike most stations, it was designed less to welcome newcomers than to enforce the Chinese exclusion laws, and for three decades it inspected, questioned and detained people from China, Japan, India, Korea, the Philippines, Russia, Mexico and dozens of other countries.[^s1][^s2] Today its restored barracks, with poems carved into the walls by Chinese detainees, are a National Historic Landmark and a museum inside Angel Island State Park.[^s4]

## History

The station grew out of anti-Asian law. Chinese workers had come to California after the 1848 gold discovery, and resentment of them grew during the hard times after the Panic of 1873. In 1882 Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act, which barred Chinese laborers and made Chinese immigrants ineligible for citizenship, the first federal law to restrict immigration by race and nationality; Congress tightened it repeatedly through 1902, and a 1907 "Gentlemen's Agreement" with Japan limited Japanese laborers.[^s1][^s2] In 1905 the War Department set aside 20 acres of the 740-acre island, and the Bureau of Immigration chose the site because isolation made detention easier. The station opened on January 21, 1910.[^s1][^s2]

Processing followed class and race. European and first-class passengers were usually cleared on board ship, while Asian and Pacific Islander passengers, especially those in steerage, were ferried to the island for medical exams and questioning before a Board of Special Inquiry.[^s2] Chinese applicants had to prove they belonged to an exempt class, such as merchants, or were the children of U.S. citizens.[^s7][^s4] Because the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire had destroyed many birth and immigration records, Chinese residents could claim U.S. birth and overseas children, and some sold those slots as false "paper son" and "paper daughter" identities.[^s2][^s4] Inspectors countered with long interrogations about family history and home villages, comparing answers with those of witnesses; mismatches could mean deportation.[^s4] The resulting Chinese Exclusion Act case files, which the National Archives now holds in regional facilities, preserve detailed family histories because applicants had to document identity and kinship.[^s6]

Detention could be grim. Chinese men were confined in crowded, unsanitary barracks, apart from women and other groups, and let out only for meals and limited exercise in a fenced yard.[^s4] The Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation estimates that about 300,000 people from more than 80 countries were held on the island, including about 100,000 Chinese, 85,000 Japanese, 8,000 South Asians, and smaller numbers of Koreans, Filipinos, Russians and Jews, and Mexicans; in the late 1930s several hundred Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi persecution also passed through.[^s2][^s3] The longest known detention lasted 756 days.[^s3]

On August 12, 1940, fire destroyed the administration building, and on November 5, 1940, the station moved to the mainland in San Francisco, returning the site to the Army; the Foundation dates the formal closure to that autumn.[^s1][^s2] During World War II the site briefly held Japanese internees and prisoners of war.[^s2] Chinese exclusion itself was overturned in 1943, though the Magnuson Act that year kept a strict quota on Chinese entries.[^s6][^s7]

## Significance

The station's most powerful legacy is its poetry. Detainees, mostly men from rural southern China with little formal schooling, carved hundreds of poems in classical Chinese forms about the voyage, confinement and their hopes; more than 200 have been documented, and the Smithsonian describes them as the first literary body of work by Chinese North Americans.[^s4] In 1970 state park ranger Alexander Weiss noticed them in barracks slated for demolition. Descendants and activists campaigned to save the building, the legislature funded restoration in 1976, the barracks opened as a museum in 1983, and the site became a National Historic Landmark in 1997.[^s2][^s4] Him Mark Lai, Genny Lim and Judy Yung raised funds to self-publish translations and oral-history excerpts in *Island*, republished in 2014.[^s4] No women's poems survive, because women were held in the building that burned.[^s4]

Scholars have also refined the station's popular image. Records of Pacific Mail passengers from 1913 to 1919 show that many Chinese arrivals were not detained at all, that Japanese arrivals were detained at a higher rate, and that many detainees were not Asian or not immigrants.[^s5] The Foundation notes that the often-cited "one million" figure counts both arrivals and departures at the port; recorded arrivals from 1910 to 1940 were about 550,000.[^s3]

## Connections

The station exists because of the **Chinese Exclusion Act**, which the National Park Service treats as the starting point of its story, so that law is listed as background and an influence.[^s1] It stands on Angel Island in San Francisco Bay, **California**.[^s1] It shaped the history of **Chinese Americans**, whose poems and paper-son stories define its memory, and of **Japanese Americans**, the second-largest group detained there.[^s4][^s3] It belongs to the wider story of **immigration to the United States** and is often contrasted with **Ellis Island**, since West Coast immigration was governed by racial exclusion.[^s2] The **1906 San Francisco earthquake** made the paper-son system possible, and the **Magnuson Act** of 1943 replaced Chinese exclusion with a strict quota.[^s2][^s7]
