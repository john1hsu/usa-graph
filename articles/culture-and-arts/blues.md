# Blues

The blues is a form of music created by African Americans in the rural South, which took recognizable shape around the turn of the twentieth century as a way to express feeling in the face of hardship.[^s6] It grew from the work songs and field hollers of enslaved people and later from the songs of Black prisoners on chain gangs, and its lyrics typically come in three-line verses in which the third line sums up or answers the first two.[^s2][^s6] Spread by sheet music and records and carried north by migrants, it became one of the foundations of American popular music.[^s4][^s2][^s1]

## Origins

Little is known about the earliest forms of the blues.[^s2] An English traveler's 1839 account of music on a Georgia rice plantation describes the songs of enslaved people that would later evolve into the blues, and during the Civil War white soldiers noted Black soldiers improvising a dissonant middle harmony part.[^s2][^s1] Smithsonian Folkways traces the music's roots to the arrival of enslaved Africans at Jamestown, Virginia, in 1619 and to field hollers and ring shouts, which carried West African traits such as call and response, polyrhythm, narrative storytelling and bent "blue" pitches.[^s6] By the 1890s, according to the Mississippi Department of Archives and History, the blues form was set and the music was being heard beyond the work camps.[^s1]

In Mississippi the music grew up in the cotton-growing Delta, played at juke joints, where young players found mentors, and at house parties.[^s1] Charley Patton, born about 1887, played Delta juke joints and house parties from 1897 to 1934; Son House, born in Coahoma County in 1902, became known for the bottleneck slide guitar characteristic of the blues.[^s1] Other regions had their own styles, such as Georgia's finger-picked Piedmont blues and Atlanta's twelve-string guitar sound.[^s2]

Trained Black musicians brought the folk blues to a wider public. W. C. Handy, a bandleader born in Florence, Alabama, in 1873, moved to Clarksdale, Mississippi, in 1903 to lead the town band.[^s3] Around that year, waiting at the Tutwiler railroad station, he heard a man play slide guitar with a knife while singing about the place where two railroad lines crossed; that encounter and a string band he heard in Cleveland, Mississippi, led him to add blues to his repertoire.[^s4] In Memphis he wrote down and combined fragments he had heard from itinerant singers into full compositions: "The Memphis Blues" (1912), whose rights he sold for $100 before it became a hit, and "St. Louis Blues" (1914).[^s3] These songs helped spark the country's first blues craze, and by 1919 he was being hailed as the "Daddy of the Blues."[^s4]

## Meaning and practice

Women were among the first professional blues singers. Gertrude "Ma" Rainey of Columbus, Georgia, said she sang blues before vaudeville audiences around 1900, when she was sixteen; she may have been the first professional female blues performer, and she influenced the teenaged Bessie Smith.[^s2] In 1920 Mamie Smith's "Crazy Blues" made her the first Black vocalist to cut a commercial vaudeville blues record.[^s5] "Race records" — recordings by Black artists marketed mainly to Black buyers — became a recognized market, and from the mid-1920s to the early 1930s record companies sent scouts across the South and into northern cities looking for blues performers.[^s2] Bessie Smith recorded more than 150 songs from 1923 on.[^s5]

Rural guitarists were recorded too. Son House recorded for Paramount in 1930, and Robert Johnson made his recordings in 1936 and 1937; Johnson's recordings later influenced rock guitarists such as Eric Clapton and Keith Richards.[^s1][^s5] Folklorists also recorded musicians outside the studio: John and Alan Lomax first recorded Lead Belly in 1933 while he was held in the Louisiana State Penitentiary, and Alan Lomax recorded Muddy Waters for the Library of Congress in 1941.[^s5] Johnson is also the subject of a legend that he sold his soul to the devil in exchange for his skill, a story told as folklore rather than documented history.[^s1]

As Black musicians from Mississippi moved north, the music changed with them. Big Bill Broonzy reached Chicago in 1920, and by 1947 Muddy Waters, born in Rolling Fork in 1915, was recording for Chicago's Aristocrat label, later Chess, alongside Howlin' Wolf and the songwriter Willie Dixon.[^s1] There musicians amplified their instruments and added drums and horns, turning the lone singer with a guitar into the blues band.[^s1] In the 1960s rediscovered Delta players such as Son House performed for college audiences and festivals, while rock bands filled their repertoires with borrowed Mississippi songs and guitar phrases.[^s1]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree that African Americans created the blues, but they weigh its roots and birthplace differently. The Mississippi Department of Archives and History's account places its birth in the Mississippi Delta and says it came from the mixing of Black and white musical cultures in the South rather than directly from Africa.[^s1] The Smithsonian's Folkways curriculum instead emphasizes continuity with West African aesthetics carried through slavery — call and response, polyrhythm, storytelling and guitar-picking styles — blended with new sounds in the Americas.[^s6] The New Georgia Encyclopedia describes the music as a blend of African and European traditions that developed across the whole South from the nineteenth century, rather than in one place.[^s2] Dating is uncertain too: Handy never gave a date for his Tutwiler encounter, and 1903 became the accepted year when the U.S. Senate declared 2003 the centennial "Year of the Blues."[^s4]

## Connections

The blues **originated among** African Americans, carrying the experience of slavery and prison labor into song.[^s2][^s6] It is most closely tied to the **Mississippi Delta**, though other Southern regions also shaped it.[^s1][^s2] It was **influenced by** field hollers and by work songs, including those sung by enslaved people and chain-gang prisoners.[^s6][^s2] The move of Black musicians to Chicago turned it into electric band music.[^s1] It **affected** rock and roll, whose musicians borrowed its songs and guitar styles,[^s1][^s5] and blues singers worked with jazz musicians — Ma Rainey's 1924 session included Louis Armstrong and Fletcher Henderson.[^s5] Robert Johnson's crossroads legend belongs to folklore rather than the documented record.[^s1]
