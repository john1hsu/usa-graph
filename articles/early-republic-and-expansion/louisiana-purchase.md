# Louisiana Purchase

The Louisiana Purchase was the 1803 agreement in which the United States paid France $15 million for Louisiana, a vaguely bounded territory of about 828,000 square miles (some 530 million acres) between the Mississippi River and the Rocky Mountains that later formed all or part of fifteen states.[^s3] It roughly doubled the size of the United States.[^s5] What France actually sold, however, was a European claim to sovereignty over a region where thousands of people from dozens of Native nations lived and held their lands, and acquiring that land took the United States most of the following century.[^s1][^s4]

## Background

Western farmers shipped their crops down the Mississippi through New Orleans. A 1795 treaty with Spain, which then held Louisiana, gave Americans the right to navigate the river and to deposit goods at New Orleans for export.[^s3][^s5] In 1800 France reacquired Louisiana from Spain.[^s3] Napoleon Bonaparte, who had taken power in France in 1799, hoped to revive a French empire in the Americas: he meant to reconquer the sugar colony of Saint-Domingue, where enslaved and free Black people had risen in revolution, and to use Louisiana to feed it.[^s3][^s5] In October 1802, as Spain's king signed the transfer decree, the Spanish official at New Orleans revoked the Americans' right of deposit.[^s5]

President Thomas Jefferson responded in January 1803 by sending James Monroe to join the U.S. minister in Paris, Robert R. Livingston. Their instructions authorized up to $10 million for New Orleans and the Floridas.[^s5][^s1]

## What happened

By early 1803 Napoleon's plans had collapsed. The revolution in Saint-Domingue devastated the French army sent to crush it, and war with Britain again threatened.[^s5][^s3] On April 11, 1803, foreign minister Talleyrand told Livingston that France would sell all of Louisiana. Monroe arrived the next day, and on April 30 the two Americans, acting beyond their instructions, signed a treaty of cession with French treasury minister François Barbé-Marbois.[^s5][^s1]

The deal came in three agreements. The treaty ceded Louisiana with the same extent it had held under Spain, leaving its borders undefined.[^s1][^s6] A first convention set the price at 60 million francs ($11,250,000), paid in 6 percent U.S. stock; a second committed the United States to pay up to 20 million francs ($3,750,000) in claims that American citizens held against France.[^s1] Article III promised that the inhabitants would be incorporated into the Union as soon as possible and meanwhile protected in their "liberty, property and the Religion which they profess."[^s1] Article VI pledged to honor Spain's treaties with Native nations until new ones were agreed by mutual consent.[^s1]

The purchase posed a constitutional problem for Jefferson, who read federal powers narrowly. He wrote that "the constitution has made no provision for our holding foreign territory" and expected that an amendment would be needed.[^s2] His Cabinet argued that it was unnecessary, and with Napoleon's deadline for ratification approaching he accepted their advice.[^s5] In the same letter he likened himself to a guardian who had invested a ward's money in valuable neighboring land, and floated giving Native peoples east of the Mississippi lands west of it in exchange for their homelands.[^s2] The Senate ratified the treaty 24 to 7 on October 20, 1803. Spain formally handed Louisiana back to France on November 30, and France transferred it to the United States at New Orleans on December 20.[^s5]

## Aftermath and legacy

In 1804 Congress organized the southern part of the purchase as the Territory of Orleans, with its major officers appointed in Washington.[^s6] Its French-speaking Creole residents pressed for more elected offices, while white residents across ethnic lines imposed new restrictions on free people of color and on enslaved people. In 1811 more than eighty enslaved people rose up on the German Coast above New Orleans in what historian Peter Kastor calls the largest single slave uprising in U.S. history; the revolt failed.[^s6] Louisiana became a state in 1812, and the purchase's borders were not settled until the Adams-Onís Treaty with Spain in 1819.[^s6] Whether states carved from the territory would permit slavery became a source of sectional conflict, addressed in the Missouri Compromise and the Compromise of 1850.[^s1]

For Native nations, the purchase began a long process of dispossession. Under the treaties and agreements that followed, Native people were pressed into land cessions, moved onto reservations and subjected to efforts to suppress their religions and cultures.[^s1] Historian Robert Lee, reconstructing government audits, counts about $2.6 billion in disbursements to Native nations for cessions within the territory between 1804 and 2012 — many times the price paid to France.[^s7][^s4]

## Debates and interpretations

**Why Napoleon sold.** Accounts weigh Napoleon's motives differently. The Thomas Jefferson Foundation stresses the revolution in Saint-Domingue, which decimated the French expedition and with it his plan for an American empire.[^s5] The State Department's Office of the Historian pairs the failed reconquest with the fact that war between France and Britain still threatened when Napoleon gave up his Louisiana plans.[^s3]

**What was bought.** The familiar account, including the National Archives' own, prices the purchase as a land bargain of about four cents an acre.[^s1] Robert Lee argues instead that most of the territory was a claim of preemption — the exclusive right to obtain Native title — layered over Native occupancy, and that real control came only through later treaties backed by violence, which legal historian Stuart Banner called "conquest by contract."[^s4]

**Jefferson and the Constitution.** The National Archives presents Jefferson as setting aside his strict reading because his hope for continental expansion outweighed it.[^s1] His own letters show him uneasy and wanting Congress to seek an amendment afterwards, even as he accepted ratification without one.[^s2][^s5]
