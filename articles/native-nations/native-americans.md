# Native Americans

**Native Americans**, also called American Indians (and, with Alaska Natives, the census category "American Indian and Alaska Native"), are the Indigenous peoples of the land that became the United States. They are not one people but many: hundreds of nations with their own languages, governments and histories. The United States recognizes 575 tribes as sovereign nations, in a relationship the Bureau of Indian Affairs describes as one between sovereigns.[^s2] All of the common terms are acceptable, but the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian notes that most Native people prefer to be called by the name of their own nation, and that many prefer "American Indian" or "Indigenous American" to "Native American."[^s3]

## History

People have lived in the Americas for well over ten thousand years. Ancestors crossed from Asia over a land bridge exposed by low seas during the last Ice Age, between about 12,000 and 20,000 years ago; sites in Florida and central Texas show human activity about 14,500 years ago.[^s4] Native peoples spoke hundreds of languages and built thousands of distinct cultures, from small mobile bands to towns and chiefdoms. Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, held perhaps 10,000 to 30,000 people around a thousand years ago; no city north of Mexico matched it until after the American Revolution.[^s4] The museum stresses that the land Europeans entered was not the empty wilderness colonizers imagined.[^s3]

Contact brought catastrophe. With no immunity to smallpox, measles, influenza and other Old World diseases, millions died in epidemics, alongside deaths from warfare and enslavement; some scholars estimate that as much as 90 percent of the population died within 150 years of contact.[^s4]

The United States first dealt with Native nations as foreign powers. Between 1778 and 1871 the Senate ratified 370 treaties, many of them land cessions, until Congress ended treaty-making in 1871. Reservations were created to resettle people forcibly moved from their homelands, and the General Allotment (Dawes) Act of 1887 broke up tribal land and opened it to non-Indian settlers, so that thousands of acres passed out of Native hands.[^s2] The federal government also operated or supported 417 Indian boarding schools in 37 states or territories that separated children from their families to erase their languages and identities; a 2024 Interior Department report documented at least 973 children who died while attending them.[^s10]

Citizenship came late. About 40 percent of Native Americans were still not citizens when Congress passed the Indian Citizenship Act on June 2, 1924, even though more than 12,000 had served in the U.S. Army in World War I. Because voting stayed under state law, some states barred Native people from the polls until 1957.[^s6] The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 established modern tribal governments, and about 25,000 Native men and women served in World War II.[^s2]

After the war, policy swung back toward assimilation. In the termination era of the 1950s, federal policy sought to assimilate Native people and end federal supervision over tribes, and from 1951 the bureau ran a relocation program that moved thousands of people to Denver, Los Angeles, Minneapolis and other cities, paying only for one-way trips; many faced discrimination and low-paying work. Native activists organized against relocation and termination, and in 1968 Dennis Banks, Clyde Bellecourt and George Mitchell founded the American Indian Movement in Minneapolis.[^s7] In a July 8, 1970, message to Congress, President Richard Nixon called forced termination "morally and legally unacceptable," rejected paternalism as well, and proposed that tribes be allowed to take over federal programs, a policy of self-determination without the threat of termination.[^s8] Congress followed with the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975, which required the government to contract with tribes, on request, to run programs previously run by the BIA and the Indian Health Service.[^s9]

## Today

The 2020 census counted 3.7 million people, 1.1 percent of the population, who identified as American Indian and Alaska Native alone, and 9.7 million (2.9 percent) alone or in combination with another race, up from 5.2 million in 2010.[^s1] More than 70 percent of Native people live in urban or suburban areas.[^s3] The United States holds about 56.2 million acres in trust for tribes and individuals. Native Americans pay the same taxes as other citizens, with specific exceptions such as federal income tax on income from trust lands. Fewer than 200 Native languages remain viable, down from more than 300 at the end of the fifteenth century.[^s2]

## Debates and interpretations

How many people lived in North America before 1492 remains disputed, and the answer shapes how the arrival of Europeans is judged. Estimates for the whole hemisphere range from about 2 million to 100 million.[^s4] Older, low counts relied on what Europeans recorded after contact: James Mooney (1928) estimated about 1.15 million people north of Mexico, and Alfred Kroeber (1934) about 8.4 million in the hemisphere. In 1966 Henry Dobyns argued that epidemics had already killed most people before anyone counted and estimated 10 to 12 million north of Mexico and 90 to 112 million in the Americas. Most historians consider his assumed 95 percent death rate too high except in places like Hispaniola, and William Denevan (1976) proposed about 55 million for the Americas and under 4 million north of Mexico. Nearly all scholars now accept that disease caused enormous losses; they dispute how large.[^s5]

## Connections

Native Americans were participants in both world wars: more than 12,000 served in the army in World War I[^s6] and about 25,000 in World War II.[^s2] Their history runs through the Dawes Act, the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 and the Indian Reorganization Act,[^s2] through the Indian Removal Act and the treaties and reservations that followed, and through the boarding schools,[^s10] termination, urban relocation and the American Indian Movement[^s7] to the self-determination policy of the 1970s.[^s8] The idea of tribal sovereignty, the government-to-government relationship administered by the Bureau of Indian Affairs, is central to Native life today.[^s2] The image of a "vanishing" people is a myth contradicted by census counts and by thriving Native communities.[^s1]
