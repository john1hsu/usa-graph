# Missouri Compromise

The Missouri Compromise was the act of Congress, approved on March 6, 1820, that admitted Missouri to statehood without a ban on slavery, paired it with the admission of Maine as a free state, and prohibited slavery in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of latitude 36°30′, the line of Missouri's southern border.[^s1][^s2] It ended the first great congressional fight over the expansion of slavery, and for 34 years its line shaped where slavery could legally spread, until Congress repealed it in 1854.[^s1]

## Background

Missouri, carved from the Louisiana Purchase, began seeking statehood in 1817 and 1818.[^s2][^s5] Slaveholding settlers had poured in, and by 1818 they had brought about ten thousand enslaved people with them.[^s2][^s3] Its admission as a slave state would have given the slave states a 12-to-11 majority in a Senate that had been evenly balanced.[^s7] Many northerners also resented the Constitution's three-fifths clause, which counted three of every five enslaved people toward a state's seats in the House and its electoral votes.[^s2]

On February 15, 1819, Representative James Tallmadge Jr. of New York proposed that Missouri be admitted only if no more enslaved people were brought in and children born to enslaved parents after statehood were freed at age 25.[^s4][^s2] The House adopted the amendment on sectional lines: by the American Battlefield Trust's count, northern members voted 80 to 14 for it and southern members 64 to 2 against.[^s11] The Senate struck it, the House refused to concur, and the bill died.[^s5] Northern supporters called slavery immoral and contrary to the nation's founding principles; southern opponents saw a threat to slaveholders' property and a first step toward ending slavery everywhere, and some began defending it as a positive good.[^s2][^s3]

## What it says

When the Sixteenth Congress met in December 1819, Senate leaders tied the admission of Maine, then a district of Massachusetts, to Missouri's admission without restriction.[^s11][^s7] Senator Jesse B. Thomas of Illinois proposed allowing slavery in Missouri while prohibiting it in the rest of the purchase north of 36°30′, and Speaker Henry Clay, President James Monroe and Senate leaders worked to assemble a majority.[^s11][^s3] The House accepted the package on March 2, 1820. When opponents moved to reconsider the next day, Clay ruled the motion out of order and sent the signed bill to the Senate before it could be raised again.[^s5]

The act's final section prohibited slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for crime, in the territory north of the line outside Missouri, but required that people escaping slavery into that territory be returned.[^s1] Monroe signed it on March 6; he said nothing publicly about it, though privately he had threatened to veto any restriction on Missouri.[^s4] Maine became the 23rd state on March 15, 1820.[^s4]

A second crisis followed. Missouri's constitution, adopted on July 19, 1820, barred the legislature from freeing enslaved people without their owners' consent and directed it to pass laws keeping free Black people from entering and settling in the state.[^s6] Some House members argued that this violated the rights of free Black people whom northern states recognized as citizens. Under a resolution sponsored by Clay, Missouri's legislature pledged in a "solemn public act" in June 1821 that the disputed clauses would not be enforced, and Monroe proclaimed Missouri the 24th state on August 10, 1821.[^s6][^s8] Missouri became the northernmost and westernmost state to permit slavery, and slave dealers such as Bernard Lynch kept selling enslaved people in St. Louis to western Missouri and down the Mississippi.[^s6][^s7]

## Legacy

Thomas Jefferson, writing to John Holmes on April 22, 1820, said the Missouri question, "like a fire bell in the night, awakened and filled me with terror," and called the settlement a reprieve rather than a final sentence.[^s10] He warned that a geographic line matching a moral and political principle would deepen with every new quarrel.[^s2] The crisis split the Democratic-Republican Party along sectional lines and left unresolved whether slavery could expand into territories acquired later.[^s3] Admitting states in free-and-slave pairs became the pattern for keeping the Senate balanced.[^s11] In 1854 the Kansas-Nebraska Act repealed the 36°30′ restriction in favor of popular sovereignty, and in 1857 the Supreme Court in *Dred Scott v. Sandford* held the compromise unconstitutional.[^s1][^s11] The Senate's role as the place where such sectional bargains had to be struck drew leaders like Clay, Daniel Webster and John C. Calhoun into a new era of Senate debate.[^s5]

## Debates and interpretations

**Morality or power?** Textbook accounts stress that the fight mixed principle and politics. Tallmadge invoked the Declaration of Independence against slavery but also sought to preserve a sectional balance of power, and northern anger over the three-fifths clause gave the dispute a sharp political edge.[^s3][^s2]

**A durable bargain or a broken consensus?** Historian Matthew Mason treats the 1820 and 1821 settlements as real compromises, won by moderates of both sections who put the Union first, and argues that the crisis clarified sectional politics and foreshadowed later conflicts.[^s8] Robert Pierce Forbes, in a revisionist study reviewed by Lacy Ford, argues that slavery shaped early national politics far more than it appeared and reads the South's unyielding resistance to restriction as a sign of how much slavery had narrowed national politics.[^s9] Mason also notes Donald Ratcliffe's argument that antislavery politics reached its peak influence in the 1820s rather than the 1830s.[^s8]
