# Equal protection

"Equal protection of the laws" is one of the great guarantees of American constitutional law: the promise, set down in the Fourteenth Amendment, that a government may not single people out for arbitrary or hostile treatment. The clause appears in a single sentence of Section 1, which commands that no state shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."[^s4] From that spare phrase the Supreme Court has built the law of racial segregation, sex discrimination, voting rights, and much else, making equal protection one of the most litigated ideas in the Constitution.[^s1]

## Origins

The Equal Protection Clause was born of the Civil War and the end of slavery. When Congress approved the Fourteenth Amendment, the House passing it 120 to 32 on June 13, 1866, and when three-fourths of the states ratified it on July 9, 1868, the central problem was the legal status of four million formerly enslaved people.[^s5][^s4] Southern governments had met emancipation with the Black Codes, laws that fenced freedpeople out of courts, contracts, and property. Republicans in Congress wanted to secure the equal civil standing they had already written into the Civil Rights Act of 1866, and to place it beyond the reach of a future Congress that might repeal an ordinary statute by simple majority.[^s3] Every draft of Section 1 contained an equal protection guarantee, and the clause gave that earlier legislation a constitutional foundation.[^s3]

The amendment's drafters wrote in broad words. In the Slaughter-House Cases (1873), the first major interpretation of the amendment, the Court acknowledged that African Americans were the clause's primary intended beneficiaries while recognizing that its language, protecting "any person," was not limited to any one group.[^s3] That generality would let the guarantee grow far beyond its original occasion.

## How it works

Equal protection does not require that everyone be treated identically. Laws sort people all the time, by age, income, or occupation, and the Constitution lets them. The modern doctrine instead asks how closely a court should examine a given classification, and it answers with tiers of scrutiny.[^s2]

Most laws face **rational basis review**, the most forgiving standard: a classification stands if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest, and only "invidious discrimination" is forbidden.[^s3] Under this test a law may be imperfect, even over- or underinclusive, and still survive. Economic and social regulation, age limits, and most ordinary distinctions are reviewed this way.[^s2]

A few classifications trigger far stricter review. **Strict scrutiny** applies to "suspect" classifications, above all race, and to laws that burden a fundamental right; such a law must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest, a standard few survive.[^s2] Between these poles sits **intermediate scrutiny**, developed for sex classifications: in Craig v. Boren (1976), building on Reed v. Reed (1971), the Court held that a sex-based line must "serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives."[^s2] The Court has been reluctant to add new suspect classes, declining in City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center (1985) to treat intellectual disability as one.[^s2]

The clause's reach widened over the twentieth century. Courts read it to guard against discriminatory enforcement of otherwise neutral laws (Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 1886), to scrutinize voting and apportionment schemes closely (Reynolds v. Sims, 1964), and even to recognize a "class of one" who is singled out for intentional, irrational mistreatment (Village of Willowbrook v. Olech, 2000).[^s3] It has been the ground for landmark rulings from Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which struck down school segregation, to Obergefell v. Hodges (2015), which invalidated bans on same-sex marriage.[^s3][^s1] At the same time the Court has set limits, holding in San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez (1973) that education is not a fundamental right for equal protection purposes.[^s1]

## Connections

Equal protection was **established by** the Fourteenth Amendment; the right has no life apart from the text that created it, which is why understanding the clause *requires* understanding that amendment.[^s4] The guarantee most directly **affected** African Americans, whose equal civil standing it was first written to secure, though its words reach every person within a state's jurisdiction.[^s3] Within Section 1 it sits beside, and is often **contrasted with**, the Due Process Clause: due process asks whether government used fair procedures and respected fundamental liberties, while equal protection asks whether government drew an unjustified line between people.[^s4]

The clause is the thread running through the graph's civil-rights story. It is the standard that [[us:plessy-v-ferguson]] betrayed in 1896 by blessing "separate but equal," and the standard that [[us:brown-v-board-of-education]] restored in 1954 by rejecting it. The system of [[us:jim-crow-laws]] was, in constitutional terms, a decades-long evasion of equal protection, and the wartime [[us:japanese-american-incarceration]] showed how even strict scrutiny of a racial classification could, in Korematsu v. United States, fail to stop a grave injustice.[^s1][^s2]
