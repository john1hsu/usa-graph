# Electric telegraph

The electric telegraph sent messages as coded pulses of current along a wire, letting news travel faster than any horse, ship or train for the first time. Samuel F. B. Morse's version, first demonstrated publicly on a government-funded line between Washington and Baltimore in 1844, spread across the country within two decades and became the first national electrical communications industry.[^s1][^s4] Before it, Americans used "telegraph" for visual semaphore systems that relayed signals between towers.[^s1]

## History

**Invention.** Morse, a painter who became an art professor at New York University, reportedly conceived of electric signaling on a voyage home from Europe in 1832, though he understood little about electricity.[^s1][^s2] The science came largely from others. Princeton's Joseph Henry had by 1831 built an electromagnet more powerful than any before it and used it to ring a bell at the end of a mile-long wire; by 1835 he had developed forerunners of all the essential parts of Morse's apparatus.[^s3] Chemistry professor Leonard Gale showed Morse how Henry's relay could boost a weak signal over distance, and technician Alfred Vail helped turn the idea into a working machine.[^s1] Morse patented his system in 1838 and demonstrated it in New York and Washington, but the Panic of 1837 stalled funding; in Europe he studied rival designs, including Charles Wheatstone's, and judged his own simpler.[^s1][^s4]

**The first line.** In 1843 Congress voted $30,000 for an experimental line of about forty miles from Washington to Baltimore, with the Senate approving it in the session's final hours and President John Tyler signing it. When underground pipe failed because of defective wire insulation, Ezra Cornell strung the wire overhead on poles and trees instead.[^s1] On May 24, 1844, Morse sent "What hath God wrought?" from the Supreme Court chamber in the Capitol to the Baltimore railroad station, a verse from Numbers 23:23 suggested by Annie Ellsworth, the young daughter of a friend.[^s1][^s2] Receivers first marked dots and dashes on paper tape, but operators soon learned to read the code by ear.[^s1]

**A new industry.** Morse hired Amos Kendall to manage the business, and the patent rights were divided geographically among the partners. The first commercial line, New York to Washington, opened in 1846, and competing systems such as House's printing telegraph followed. By 1851 the census counted 75 companies operating 21,147 miles of wire. Newspapers formed the Associated Press in 1848 to share telegraphed news, and railroads began dispatching trains by wire in 1851.[^s4] Morse sued imitators, and in *O'Reilly v. Morse* (December Term 1853), in an opinion by Chief Justice Roger B. Taney, the Supreme Court upheld his patents but struck down his eighth claim, to any use of electromagnetism for marking characters at a distance, because, in the words of the reported decision, it was "too broad and covers too much ground."[^s5] Henry testified for the defendants in such suits, and the bitter public quarrel that followed reflected two men's competing views of whether credit belonged to the scientist or the inventor.[^s3]

**Spanning the continent.** Congress offered a subsidy for a line to the Pacific, and Western Union won the job with a $40,000 bid. Edward Creighton, who had surveyed the route, dug the first post hole on July 2, 1861, and crews set about 27,000 poles across 1,086 miles between Fort Kearny, in Nebraska Territory, and Fort Churchill in roughly four months; the line was finished on October 24, 1861. At about $7 for ten words, it undercut horseback mail, and the Pony Express shut down that month.[^s6][^s2] During the Civil War, mobile stations and hastily strung wire connected armies in the field, and President Abraham Lincoln often went to the War Department telegraph office for news from the front.[^s2]

The western lines also ran through lands contested by Plains nations. On February 6–7, 1865, Lakota and Cheyenne fighters who had recently besieged Julesburg, in retaliation for the massacre of Cheyenne people at Sand Creek, Colorado, attacked the Mud Springs station in Nebraska; its telegrapher wired Fort Mitchell and Fort Laramie, and troops arrived within a day.[^s7]

**Monopoly.** Early competition gave way to regional pools and mergers. Western Union, which grew out of a company Hiram Sibley and associates incorporated in 1851, absorbed its last major rivals in 1866, the same year the first successful Atlantic cable was laid. Between 1867 and 1900 its annual traffic rose from 5.8 million to 63.2 million messages while the average rate fell from $1.09 to 30 cents, and it held over 90 percent of the market. It also added stock tickers (1867) and money orders (1870).[^s4]

## Today

The telephone, patented by Alexander Graham Bell in 1876, eventually overtook the telegraph; Western Union had declined to buy Bell's patent. AT&T bought a large stake in Western Union around 1909 but gave it up in 1913 under federal antitrust pressure. Telegraph traffic peaked in 1945 at about 236 million messages, fell to about 70 million by 1970 as long-distance calls became cheaper, and in 1988 Western Union sold its telecommunications business to concentrate on money transfers.[^s4] Its lasting economic effect was to tie scattered markets together: as prices moved by wire, trading concentrated in New York, and by 1910 the New York Stock Exchange handled 90 percent of U.S. bond trades and two-thirds of stock trades.[^s4]

## Connections

The telegraph was **introduced by** Samuel F. B. Morse as patentee and promoter, while being **influenced by** Joseph Henry's electromagnetic research, which made long-distance signaling possible.[^s1][^s3] Its transcontinental line **affected** the Pony Express by ending its reason to exist, and it **affected** the American Civil War by linking commanders and the president to distant armies.[^s2] It **affected** the growth of railroads, which used it to coordinate trains from 1851.[^s4] Related nodes include *O'Reilly v. Morse*, the patent case that defined its legal limits, and Western Union, the company that came to dominate it.[^s5][^s4]
