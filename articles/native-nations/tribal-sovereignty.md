# Tribal sovereignty

Tribal sovereignty is the principle that Native nations are self-governing peoples with authority over their own members and lands. The Bureau of Indian Affairs describes federally recognized tribes as holding inherent rights of self-government and a government-to-government relationship with the United States, and it lists 575 such tribes, counting American Indian tribes and Alaska Native villages (BIA, 2020).[^s1] The bureau characterizes the relationship as one between sovereigns, grounded in the Constitution.[^s2] The National Congress of American Indians (NCAI), which represents tribal governments, stresses that a tribal citizen belongs to three sovereigns at once, the tribe, the state and the United States, and that tribal status is political rather than racial.[^s7]

## Origins

Native nations governed themselves for thousands of years before Europeans arrived, and NCAI notes that they were treated as sovereigns from their first dealings with European settlers.[^s7] The United States continued that practice: beginning with a 1778 treaty with the Lenape (Delaware), early treaties were serious diplomatic agreements in which each side recognized the other's sovereignty, although in the nineteenth century the government increasingly used coercive treaties to take Native land.[^s9] The Senate ratified more than 370 treaties with tribes between 1778 and 1871, and NCAI points out that treaties exchanged and acknowledged rights rather than granting tribes powers they already held.[^s7] The Constitution names "the Indian Tribes" in the Commerce Clause beside foreign nations and the states.[^s7][^s5]

The doctrine took its legal shape in two Supreme Court cases arising from Georgia's attempts to extend its laws over the Cherokee Nation. In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831), Chief Justice John Marshall wrote that tribes were not foreign states able to sue in the Court's original jurisdiction; instead they might be called "domestic dependent nations," in a relation he likened to that of a ward to a guardian.[^s3] A year later, in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832), Marshall described Indian nations as "distinct, independent political communities" and held that the Cherokee Nation was a distinct community occupying its own territory where Georgia law had no force; the state statute used to imprison the missionary Samuel Worcester was declared void.[^s4] The Constitution Annotated treats these two decisions as the foundation of the modern doctrine of tribal sovereignty.[^s5]

## How it works

Federal courts describe tribes as unique aggregations holding attributes of sovereignty over their members and territory, and in *United States v. Wheeler* (1978) the Supreme Court treated tribal powers as inherent rather than delegated by Congress.[^s5] Tribes are not bound by the federal or state constitutions, though Congress has applied a statutory bill of rights to them, and they share the sovereign immunity from suit that the United States and the states enjoy.[^s5] In practice tribal governments decide their own citizenship and governing structures, make civil and criminal laws, run courts and police, tax and license, and provide services from education to environmental protection.[^s7]

Sovereignty is also limited. Congress stopped making treaties with tribes in 1871; in *United States v. Kagama* (1886) the Court upheld federal criminal laws on reservations as an exercise of a federal power to protect dependent peoples, and later decisions call congressional power over Indian affairs plenary but not absolute. The same *Wheeler* opinion said tribal sovereignty "exists only at the sufferance of Congress and is subject to complete defeasance."[^s5] Courts have held that tribes lack criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians without congressional authorization (*Oliphant*, 1978), and when the Court denied tribes criminal jurisdiction over members of other tribes in 1990, Congress restored it by statute, which the Court upheld in *United States v. Lara* (2004).[^s5]

Tribal sovereignty also limits the states. Absent congressional consent, states generally cannot tax reservation lands or tribal income earned on the reservation, and state regulation that would interfere with tribal self-government is barred; in *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians* (1987) the Court held that state regulation of on-reservation bingo would infringe tribal government.[^s6] In *McGirt v. Oklahoma* (2020) the Court reaffirmed that states have no authority to reduce federal reservations within their borders and that only Congress, by clearly expressing its intent, can disestablish one.[^s6][^s5]

Federal policy has swung between undermining and supporting this principle. Under termination, announced in House Concurrent Resolution 108 on August 1, 1953, the government sought to end federal supervision of tribes; Public Law 280 that month handed reservation jurisdiction in five states to state authorities, Congress began 60 termination proceedings by 1970, and more than three million acres of tribal land were relinquished.[^s8] The Nixon administration repudiated termination in 1970 in favor of self-determination, a turn NCAI associates with the start of the self-determination era in federal Indian policy.[^s8][^s7]
