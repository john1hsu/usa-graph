# North Atlantic Treaty Organization

The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) is a military alliance of North American and European states founded on the North Atlantic Treaty, which the United States, Canada and ten Western European nations signed in Washington on April 4, 1949.[^s2][^s4] It was the first peacetime military alliance the United States had entered outside the Western Hemisphere, and it placed Western Europe under the American nuclear umbrella.[^s1] Formed in response to the Cold War, it outlasted that conflict and took in former members of the Soviet bloc; the Office of the Historian calls it the largest peacetime military alliance in the world.[^s1]

## History

**Origins.** After the Second World War, Western Europe faced economic ruin and growing Soviet power. The Marshall Plan offered American aid for recovery, but the Soviet Union refused to take part or to let its satellites accept it.[^s1] Crises in 1947 and 1948 deepened Western alarm: civil war in Greece, a Soviet-backed communist coup in Czechoslovakia, and Stalin's blockade of West Berlin, which an airlift broke.[^s1] In March 1948 Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg formed a collective-defense pact, the Brussels Treaty.[^s1] For the United States, joining such an alliance meant breaking a roughly 150-year tradition of avoiding alliances with Europe.[^s4]

**Senate groundwork.** Senator Arthur Vandenberg of Michigan, the Republican chair of the Foreign Relations Committee, had long favored an independent foreign policy but changed course in the face of the Soviet threat.[^s4] On June 11, 1948, the Senate adopted his resolution advising the President to encourage regional self-defense arrangements consistent with the UN Charter and to associate the United States with them through constitutional processes.[^s3] State Department officer Jack Hickerson insisted on bipartisan language and Senate involvement, and neither Truman nor Thomas Dewey made the treaty an issue in the 1948 election.[^s4]

**Negotiation and ratification.** Europeans wanted a guarantee of automatic American intervention, but the Constitution reserves to Congress the power to declare war.[^s1] U.S. officials also pressed, against the Brussels powers' wish for a small pact, to include North Atlantic states such as Iceland, Norway and Portugal.[^s1] Dean Acheson signed for the United States on April 4, 1949, with Truman and Vice President Alben Barkley standing behind him.[^s4] After 16 days of hearings, the Foreign Relations Committee reported the treaty 13 to 0 on June 6; on July 21 the Senate rejected three reservations and gave its consent, 82 to 13.[^s5] Truman ratified it on July 25, and the treaty entered into force on August 24, 1949.[^s7]

**Cold War alliance.** The Korean War pushed the allies to build an integrated command, and the United States increased its troop commitments to Europe.[^s1] General Dwight D. Eisenhower became the first Supreme Allied Commander Europe in December 1950, and every holder of that post since has been an American officer.[^s9] Greece and Turkey joined in 1952 and West Germany in 1955; the Soviet Union answered West German membership by forming the Warsaw Pact with its Eastern European satellites.[^s1]

**After the Cold War.** Between 1999 and 2024 the alliance admitted sixteen more states, from Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic in 1999 to Finland in 2023 and Sweden, its 32nd member, on March 7, 2024; new members are invited only by consensus of all allies.[^s10] On September 12, 2001, the day after the September 11 attacks, the allies invoked Article 5 for the first time; NATO radar aircraft then patrolled American skies from October 2001 to May 2002.[^s8]

## Role

The treaty's core is Article 5: an armed attack on one or more members in Europe or North America is to be considered an attack on them all.[^s2] Each member then assists by taking "such action as it deems necessary, including the use of armed force," so each government decides its response, which need not be military.[^s2][^s8] Article 10 lets the allies, by unanimous agreement, invite other European states to join, and Article 11 requires ratification under each member's own constitutional processes.[^s2] For the United States that means the Senate's advice and consent: earlier rounds of enlargement, from Greece and Turkey in 1952 to Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic in 1998, came before the Senate as protocols to the treaty.[^s5][^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians have disagreed about what NATO meant for American power. Klaus Larres notes that the historian Lawrence Kaplan saw the long-term peacetime commitment as a sweeping change in American foreign policy, and that the U.S. nuclear guarantee gave Washington a dominant position in the alliance.[^s11] Critics in the 1960s described that position as an American empire; historians in the 1970s and 1980s preferred the milder phrase "empire by invitation," and Larres himself reads NATO as a means by which the United States shaped its sphere of influence in Europe.[^s11]

Americans have also debated the alliance's purpose after the Cold War. During the 1998 Senate debate on admitting Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic, supporters argued that enlargement would keep the United States engaged in Europe, extend stability eastward and right the division of Europe after Yalta.[^s6] Supporters included Senators William Roth, Joseph Biden and Richard Lugar.[^s6] Opponents such as Senators John Ashcroft, Tom Harkin and Daniel Patrick Moynihan warned of mission creep beyond the 1949 treaty, open-ended costs, and harm to relations with Russia that could draw a new dividing line in Europe; the Senate approved the accessions 80 to 19.[^s6]

## Connections

- The alliance was **established by** the North Atlantic Treaty, which the State Department's diplomacy museum describes as its legal basis.[^s4]
- It was **influenced by** the Senate's Vandenberg Resolution and by the Brussels Treaty, which preceded the negotiations.[^s1]
- It took shape **during** the Cold War, as a Western response to Soviet power in Europe.[^s1]
- West German membership **led to** the Soviet-sponsored Warsaw Pact in 1955.[^s1]
- Its only Article 5 invocation answered the September 11 attacks, which also preceded the U.S.-led war in Afghanistan.[^s8]
- Its first commander in Europe, Dwight D. Eisenhower, left the post in 1952 to become the 34th president.[^s9]
