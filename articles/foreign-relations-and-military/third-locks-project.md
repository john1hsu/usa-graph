# Third Locks Project

The Third Locks Project was a U.S. effort to give the Panama Canal a third, larger set of locks on the eve of the Second World War. Congress authorized it on August 11, 1939, to improve and enlarge the canal's capacity for defense and interoceanic commerce, within a cost ceiling of $277 million.[^s1] Crews began digging in July 1940 near the canal's existing Gatun, Pedro Miguel and Miraflores locks, but Secretary of War Henry Stimson halted the work on May 23, 1942, before any lock was built.[^s5] The channels it left behind lay dormant for decades, until Panama's own expansion put some of them to use.[^s5][^s6]

## Background

By the late 1930s ocean-going ships, especially warships, were outgrowing the canal's 110-foot-wide locks.[^s5][^s8] Officials also worried that a single well-placed explosion could cripple the lock system, so with war looming in Europe and Asia, canal officials recommended a separate third set of locks.[^s5] The 1939 law directed the Governor of the Panama Canal, under the Secretary of War, to carry out the work along the lines of a report dated February 24, 1939 (House Document No. 210), and limited the first year's appropriation to $15 million.[^s1]

The law also required that all new personnel in skilled, technical, clerical, administrative and supervisory construction jobs be U.S. citizens.[^s1] President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed it the same day but issued a statement objecting to that rule. He noted that the canal had long offered Panamanian citizens jobs not open to other foreigners, and that in the 1936 General Treaty the United States had pledged to keep giving Panamanians equal employment opportunities.[^s2] He signed because the bill authorized "a third set of locks" that he said would strengthen the security of the canal and the hemisphere, and he promised to ask Congress to bring the law into line with the commitments to Panama.[^s2] The State Department sent the statement to the U.S. ambassador on August 19, 1939, to share with Panama's government.[^s3] In January 1940 it warned Panama confidentially that a new funding bill would again lack equal hiring, because the War Department wanted contractor bids by February 7 and feared that an equal-employment clause would stir labor-union opposition; the Secretary of War promised a follow-up bill to place Panamanians on a par with Americans.[^s4]

## What happened

The new locks were to be 200 feet longer and 30 feet wider than the old ones, placed at a safe distance from the existing locks.[^s5] The University of Florida's centennial exhibit gives their planned size as 1,200 feet long and 135 feet wide, with a navigable depth of 45 feet.[^s6] Unlike the paired chambers of 1914, each new flight was to be a single lane running alongside, but apart from, the old double locks; GlobalSecurity's account ties the larger size to the Navy's planned Montana-class battleships.[^s9] The Naval History and Heritage Command confirms that those five ships, part of the 1940 "Two Ocean Navy" program, had a beam of 121 feet 2 inches, too wide for the existing locks.[^s7]

Work began on July 1, 1940, when a dredge started cutting the Pacific approach to a new Miraflores lock site; dry excavation in the Gatun sector on the Atlantic side began on February 19, 1941, and the schedule aimed at completion by mid-1946.[^s9][^s5] In all, nearly 13 million cubic yards were removed from the Atlantic and Pacific approach channels.[^s5]

## Aftermath and legacy

Stimson ended the project on May 23, 1942, as wartime money shifted to other priorities.[^s5] Other accounts add that after Pearl Harbor the Navy's priorities shifted from battleships to aircraft carriers, and that Army commanders argued new locks would add another target for air attack while shipping and materials ran short.[^s6][^s9] The Montana-class battleships were suspended that May before any keel was laid and cancelled in July 1943.[^s7] Some sources date the shutdown slightly differently; an engineering history by geologist J. David Rogers places it in March 1942.[^s8]

The unfinished channels lay dormant until Panama began a new set of locks in 2007 at a cost of about $5.2 billion.[^s5] On the Atlantic side, the new, larger chambers were built on the original third-locks excavation, which sped construction; on the Pacific side they were placed elsewhere and the old cut was filled in.[^s6] Panama broke ground on September 3, 2007, and the expanded canal opened on June 26, 2016.[^s10] Rogers counts the 1939 project as the first of several generations of plans to enlarge the canal, followed by sea-level canal studies and 1960s proposals to excavate with nuclear explosives.[^s8]

## Connections

- **Panama Canal:** the project aimed to enlarge the U.S.-built canal for defense and commerce.[^s5]
- **Gatun Locks and Miraflores Locks:** new lock flights were to be cut alongside these sites, with dry excavation in the Gatun sector and dredging toward a new Miraflores site.[^s9]
- **Panama Canal Zone:** the 1939 act authorized the work as additional facilities on the Canal Zone.[^s1]
- **The 1939 act:** Public No. 391 (H.R. 5129) authorized and capped the work and set its hiring rules.[^s1]
- **Panama Canal expansion:** Panama's 2007–2016 project built its Atlantic locks on the old excavation.[^s6][^s10]
