# Siege of Vicksburg

The Siege of Vicksburg was the 47-day Union operation that ended on July 4, 1863, when Confederate general John C. Pemberton surrendered the river city of Vicksburg, Mississippi, to Union general Ulysses S. Grant.[^s1][^s2] Its fall ended the Confederacy's last realistic chance to hold the Mississippi River, and together with the Union victory at Gettysburg the same week it is widely regarded as the turning point of the Civil War.[^s1][^s2]

## Background

Vicksburg sat on high bluffs above a bend in the Mississippi. Confederate batteries there threatened Union shipping on a river that Midwestern farmers relied on to send their crops to the Gulf, and the city connected the eastern Confederacy with the men and supplies of the Trans-Mississippi — western Louisiana, Arkansas and Texas.[^s2] Lincoln called it "the key" to the river.[^s2]

Taking it proved hard. Union gunboats attacked from upriver in the spring of 1862 and eventually withdrew; that fall Grant invaded northern Mississippi while William T. Sherman's river expedition reached Chickasaw Bayou north of the city, but Vicksburg held.[^s2] Union commanders also tried to dig canals that would let boats bypass the bluffs. Formerly enslaved people were pressed into that work in punishing heat, many fell ill, and the canals failed; some of the laborers later joined the U.S. Colored Troops.[^s2]

## What happened

In the spring of 1863 Grant used diversions to mask his main move: on the night of April 30–May 1 his army crossed the river from Louisiana into Mississippi south of Vicksburg.[^s2] Marching inland, it won at Port Gibson on May 1 and then at Raymond, Jackson, Champion Hill and the Big Black River between May 12 and 17, driving Pemberton's army back inside the Vicksburg defenses.[^s2] Grant's army reached those works on May 18.[^s6]

Grant tried to storm the city twice. On May 19 Sherman's corps attacked the Stockade Redan and was thrown back with about 1,000 Union casualties; on May 22, after a four-hour bombardment, infantry attacked along a three-mile front, and only one regiment briefly broke into a Confederate position before being driven out.[^s6] Grant then laid siege, and his artillery and the navy's gunboats on the river shelled the defenders.[^s6] On June 25 Union miners exploded 2,200 pounds of black powder under a redan on the Jackson Road, but some 20 hours of fighting in the crater gained no ground.[^s6]

Black soldiers fought in the campaign around the city. On June 7, about 1,500 Confederates attacked the Union supply depot and hospital at Milliken's Bend, Louisiana, hoping to distract the besiegers; newly raised Black regiments fought with bayonets and clubs until gunboats and reinforcements arrived, and one, the 9th, lost 45 percent of its men killed or mortally wounded.[^s3]

Inside Vicksburg, civilians dug caves into the hillsides, mostly with the labor of enslaved people, to escape the shelling.[^s2] Food and water ran short; one diarist recorded living on little more than mule meat, rice and milk.[^s2][^s6] Black refugees crowded into empty houses, churches, sheds and caves, many sick and without food or medicine.[^s2] By early July Pemberton's commanders judged a breakout impossible.[^s2] On July 3 he met Grant between the lines; that night Grant offered to parole the garrison rather than demand unconditional surrender, and at 10 a.m. on July 4 some 29,000 Confederates marched out and stacked their arms.[^s1][^s6]

## Aftermath and legacy

Five days later, on July 9, the Confederate garrison at Port Hudson, Louisiana, surrendered after learning that Vicksburg had fallen, opening the Mississippi to Union navigation from its source to New Orleans and cutting the Confederacy in two.[^s4][^s6] The victory made Grant's reputation and set him on the path to command of all Union armies.[^s6] In an August 26, 1863, public letter to James C. Conkling, Lincoln wrote that "the Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea," crediting soldiers from many states — and telling critics of emancipation that some victories could not have been won when they were without Black soldiers.[^s5]

U.S. Colored Troops helped occupy Vicksburg after its capture, under officers who disciplined them more harshly than white soldiers; the last troops left in 1877, and many veterans stayed and are buried in the national cemetery there.[^s3] White memory of the siege followed a different path: some of the city's women became leaders of the Lost Cause movement, which honored Confederate soldiers and blamed the North for the war, while veterans of both armies later met at joint reunions.[^s2] Since 2004 the Mississippi African-American Monument has stood on the park's tour road.[^s3]

A popular local story holds that because of the surrender, Vicksburg did not celebrate the Fourth of July again until 1945. As reported by historian Kevin Levin, the historian Christopher Waldrep, who studied the siege's memory, called this a myth: he found white and Black residents holding Fourth celebrations between 1863 and 1945, and traced the story to the park's superintendent in 1945.[^s7] Levin notes that Warren County counted 13,800 Black and 6,896 white residents in the 1860 census, and other writers stress that for many Black Vicksburgians July 4, 1863, marked the coming of freedom.[^s7][^s8]

## Connections

- The siege was part of the **American Civil War**, and its July 4 surrender is described by the Park Service as a turning point alongside **Gettysburg**.[^s1]
- It took place on and over the **Mississippi River**, whose control was the campaign's whole purpose, at **Vicksburg, Mississippi**.[^s2][^s1]
- It was **led** on the Union side by **Ulysses S. Grant** and on the Confederate side by **John C. Pemberton**.[^s6][^s1]
- It **led to** the surrender that ended the **Siege of Port Hudson**.[^s4]
- **U.S. Colored Troops** fought at **Milliken's Bend** and later garrisoned the city, a role **Abraham Lincoln** cited in defending emancipation.[^s3][^s5]
