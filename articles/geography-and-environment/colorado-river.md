# Colorado River

The Colorado River is the principal river of the American Southwest. From headwaters in the Rocky Mountains of Colorado and Wyoming it runs about 1,450 miles across the Colorado Plateau, through the Grand Canyon and along the desert toward Mexico, according to the U.S. Geological Survey.[^s1] It irrigates farms, supplies cities and, under a body of compacts, statutes, court decrees and a treaty known as the "Law of the River," is shared among seven states, Native nations and Mexico.[^s1][^s3]

## Geography

The USGS puts the basin at about 246,000 square miles, taking in all of Arizona and parts of California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming.[^s1] Its major tributaries are the Green and San Juan rivers.[^s6] Much of the flow begins as mountain snow; groundwater seeping into streams supplies an average of 48 percent of streamflow in the Upper Basin.[^s1] The river serves nearly 40 million people inside and outside the basin — including Denver, Salt Lake City, Albuquerque, Los Angeles and San Diego — and nearly 5.5 million acres of irrigated farmland, among them California's Imperial Valley (USGS, 2018).[^s1]

The law divides the basin at Lee Ferry, defined in the 1922 Compact as a point one mile below the mouth of the Paria River in northern Arizona: Wyoming, Colorado, Utah and New Mexico form the Upper Basin, and Nevada, Arizona and California the Lower.[^s2][^s4] The river once emptied into the Gulf of California; the Utah History Encyclopedia says it did so only "until recently."[^s6] When a planned release of about 132 million cubic meters was sent down the channel in Mexico between March 23 and May 18, 2014, it had to cross more than 40 kilometers of dry riverbed before reaching the estuary at the head of the Gulf.[^s12]

## Human history

Native peoples have lived along the river for thousands of years. Ancestral Puebloan (often called Anasazi) and Fremont communities farmed its side canyons and built trails across it, and Paiute, Ute and Navajo people later lived along its Utah reaches; in Navajo tradition the river's female spirit is called "Life Without End."[^s6] Downstream, the Mohave have farmed the river's floodplain for more than 800 years, alongside the Chemehuevi.[^s7] In 1540 Hopi guides led a party from Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's expedition to the Grand Canyon's rim; Hopi leaders told the guides to take the Spaniards by a roundabout route and reveal nothing useful, and three soldiers who climbed partway down found the river far wider than it had looked from above.[^s5] In 1776 the Domínguez–Escalante expedition forded it at the Crossing of the Fathers.[^s6]

John Wesley Powell's 1869 expedition, sponsored by the Smithsonian, launched four boats at Green River, Wyoming, on May 24 and emerged from the Grand Canyon on August 28 after losing a boat at Disaster Falls; a federally funded second expedition followed in 1871–72.[^s5][^s6]

As western farms and cities grew, the seven states sought a division before building large dams. Negotiations with Commerce Secretary Herbert Hoover, who proposed splitting the basin into upper and lower halves, began on January 26, 1922, and the Colorado River Compact was signed at Santa Fe on November 24.[^s2][^s3][^s4] It gave the Upper and Lower basins 7.5 million acre-feet a year each, let the Lower Basin add 1 million, and barred the Upper states from depleting the flow at Lee Ferry below 75 million acre-feet in any ten years.[^s2] No tribe took part, and the Compact said only that it did not affect the "obligations of the United States of America to Indian tribes."[^s2][^s13] The Boulder Canyon Project Act of 1928 put the Compact into effect, authorized Hoover Dam and divided the Lower Basin's share: 4.4 million acre-feet to California, 2.8 million to Arizona and 300,000 to Nevada.[^s3] Construction began in 1931 and took about six years; Reclamation officially counts 96 deaths at the dam site.[^s4][^s14] A 1944 treaty committed 1.5 million acre-feet a year to Mexico, a 1948 compact divided the Upper Basin's share, and the Colorado River Storage Project Act of 1956 authorized Glen Canyon Dam, whose reservoir, Lake Powell, formed in the 1960s.[^s3][^s6]

Arizona sued California in 1952. In 1963 the Supreme Court settled the Lower Basin division and, applying the 1908 Winters doctrine, held that the Fort Mojave, Fort Yuma (Quechan), Chemehuevi, Colorado River and Cocopah reservations had reserved enough water to irrigate their practicably irrigable land.[^s3][^s8] The Colorado River Reservation, created in 1865 for the Mohave and Chemehuevi and later shared with Hopi and Navajo people relocated there in 1945, includes about 90 miles of shoreline; during World War II the Poston incarceration camp for Japanese Americans was built on it.[^s7]

The Compact's figures rested on an unusually wet period. Negotiators had about 20 years of gauge records, while a 1976 tree-ring study by Charles Stockton and Gordon Jacoby put the average flow for 1564–1961 at about 13.5 million acre-feet a year and found 1905–1930 the wettest such stretch in 400 years.[^s10][^s11] After years of drought, Reclamation declared on August 16, 2021, the first shortage in Lake Mead's history, cutting 2022 deliveries to Arizona by 512,000 acre-feet, Nevada by 21,000 and Mexico by 80,000.[^s9]

## Connections

The river lies within the United States (`locatedIn`) before crossing into Mexico.[^s1] Its scarce, contested water shaped the Colorado River Compact (`affected`) and the decades-long case of Arizona v. California (`affected`).[^s2][^s8] It shaped California, whose cities and Imperial Valley farms depend on it (`affected`), and the Colorado River Indian Tribes, whose reservation economy rests on irrigated farming along it (`affected`).[^s1][^s7] It is closely related to the Grand Canyon, Hoover and Glen Canyon dams, John Wesley Powell, Herbert Hoover, Mexico, tribal sovereignty and Japanese American incarceration at Poston.[^s3][^s5][^s7]
