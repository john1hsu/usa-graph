# Reconstruction era

Reconstruction was the attempt, during and after the Civil War, to reunite the nation and to decide what freedom would mean for the formerly enslaved. Historians conventionally date it from Lincoln's first reunion plan in 1863 to the Compromise of 1877, the frame Eric Foner used in his standard history.[^s6] For a few years Black Southerners voted, held office and built institutions of their own, until violence and a Northern retreat ended federal protection.[^s1][^s2]

## Background

Lincoln began planning reunion in the fall of 1863, offering amnesty and allowing a state government to form once 10 percent of its voters swore loyalty. The Emancipation Proclamation freed people only in rebel-held areas, leaving more than 700,000 enslaved in loyal border states and Union-occupied regions, so Congress passed the Thirteenth Amendment on January 31, 1865, and it was ratified by year's end.[^s1] After Lincoln's assassination in April 1865, Andrew Johnson asked only that Southern states void secession, repudiate Confederate debts and ratify the amendment.[^s1]

Freedpeople pressed their own vision of freedom: land, schooling, reunited families and churches. General Sherman's Field Order No. 15 set aside coastal land for Black families, but in 1866 confiscated land was returned to former Confederates. Without land, many freedpeople ended up sharecropping, working plots for a share of the crop and often falling into debt.[^s1][^s2]

## Key developments

**From Presidential to Congressional Reconstruction.** Under Johnson's governments, South Carolina and Mississippi led in passing Black Codes; Mississippi's vagrancy law required freedpeople to carry proof of employment. Congress answered with the Civil Rights Act of 1866, passed over Johnson's veto, and with the Fourteenth Amendment, approved June 13, 1866, which made all persons born in the United States citizens and barred states from denying due process or equal protection.[^s1][^s4] The first Reconstruction Act, passed over another veto on March 2, 1867, divided the former Confederacy except Tennessee into five military districts and required each state to write a constitution approved by voters including Black men, and to ratify the Fourteenth Amendment. Arkansas was readmitted first, on June 22, 1868.[^s3] The House impeached Johnson, and he escaped conviction in the Senate by a narrow margin.[^s1]

**Black political life.** By 1877 more than 2,000 Black men had held office, including about 800 state legislators, fourteen U.S. representatives, and Senators Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce of Mississippi; P. B. S. Pinchback briefly served as governor of Louisiana.[^s1] Reconstruction governments created the South's first public school systems, and Black communities founded many schools themselves.[^s5] The Fifteenth Amendment, celebrated with a parade in Baltimore in May 1870, prohibited discrimination in voting on the basis of race, color or previous status.[^s1]

**Terror and retreat.** The Ku Klux Klan, organized in Tennessee in 1866, spread across the former Confederacy. Congress passed Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871 and investigated the Klan, but massacres continued; at Colfax, Louisiana, in 1873, as many as 150 Black Republicans were killed.[^s2] The Panic of 1873 began a depression that lasted most of the decade, Democrats won the House in 1874, and white "Redeemers" retook state after state.[^s1][^s2] After the disputed 1876 election, an electoral commission voted 8–7 for Rutherford B. Hayes; under the Compromise of 1877 federal troops were withdrawn, effectively ending Reconstruction.[^s2]

## Legacy

Afterward most Black officeholders were driven out and did not return in large numbers until the twentieth century.[^s1] In 1883 the Supreme Court struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875, and sharecropping and crop liens held much of the South in poverty for generations.[^s2] Yet the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments endured, and later arguments built on them laid groundwork for twentieth-century civil rights victories.[^s4][^s5] In January 2017 presidential proclamations created a national monument in Beaufort County, South Carolina, later renamed Reconstruction Era National Historical Park, to preserve and interpret the era's stories, from the quest for freedom to the challenge of abolishing slavery.[^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

For decades the dominant account, given scholarly standing by William Dunning and his Columbia students and popularized by Claude Bowers's *The Tragic Era*, portrayed Reconstruction as a corrupt failure. W. E. B. Du Bois's *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935) charged that racial bias distorted this work, and in the 1960s revisionists such as Kenneth Stampp largely overturned it.[^s5] Foner, following Du Bois, calls it a "splendid failure": it did not redistribute land, but its constitutional principles kept future struggles alive.[^s5]

Historians still debate its boundaries and meaning. Thomas Holt notes that the 1863–1877 frame cannot explain why many Black Southerners kept voting and holding office into the 1890s, and points to work such as Steven Hahn's that runs from slavery into the twentieth century and treats grassroots organizing as politics.[^s6] Elliott West proposes a "Greater Reconstruction" beginning around 1845, linking Western expansion, the Mexican–American War and federal policy toward Native nations, including reservations and assimilation schooling, to the remaking of the South.[^s7]
