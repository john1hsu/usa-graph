# Susan B. Anthony

Susan B. Anthony (1820–1906) was an organizer, lecturer and editor who spent more than five decades campaigning against slavery and for women's rights, and the National Park Service calls her perhaps the best-known suffragist of her generation.[^s1] Her 1872 vote in Rochester, New York, and her conviction for it the next year made her the public face of the demand that the Constitution already guaranteed women the ballot. The federal amendment she fought for became the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920, fourteen years after her death.[^s1][^s8]

## Life and work

Anthony was born on February 15, 1820, in Adams, Massachusetts, to Daniel and Lucy Anthony.[^s1][^s3] Her father was a Quaker who refused cotton grown by enslaved people, and she joined the Society of Friends as a girl and studied at a Friends' seminary near Philadelphia in 1837.[^s3] In 1845 the family moved to a farm near Rochester, where antislavery Quakers met almost every Sunday and Frederick Douglass and William Lloyd Garrison sometimes joined them.[^s1]

Teaching in Canajoharie, New York, in 1848, Anthony found that men earned $10 a month and women $2.50, and she joined the teachers' union.[^s1][^s3] She worked in the temperance and antislavery causes, and in 1851 Amelia Bloomer introduced her to Elizabeth Cady Stanton in Seneca Falls, beginning a partnership that lasted their lifetimes.[^s1][^s3] By 1856 she was an agent of the American Anti-Slavery Society, facing hostile mobs; in Syracuse her effigy was dragged through the streets.[^s1] During the Civil War she helped found the Women's Loyal National League in 1863 to press for emancipation.[^s3]

After the war she and Stanton helped found the American Equal Rights Association, and in 1868 they began editing *The Revolution*, whose masthead read "Men, their rights, and nothing more; women, their rights, and nothing less."[^s1] They had petitioned since 1865 for an amendment barring states from disfranchising citizens on the ground of sex, but the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments left women out.[^s9] When Douglass and others backed the Fifteenth Amendment, which barred racial but not sex discrimination in voting, Anthony and Stanton opposed it; the break became public in 1869, and they formed the National Woman Suffrage Association (NWSA) to seek a federal amendment.[^s1][^s3]

Suffragists then argued that women, as citizens, already had the vote. On November 1, 1872, Anthony, her three sisters and other Rochester women persuaded election inspectors in the Eighth Ward to register them under the Fourteenth Amendment, and on November 5 they voted; Anthony voted for Ulysses S. Grant and the Republican ticket.[^s6][^s3][^s4] Warrants followed for all fifteen women, but only Anthony was prosecuted.[^s8] She was arrested at home on November 18 and charged under the Enforcement Act of 1870 with voting without a lawful right to do so.[^s2][^s4] On December 26 a federal commissioner ordered her committed to the Albany jail when she refused to post $500 bail; a federal judge denied her habeas corpus petition on January 21, 1873, she was indicted three days later, and her lawyer Henry Selden paid her bail.[^s5][^s4][^s6]

The trial was moved to Canandaigua, where Justice Ward Hunt of the Supreme Court, sitting as a circuit judge, opened the case on June 17, 1873.[^s4][^s6] Hunt held that voting was not a privilege of national citizenship under the Fourteenth Amendment and that her good-faith belief was no defence: in his words, "she voluntarily gave a vote which was illegal."[^s2] He did not let her testify, refused to send the case to the jury, and directed a guilty verdict.[^s8][^s2] At sentencing she said she had been "degraded from the status of a citizen to that of a subject," and Hunt fined her $100 plus costs.[^s4] She refused to pay. Because Hunt did not jail her, she could not reach the Supreme Court through habeas corpus, and the law allowed no appeal; the fine was never collected.[^s2][^s1] The inspectors who registered the women were fined $25 each.[^s8] In *Minor v. Happersett* (1875) the Supreme Court confirmed that the Reconstruction amendments did not give women the vote.[^s2]

The movement turned back to Congress. On July 4, 1876, Anthony read a Declaration of Rights of Women outside Independence Hall after being refused a place in the Centennial program.[^s6] On January 10, 1878, Senator Aaron Sargent of California introduced an amendment providing that the vote "shall not be denied or abridged" on account of sex.[^s9][^s6] With Stanton and Matilda Joslyn Gage she compiled the *History of Woman Suffrage* from 1881.[^s1][^s3] After the rival associations merged in 1890 as the National American Woman Suffrage Association, she led it as president from 1892 until Carrie Chapman Catt succeeded her in 1900.[^s1] In February 1906, at her last convention in Baltimore, her final public words were "failure is impossible."[^s7] She died at her Rochester home on March 13, 1906.[^s7][^s1]

## Legacy

The amendment ratified in 1920 was widely known as the Susan B. Anthony Amendment, and she became the first non-fictional woman depicted on U.S. currency, on the dollar coin of 1979–1981 and 1999.[^s1][^s3] A posthumous presidential pardon issued in 2020 was rejected by the National Susan B. Anthony Museum and House, which said it would validate the proceedings against her.[^s8]

Her record also has a harder side. In opposing the Fifteenth Amendment, she and Stanton argued that educated white women should be enfranchised before Black men, adopting the racist positions common among white suffragists of the time despite their abolitionist work.[^s3][^s11] The Park Service notes that the *History of Woman Suffrage* she co-wrote covers only white women suffragists.[^s1]

## Connections

Anthony **participated in** the **women's suffrage movement** and in **abolitionism**, and she was a **member of** the **Women's Loyal National League**, the **American Equal Rights Association** and the **National American Woman Suffrage Association**, which she led.[^s1][^s3] Her partnership with **Elizabeth Cady Stanton** and her break with **Frederick Douglass** over the **Fifteenth Amendment** shaped the movement for decades.[^s1] Her vote rested on a reading of the **Fourteenth Amendment**, background a reader needs; her prosecution under the **Enforcement Acts** became the case of ***United States v. Anthony***, whose reasoning ***Minor v. Happersett*** confirmed.[^s2] Women voted in **Wyoming** Territory, and earlier in New Jersey, before her.[^s10][^s11] The **Nineteenth Amendment** carries her name in popular memory, and **Carrie Chapman Catt** succeeded her at NAWSA.[^s1]
