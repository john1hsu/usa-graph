# Mexican–American War

The Mexican–American War, also called the U.S.–Mexican War or simply the Mexican War, was fought between the United States and Mexico from the spring of 1846 to early 1848.[^s4][^s1] It began in a borderland both nations claimed, carried American armies from California to Mexico City, and ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, under which Mexico gave up California and most of today's Southwest.[^s1][^s4] By reopening the question of slavery in the West, it also helped set the country on the road to civil war.[^s7][^s12]

## Background

Mexico won independence from Spain in 1821 and abolished slavery in 1829.[^s12] Anglo-American colonists in its province of Texas rebelled in 1835–1836 and won independence; Mexico did not accept Texas as American territory, and its annexation angered Mexican officials and citizens.[^s12][^s2] In early 1845 Congress approved Texas annexation by joint resolution, a device needing only simple majorities, and Texas entered the Union as a slave state in December 1845.[^s5] Texas claimed the Rio Grande as its southern border; Mexico, and many Americans, held that the strip down to the Nueces River had never been part of Texas.[^s2][^s5]

President James K. Polk wanted that Rio Grande boundary and hoped to buy California.[^s4] He sent the diplomat John Slidell to Mexico with an offer for California, but Slidell's arrival helped trigger an army revolt against a Mexican president willing to talk with him, and no Mexican politician could accept giving up territory.[^s5][^s4] On January 13, 1846, Polk ordered General Zachary Taylor to advance from Corpus Christi to the Rio Grande, where his roughly 4,000 men trained artillery on the Mexican city of Matamoros.[^s4][^s2]

## What happened

On April 25, 1846, Mexican troops who had crossed the river ambushed a U.S. patrol, killing eleven soldiers.[^s12][^s2] Polk's war message of May 11 declared that Mexico had "shed American blood upon the American soil."[^s6] The House voted for war 174 to 14 and the Senate 40 to 2; Senator John C. Calhoun abstained, fearing a fight over slavery in any conquered land.[^s8][^s7] War was declared on May 13.[^s2]

Taylor had already won the war's first battles. At Palo Alto on May 8, his artillery broke a larger Mexican army under General Mariano Arista, which lost 102 killed against nine Americans; Taylor won again at Resaca de la Palma the next day.[^s3][^s4] He took Monterrey in September 1846 and, outnumbered about three to one, beat Antonio López de Santa Anna at Buena Vista in February 1847.[^s4][^s5] Meanwhile Stephen W. Kearny's column seized New Mexico, and American forces held California by January 1847.[^s4]

Because Mexico still would not negotiate, General Winfield Scott landed about 10,000 men near Veracruz on March 9, 1847 — the first large American amphibious assault — and fought his way inland through Cerro Gordo, Contreras, Churubusco and Chapultepec, entering Mexico City on September 14.[^s4][^s5] The occupation was brutal: the Park Service notes that looting, sexual assault and violence against civilians and surrendered soldiers were common.[^s2] Historian Pedro Santoni writes that Mexicans fought to defend their national honor and territorial integrity.[^s12]

## Aftermath and legacy

Nicholas Trist, a State Department official whom Polk had recalled, kept negotiating anyway and signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo on February 2, 1848.[^s1] Mexico gave up its claim to Texas and ceded present-day California, Nevada and Utah, most of Arizona and Colorado, New Mexico, and parts of other states; the United States paid $15 million.[^s1] The National Archives puts Mexico's loss at 55 percent of its territory; the Miller Center estimates that the cession enlarged the United States by about a quarter.[^s1][^s5] Mexicans in the ceded lands could keep Mexican citizenship or become U.S. citizens, but the Senate struck out Article X, which had protected their land grants.[^s1]

The human cost was high. Of 78,718 Americans who served, 1,733 died in battle and 11,550 died of other causes in the theater of war (Department of Veterans Affairs, 2023).[^s11] Mexico suffered nearly 50,000 casualties.[^s5]

At home, the war divided Americans. Henry David Thoreau went to jail rather than pay taxes supporting it.[^s2] In December 1847 Congressman Abraham Lincoln introduced resolutions demanding that Polk name the exact "spot" where blood was first shed; the House never acted on them.[^s9] And the Wilmot Proviso, which would have barred slavery from any land taken from Mexico, passed the House but was stripped out by the Senate in early 1847, deepening a sectional rift that the Compromise of 1850 only temporarily settled.[^s8][^s7][^s12]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians still disagree about who bears responsibility for the war. One long-standing view, voiced at the time by Ohio congressman Joshua Giddings, holds that Polk deliberately provoked a war of conquest in part to extend slavery; the Park Service likewise describes Polk as working to aggravate Mexico.[^s12][^s2] Other historians give more weight to factors Polk himself stressed or that lay on the Mexican side — Mexico's refusal to receive his envoy, American claims against Mexico, Mexican political instability and the attack north of the Rio Grande.[^s14][^s6] Miller Center scholar John Pinheiro recounts both Polk's troop movements and his justification, leaving readers to weigh them.[^s5] Historian Amy Greenberg, cited by the Park Service, argues that racism and anti-Catholic prejudice were used to justify extreme violence against Mexicans.[^s2] Brian DeLay adds a third party often left out: years of raids by Native nations across ten northern Mexican states had killed thousands and depopulated the region, which strengthened U.S. arguments for taking it and left northern Mexicans too divided and exhausted to resist.[^s13]

## Connections

- The war grew out of **Texas annexation** and the boundary dispute that came with it, and was justified by the idea of **Manifest Destiny**, which American victory strengthened.[^s5][^s2]
- It was fought in **Mexico**, **California** and **New Mexico**, and was **led** by **Zachary Taylor** and **Winfield Scott** for the United States and **Antonio López de Santa Anna** for Mexico.[^s4]
- It **led to** the **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo**, the **Wilmot Proviso**, and, through the slavery fight over the cession, the **Compromise of 1850**.[^s1][^s7][^s12]
- **Abraham Lincoln's** Spot Resolutions were among the sharpest congressional challenges to the war.[^s9][^s10]
