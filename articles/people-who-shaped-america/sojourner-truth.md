# Sojourner Truth

Sojourner Truth (c. 1797–1883) was born into slavery in New York's Hudson Valley as Isabella Baumfree and became one of the best-known Black speakers of the nineteenth century. After freeing herself and winning her son back from slavery in court, she took a new name in 1843 and travelled for four decades preaching against slavery and for the rights of women and freed people.[^s1][^s10] She could not read or write, so most of what survives of her words was recorded by others, which is why her most famous speech is also her most misremembered.[^s5][^s3]

## Life and work

Isabella was born about 1797 in Ulster County, New York, to James and Elizabeth Baumfree, people enslaved by the Hardenbergh family; Dutch was her first language.[^s1][^s3] At about nine she was sold, with a flock of sheep, for $100; after two more sales she was bought in 1810 by John Dumont of West Park, who held her until 1826.[^s1] She had five children: James, who died young; Diana, born after Dumont raped her; and Peter, Elizabeth and Sophia with Thomas, a man she was forced to marry.[^s1][^s2]

New York had begun legislating a slow, staged end to slavery in 1799, with general emancipation set for July 4, 1827.[^s6] Dumont promised to free Isabella a year early and then refused, so in 1826 she left with her infant daughter Sophia and found refuge with the Van Wagenen family, who paid Dumont $20 for her remaining labor.[^s1][^s5] Meanwhile her son Peter, about five, had been sold and taken to Alabama, in violation of an 1817 state law against selling enslaved New Yorkers out of state.[^s6] With the Van Wagenens' help and the lawyer Charles H. Ruggles, who charged no fee, she sued Solomon Gedney in *People v. Solomon Gedney* and won in 1828; Peter came home.[^s6][^s7][^s8] Rediscovered records of the case, including her deposition, are held by the New York State Archives.[^s7] The Historical Society of the New York Courts calls her the first Black woman to win a lawsuit in the United States.[^s6]

After a religious conversion she moved to New York City in 1829, became involved in the commune of the self-styled prophet Matthias, and later joined the Northampton Association in Massachusetts.[^s10][^s8] On June 1, 1843, she took the name Sojourner Truth and began travelling to preach and speak against slavery.[^s10][^s9] Her *Narrative of Sojourner Truth*, dictated to Olive Gilbert and, the Park Service says, published by William Lloyd Garrison, appeared in 1850.[^s1][^s8]

On May 29, 1851, she spoke at the Woman's Rights Convention in Akron, Ohio.[^s3][^s2] Marius Robinson, a convention secretary who edited the *Anti-Slavery Bugle*, printed her remarks on June 21, 1851; his text stresses women's intellect and closes with a sharp warning to men.[^s3][^s5] In 1857 she moved to Harmonia, Michigan, and she later made her home in nearby Battle Creek.[^s1][^s10]

During the Civil War she helped recruit Black men for the Union Army; her grandson James Caldwell enlisted in the 54th Massachusetts in 1863.[^s1][^s5] She raised money and food for the 1st Michigan Colored Infantry and paid for her work by selling her book and cartes de visite, sitting for at least eleven portraits sold for 25 to 50 cents. Her 1864 copyright claim recorded their title as "I sell the Shadow to Support the Substance."[^s4][^s9] In Washington she worked with the National Freedman's Relief Association, met President Lincoln on October 29, 1864, and rode the city's streetcars to press for their desegregation.[^s1][^s5][^s10] In 1870 she campaigned for federal land grants for formerly enslaved people and met President Grant, but the effort failed.[^s1] After the war she also spoke for women's suffrage and temperance.[^s9] She died at her Battle Creek home on November 26, 1883, aged about 86, and is buried in Oak Hill Cemetery.[^s1][^s5]

## Legacy

Truth's reputation rests heavily on the Akron speech, but in a version she did not write. In April 1863, prompted by Harriet Beecher Stowe's magazine portrait of Truth, Frances Dana Gage, who had presided at Akron, published an account in *The Independent* that gave Truth heavy Southern dialect and the refrain "Ar'n't I a woman?"[^s3][^s5] Gage wrote twelve years after the event without Truth's review, while the Library of Congress notes that Robinson is believed to have gone over his text with her.[^s3] The National Women's History Museum adds that Gage's version leans on the "Mammy" stereotype and falsely claims thirteen children.[^s5] Gage's text entered later editions of the *Narrative*, and the Park Service still publishes it under the famous title, with a note that Robinson's version lacks the phrase.[^s3][^s2] Historian Nell Irvin Painter, quoted by the Library, concludes: "We cannot know exactly what Truth said at Akron in 1851."[^s3]

Her cartes de visite are now in the public domain, and the Library of Congress makes scans of them freely available.[^s4] On April 28, 2009, a bust by Artis Lane was unveiled in Emancipation Hall of the U.S. Capitol Visitor Center, the first sculpture there to honor an African American woman.[^s10]

## Connections

Truth belongs to **African American history**, and **slavery in the United States**, in its Northern form, is the background to her life.[^s1] She **participated in** **abolitionism**, publishing her *Narrative* with Garrison's help, and in the **Akron Woman's Rights Convention** of 1851.[^s1][^s3] She **participated in** the **American Civil War** effort by recruiting Black men for the Union Army and raising support for Black regiments, the forerunners of the **United States Colored Troops**, and she later spoke for the **women's suffrage movement**.[^s1][^s4][^s9] Like **Frederick Douglass** and **Harriet Tubman** she escaped slavery to become a public voice against it, and she met **Abraham Lincoln** in 1864.[^s1][^s5] The popular dialect version of her speech is treated as the **"Ain't I a Woman?" myth**.[^s3]
