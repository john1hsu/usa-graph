# Betsy Ross flag myth

Few American stories are better known than Washington calling on a Philadelphia seamstress for the first flag. Betsy Ross was a real and skilled flag maker, but the claim that she designed and sewed the first Stars and Stripes at Washington's request rests on a family tradition first made public in 1870, and no document from the Revolutionary era supports it.[^s2][^s3] The Library of Congress instead credits the design to Francis Hopkinson, a signer of the Declaration of Independence.[^s1]

## The myth

In the familiar version, Washington, financier Robert Morris and Colonel George Ross, the uncle of Betsy's late husband John, visited her upholstery shop in 1776 with a sketch of a flag with thirteen stripes and thirteen six-pointed stars. She is said to have suggested five-pointed stars and shown how to cut one with a single snip of the scissors.[^s5] Later tellings added that she arranged the stars in a circle.[^s8]

The story was first told publicly by her grandson William Canby, who read a paper on the history of the flag to the Historical Society of Pennsylvania in 1870. Canby had been eleven when his grandmother died in 1836.[^s2][^s4] He and other relatives signed sworn affidavits that Betsy had told them the story herself.[^s5] An 1878 book by J. Franklin Reigart said she had designed and made the first flag at the request of Benjamin Franklin, Morris and George Ross, and that she formed a five-pointed star "with one cut."[^s9]

## What the evidence shows

Elizabeth Griscom was born in 1752 in Gloucester City, New Jersey, and raised a Quaker; she was expelled from the Quaker meeting after eloping with John Ross in 1773, and he died in 1775 during the war.[^s7] With her three husbands she ran an upholstery business, and Ross biographer Marla Miller calls her "one of Philadelphia's most important flag makers of the Revolution."[^s2] Her documented flag work begins with a payment from the Pennsylvania State Navy Board on May 29, 1777, for making ship's colours; by 1811 she was supplying garrison flags to the U.S. Arsenal on the Schuylkill River.[^s5][^s8]

Nothing in the record ties her to the design. No document shows that a congressional flag committee existed in 1776, Washington was not then a member of Congress, and no record shows him dealing with Ross about a flag.[^s4] Congress took up the flag only on June 14, 1777, resolving that it have thirteen red and white stripes and thirteen white stars on a blue field, "a new constellation"; the resolution did not specify how the stars were arranged.[^s1][^s4] Before that, Washington's army used the Continental Union flag, which kept the British crosses in its canton.[^s3][^s8] The circular "wreath" of stars associated with Ross is not documented before 1782, and surviving early illustrations mostly show six- or eight-pointed stars in rows.[^s3][^s8]

Canby's own research turned up no documents, and his sources were relatives who had not witnessed the events.[^s4] Vexillologist Peter Ansoff notes that Canby's paper did not claim Ross designed the flag or mention the circle of stars, and that a contemporary, Admiral George Preble, rejected the 1776 date because it came more than a year before the flag was adopted.[^s8] Ross was also one of several Philadelphia women who made flags for the Continental Army and Navy, alongside Rebecca Young, Margaret Manney and Sarah Barry; the maker of the very first Stars and Stripes is unknown.[^s3]

The strongest documentary claim to the design belongs to Francis Hopkinson. In 1780 he asked Congress to compensate him, famously requesting a quarter cask of public wine, for designing the flag along with seals and other emblems.[^s3][^s7] The claim was refused, but Ansoff writes that no one disputed his authorship; officials judged the work part of his official duties.[^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

Historians agree the Washington visit is unsupported, but they differ on what remains. The Library of Congress credits Hopkinson with the design, and Ansoff judges him the most likely designer.[^s1][^s8] Colonial Williamsburg stresses that Congress denied Hopkinson's claim partly because he was not the only person consulted, and concludes that the maker of the first flag is unknown.[^s3] The Betsy Ross House, which interprets her life, argues that her family ties to George Ross and to Delaware delegate George Read, and her earlier work making bed hangings for Washington, make a meeting plausible, while acknowledging that no written record proves the story; the Library of Congress likewise allows that she probably met Washington.[^s5][^s1]

## Why it persists

The legend spread just as the flag itself was becoming sacred. National journals carried Canby's account by 1873, and Charles Weisgerber's painting *The Birth of Our Nation's Flag* drew crowds at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago and was widely reproduced.[^s4][^s6][^s7] Patriotic education, books and toys reinforced the image in the early twentieth century, and a 1909 book by Canby's brother and nephew fixed it in public memory.[^s4][^s6] It fit a wider devotion to the flag that, beginning in the Civil War era, also produced the Pledge of Allegiance and Flag Day, which President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed nationally in 1916.[^s1][^s7]

Historian Marc Leepson suggests Americans want heroes and heroines, and the story is now part of school lessons, merchandise and Philadelphia tourism.[^s4] Miller observes that Ross offered later audiences a female founder, and that the legend has overshadowed her real working life, which is hard to recover because the family did not keep her letters or accounts and a satchel of her papers was burned in the 1880s.[^s2][^s7]

## Connections

The myth is challenged by the Flag Resolution of 1777, which shows Congress adopted the flag more than a year after the supposed 1776 visit and without fixing its star pattern (`challengedBy`).[^s4][^s1] It is also challenged by Francis Hopkinson's documented claim to the design (`challengedBy`).[^s1][^s8] The story was introduced by William Canby in his 1870 paper (`introducedBy`) and originated in the Ross family's memories of their Philadelphia upholstery shop (`originatedIn`).[^s2][^s5] It belongs to the history of the flag and the national symbols, and requires the Revolutionary War and the Second Continental Congress as background.[^s1][^s3]
