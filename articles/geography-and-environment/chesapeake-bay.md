misconception:
  myth: The Chesapeake Bay was created by a meteor or comet impact.
  fact: A bolide did strike about 35 million years ago, leaving a crater about 85 km across now buried 300–500 m beneath the southern bay, but the bay itself formed only about 10,000 years ago, when rising seas flooded river valleys; the old crater's depression helped steer those rivers, and so the bay's location.
  why: The impact story is dramatic and true in part, but treating it as the bay's origin skips the Ice Age sea-level rise that actually made the estuary, and hides why the crater still matters today through land subsidence and salty groundwater.
review:
  - question: If the Chesapeake Bay impact crater did not create the bay, why does it matter to the bay's geography?
    answer: The 35-million-year-old crater left a long-lasting depression that changed river courses, so when rising seas flooded the Susquehanna and other valleys about 10,000 years ago the bay formed where those valleys converged over it; the crater's breccia also compacts and lowers the land, and its very salty pore water limits fresh groundwater.
  - question: How did control of the Chesapeake Bay shape the outcome at Yorktown in 1781?
    answer: After the Battle of the Capes on September 5, 1781, the damaged British fleet withdrew to New York and the French controlled the bay, so no British reinforcements could reach Cornwallis while Washington's forces used the bay to move troops and supplies; the relief fleet sailed only on October 19, the day Cornwallis surrendered.
  - question: Why did the Chesapeake oyster fishery collapse after its 1880 peak?
    answer: Heavy, often illegal dredging and tonging stripped and broke up the reefs, reef habitat shrank, and the introduced MSX parasite (from 1959) and a worsening native Dermo parasite in the 1980s killed oysters, until both public and private fisheries collapsed in the mid-1980s.
  - question: What does the Chesapeake Bay TMDL limit, and why?
    answer: It caps the nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment entering the bay from all seven watershed jurisdictions, because those pollutants cloud the water and feed algae blooms that shade underwater grasses and lower the dissolved oxygen fish, crabs and oysters need.

# Chesapeake Bay

The Chesapeake Bay is the largest estuary in the United States, a long arm of the Atlantic where the fresh water of more than 150 rivers and streams mixes with the sea.[^s1] Its shores were the homeland of Native nations, the place where English colonization and the slavery of the tobacco colonies took root, and a stage of the Revolution and the War of 1812. Since 1983 it has been the focus of a federal–state cleanup partnership.[^s14]

## Geography

The Maryland State Archives measures the main bay at 191 miles from Havre de Grace, Maryland, to Virginia Beach, Virginia; it is 30 miles wide near Cape Charles, only 4 miles wide at Annapolis, and 12 miles across the mouth between Cape Charles and Cape Henry.[^s2] For all its size it is shallow, averaging about 21 feet deep, with a deepest point of 174 feet southeast of Annapolis. The Susquehanna, Potomac and James rivers supply about 80 percent of its fresh water, and salinity falls from about 30 parts per thousand at the mouth to nearly fresh water at the head.[^s2]

The watershed covers about 64,000 square miles, taking in parts of Delaware, Maryland, New York, Pennsylvania, Virginia and West Virginia and all of the District of Columbia (NOAA).[^s1] More than 18.6 million people lived in it in 2023, according to the Maryland State Archives, which also counts about 3,600 species of plants and animals.[^s2] Baltimore and Hampton Roads, two of the nation's leading ports, sit on its shores.[^s2]

The bay is a drowned river valley. As Ice Age glaciers melted, rising seas pushed the coast back from a line about 180 miles east of today's and flooded the valley of the Susquehanna River, whose old channel survives as deep troughs along the bay floor; the bay took roughly its present shape about 3,000 years ago and is still reshaped by erosion and sediment.[^s3] Far older is the buried crater of a bolide that struck about 35 million years ago. The U.S. Geological Survey describes a crater about 85 km wide lying 300–500 m beneath the southern bay; its depression steered the rivers that the sea later flooded, so it helped decide where the bay lies. Its compacting rubble still lowers the land around Hampton Roads, and its pore water, saltier than seawater, limits fresh groundwater.[^s4]

## Human history

People have lived around the bay for more than 10,000 years.[^s6] By the 1600s most of the peoples along its shores spoke Algonquian languages; the larger nations included the Powhatan, Piscataway and Nanticoke. Some were organized in tribute networks in which local chiefs paid food and goods to a paramount chief, such as Powhatan in eastern Virginia.[^s6][^s17] The name "Chesapeake" comes from an Algonquian word, written *chesepiooc* and in other spellings; it is often translated "great shellfish bay," but the Chesapeake Bay Program and the Maryland State Archives both say its exact meaning is unknown.[^s5][^s2] Spanish mapmaker Diego Gutiérrez had labelled it Bahia de Santa Maria in 1562.[^s2]

After Jamestown was founded in 1607, John Smith made two voyages around the bay in the summer of 1608, from June 2 to July 21 and from July 24 to September 7, reaching the Eastern Shore, the falls of the Potomac and the Susquehanna River.[^s7] His 1612 map recorded Native towns and remained the colonists' main map of the region for nearly a century.[^s17] Colonization brought war, disease and dispossession, and many Native families were forced out; others stayed. Today Virginia, Maryland and Delaware recognize Native nations of the Chesapeake, and the Pamunkey were the first Virginia tribe to gain federal recognition.[^s6]

The bay's rivers carried the tobacco economy of Virginia and Maryland. Africans were in Maryland from its 1634 founding, and through the 1600s planters replaced mostly white indentured servants with enslaved Africans. Virginia made slavery hereditary in 1662 and Maryland in 1663.[^s9] In 1781 the bay was central to the Revolution's last major campaign: after the Battle of the Capes on September 5, the British fleet withdrew and French control of the bay cut Cornwallis off at Yorktown.[^s10] In 1814 British forces used the bay to raid towns, burn Washington on August 24 and bombard Fort McHenry at Baltimore for 25 hours from September 13; the fort held, and its garrison flag became known as the Star-Spangled Banner.[^s11] In March 1862 the ironclads *Virginia* and *Monitor* fought to a draw near Hampton Roads.[^s2]

By 1860 the Chesapeake supplied most of the nation's oysters, and Black watermen were the backbone of the trade, though they faced discrimination at the docks.[^s13] Harvests peaked in 1880 at about 6.3 million bushels of market oysters, then fell through overharvest, reef destruction and the parasites MSX (from 1959) and Dermo, until the fisheries collapsed in the mid-1980s.[^s12]

A congressional study sponsored by Senator Charles Mathias in the late 1970s found that excess nutrients were the main cause of the bay's decline, leading to the 1983 Chesapeake Bay Agreement.[^s14] On December 29, 2010, the EPA set a Total Maximum Daily Load capping nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment, which cloud the water and lower its oxygen, with practices to be in place by 2025.[^s15] Regional 2025 goals were not met, and on December 2, 2025 the Executive Council approved a revised agreement with most deadlines in 2040.[^s16] Congress established the Captain John Smith Chesapeake National Historic Trail, more than 3,000 miles of water routes, in 2006.[^s8]

## Connections

The bay lies within the United States (`locatedIn`) and borders Maryland and Virginia (`borders`), whose waters divide its 3,237 square miles of main basin.[^s1][^s2] Its waters shaped the Siege of Yorktown (`affected`), since French control after the Battle of the Capes kept relief from Cornwallis.[^s10] Its decline produced the Chesapeake Bay Agreement of 1983 and the 2010 Bay TMDL (`affected`).[^s14][^s15] It is tied to Jamestown, Powhatan and John Smith; to the 1814 campaign and Fort McHenry; to the Susquehanna, Potomac and James rivers; and to the region's Native, African American and slavery history.[^s7][^s11][^s9]
