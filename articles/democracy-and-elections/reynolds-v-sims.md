# Reynolds v. Sims

*Reynolds v. Sims* is the 1964 Supreme Court decision that required both houses of every state legislature to be apportioned by population. Decided on June 15, 1964, in an opinion by Chief Justice Earl Warren, it held that the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause is violated when the weight of a citizen's vote for state legislators is substantially diluted compared with votes cast elsewhere in the same state.[^s1][^s2] Together with a group of companion cases decided the same day, it forced legislatures across the country to redraw their maps and shifted representation toward the growing cities and suburbs.[^s1][^s4]

## Background

Alabama's Constitution of 1901 required legislative districts to be as equal as possible by number of inhabitants and called for reapportionment after every federal census, but the legislature had not carried out a statewide reapportionment since the 1900 census.[^s2] Meanwhile the state grew from about 1.8 million people to about 3.2 million, with nearly all of the growth in urban areas.[^s2] By the 1960 census the gaps were stark: Bullock County (13,462 people) and Henry County (15,286) each had two seats in the state House, while Jefferson County, home to Birmingham, had 634,864 people and seven seats.[^s1] In the Senate, district populations varied by a ratio of up to about 41 to 1, and only about 25 percent of Alabamians lived in districts whose senators made up a majority; the House figure was about 26 percent.[^s1]

Alabama was not unusual. Many states had gone decades without redistricting, and some gave each county a legislator regardless of population, leaving rural areas heavily overrepresented.[^s3] Federal courts had long treated such complaints as political questions they could not decide, as in *Colegrove v. Green* (1946), until *Baker v. Carr* (1962) held that apportionment challenges were justiciable.[^s7] In *Gray v. Sanders* (1963) the Court struck down Georgia's county-unit system for statewide elections, concluding that political equality meant one person, one vote, and in *Wesberry v. Sanders* (1964) it required congressional districts to be as nearly equal in population as practicable.[^s5][^s7]

In August 1961 a group of young Jefferson County lawyers sued the state officials who supervised Alabama's elections, among them B. A. Reynolds, a Dallas County judge; M. O. Sims was among the plaintiffs.[^s2] In May 1962 a three-judge district court, with Judges Frank M. Johnson and Richard T. Rives in the majority, ruled for the plaintiffs and ordered a temporary plan for the 1962 election.[^s2][^s4] The legislature's own proposals, the 67-Senator Amendment and the Crawford-Webb Act, would still have left wide gaps; under the amendment, Senate district populations would have varied by about 59 to 1.[^s1]

## The decision

The Supreme Court heard argument on November 13, 1963, and chose the Alabama case as the lead opinion among six reapportionment cases because its malapportionment was so severe.[^s1][^s2] The vote was 8 to 1.[^s3] Warren's majority held that the Equal Protection Clause requires the seats in both houses of a bicameral state legislature to be apportioned on a population basis, and that states must make an honest, good-faith effort to draw districts as nearly equal in population as practicable.[^s1] "Legislators represent people, not trees or acres," he wrote, so the place a citizen lives cannot make that person's vote count for less.[^s1]

The Court rejected the so-called federal analogy—the argument that a state senate, like the U.S. Senate, could represent geographic units. The federal arrangement, Warren wrote, came from a unique historical compromise among sovereign states, whereas counties are subordinate instruments of state government.[^s1] The opinion allowed some flexibility for state legislatures, such as respecting county lines, and upheld the district court's power to impose a provisional plan while leaving the legislature the first chance to act.[^s1][^s4]

Justices Tom C. Clark and Potter Stewart concurred on narrower grounds. Clark would have struck Alabama's plans as an irrational patchwork of discrimination and suggested one house might depart from strict population equality if the other met it; Stewart found the unchanged 1901 apportionment wholly irrational after six decades.[^s1][^s3] Justice John Marshall Harlan II dissented, arguing that the Fourteenth Amendment's history showed it was never meant to govern state apportionment, that courts lacked manageable standards in this political thicket, and that the majority had in effect amended the Constitution by judicial decision.[^s1][^s3]

## Legacy

States across the country had to redraw their legislatures to approach equal population, and the case confirmed that courts could put their own plans in place when legislatures failed to act.[^s4] Warren later regarded the reapportionment cases as the most important of his tenure, because they gave citizens equal participation in their government.[^s2] Opponents sought a constitutional amendment allowing one house of a state legislature to be apportioned using geography and political subdivisions as well as population; on August 4, 1965, it won a 57 to 39 majority in the Senate, short of the two-thirds required.[^s6][^s4]

Later cases filled in the doctrine. *Avery v. Midland County* (1968) applied the rule to elected local governments; *Mahan v. Howell* (1973) upheld a state map with a 16.4 percent total deviation, giving states more room than in congressional districting; and *Evenwel v. Abbott* (2016) held that states may draw districts using total population rather than only eligible voters.[^s7] Reynolds remains the starting point for constitutional challenges to state and local districting plans.[^s7]

## Connections

Reynolds interprets the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and was written by Earl Warren.[^s1] It built directly on *Baker v. Carr*, which opened federal courts to apportionment claims, and on the equality standard of *Gray v. Sanders*.[^s4][^s5] Its subject was Alabama's legislature, and its broader result was the modern practice of redrawing state legislative districts by population.[^s2][^s4][^s7]
