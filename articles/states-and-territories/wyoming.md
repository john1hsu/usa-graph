# Wyoming

Wyoming is a Rocky Mountain and Great Plains state that Congress admitted as the forty-fourth state on July 10, 1890.[^s1][^s2] Its nickname, the Equality State, recalls the territorial law of December 10, 1869, that made it the first U.S. territory or state to grant women the right to vote.[^s5][^s1] It is also the nation's least populous state: the 2020 census counted 576,851 residents.[^s8]

## Geography

Wyoming covers about 97,089 square miles of land, with 5.9 residents per square mile in 2020 (Census Bureau).[^s7] The Continental Divide crosses the state; between the southern Wind River Mountains and the Atlantic Rim it splits to enclose the Great Divide Basin, also called the Red Desert Basin for its red soils.[^s15] West of it lies the Green River Basin. Both were covered by the ancient Lake Gosiute, whose deposits hold the world's largest beds of trona, the mineral that supplies about two-thirds of the world's soda ash, according to the Wyoming State Geological Survey.[^s15] Coal is the other great buried resource: the Energy Information Administration reported that Wyoming produced more than 40 percent of U.S. coal in 2015, much of it from the Powder River Basin, burned in power plants in more than 30 states.[^s14]

## History

People have lived in the region for at least 12,000 years, beginning with Clovis hunters.[^s1] By the nineteenth century the Shoshone, Arapaho, Cheyenne and Lakota were among the nations whose homelands and hunting grounds lay here.[^s1] Fur traders built the post that became Fort Laramie in 1834, and the Army bought it in 1849.[^s1] From 1843 large wagon trains on the Oregon, California and Mormon trails crossed the Divide at South Pass.[^s15]

Treaties signed at Wyoming forts reshaped Native lands. The 1851 Fort Laramie treaty regulated contact and promised annual goods; after Lakota leader Red Cloud forced the Army to negotiate, the 1868 treaty closed the Bozeman Trail and pledged to keep settlers out of the Black Hills, while confining the signers to reservations.[^s13] At Fort Bridger, Eastern Shoshone leaders including Washakie signed treaties in 1863 and on July 3, 1868; the second set aside about 3.2 million acres in the Wind River Valley, a reservation later cut to about 2.3 million acres by cessions.[^s11] On March 18, 1878, Northern Arapaho led by Black Coal arrived there under military escort; Shoshone leaders reportedly agreed to let them stay only temporarily, yet the two nations still share the Wind River Reservation.[^s12]

Congress created Wyoming Territory on July 25, 1868, a year before the transcontinental railroad was completed, and its government was inaugurated on May 19, 1869.[^s1][^s15] That December the first legislature—22 Democrats—passed a bill by Council president William Bright of South Pass City granting women the vote and the right to hold office, and Republican Governor John Campbell signed it.[^s4] Historians cite mixed motives: publicity to draw settlers when men outnumbered women about six to one, partisan calculation, the racist argument that white women deserved a vote that Black and Chinese men had, and real conviction.[^s4] Historians reject a popular story that Esther Hobart Morris won the bill at a tea party, but Morris did become a justice of the peace in 1870, women served on Laramie juries that March, and Louisa Swain cast the first ballot by a woman on September 6, 1870.[^s4][^s1][^s16] Wyoming was not the first place American women voted: under New Jersey's 1776 constitution some unmarried women with property voted until an 1807 law limited the vote to white male taxpayers. But Wyoming's was the first territorial or state law to enfranchise women, and it lasted.[^s6][^s5]

The Union Pacific owned the coal mines that fueled its trains. After an 1884 strike it had its managers hire only Chinese miners, and on September 2, 1885, white miners in Rock Springs killed 28 Chinese miners and wounded 15. Federal troops came, but no one was charged; Congress later reimbursed the Chinese miners' losses after China's diplomats pressed claims of nearly $150,000.[^s9]

Statehood was hard-won. A convention met in Cheyenne in September 1889, and voters ratified its constitution on November 5, 1889, by 6,272 votes to about 1,900 (accounts give 1,903 and 1,923).[^s2][^s1] With only about 55,500 residents estimated in 1888, short of Congress's informal 60,000 standard, and an equal-suffrage article that House Democrats attacked, the bill passed the House only 139 to 127 and the Senate 29 to 18.[^s2][^s3] At the convention only one delegate opposed suffrage, and another said he would rather stay a territory than enter without it.[^s3] Wyoming later ratified the Nineteenth Amendment on January 27, 1920, and in 1925 Nellie Tayloe Ross became the first woman elected governor to take office in the United States.[^s5][^s1]

During World War II the government incarcerated Japanese Americans at Heart Mountain, between Cody and Powell, from August 12, 1942, to November 10, 1945; at its peak, with more than 10,000 people, it was the state's third-largest town. Sixty-three young men from the camp who resisted the draft were convicted in Cheyenne and later pardoned by President Truman in 1946.[^s10]

## Today

The Census Bureau estimated Wyoming's population at 588,753 on July 1, 2025.[^s7] Coal, trona and other minerals remain central to its economy, and Yellowstone, set aside in 1872 as the world's first national park, draws visitors along with the trail and treaty sites.[^s14][^s15][^s1] The Eastern Shoshone and Northern Arapaho remain two nations sharing the Wind River Reservation.[^s12][^s11]
