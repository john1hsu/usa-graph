# Chunkey

Chunkey (also spelled chunky or chungke, and recorded among the Mandan as tchung-kee) is a Native American game of skill played on a smooth, packed-earth court. One player rolls a polished stone disk with concave sides, and contestants throw or slide long poles after it; the winner is the one whose pole comes to rest nearest the stone once it stops.[^s3][^s9] During the Mississippian period, roughly 1000 to 1600, it was the most popular and important game among Native peoples of the Southeast, and its finest stones are tied closely to the ancient city of Cahokia in present-day Illinois.[^s3][^s1]

## Origins

Archaeologists date the game's beginnings to the American Bottom, the Mississippi floodplain around Cahokia. Maryland's state archaeology lab, citing Timothy Pauketat, gives about A.D. 600 for its start, and stones become common there after 900.[^s9][^s1] Early stones were rougher and heavier, sometimes bulging rather than dished; Pauketat illustrates a small pre-Cahokian sandstone roller.[^s1][^s2] After Cahokia's sudden growth around 1050, workshops in the city turned out large numbers of finely ground disks of honey-coloured sandstone or quartzite, and Gregory's study concludes that the chunkey stone itself seems to have originated in the Cahokia area.[^s1]

The game then travelled. After 1050, communities in the Missouri watershed, the Great Lakes and beyond adopted chunkey along with other Cahokian practices, and Cahokia-style stones turn up south of the city and north of the Mississippian heartland.[^s1][^s2] A carved stone figure of a chunkey player found in Oklahoma in 1900 was probably made at Cahokia.[^s2] Native traditions give their own account: in tribal folklore the game was created by the gods and played by culture heroes, and Chickasaw cultural demonstrator Isaac Kemp says it reaches back to prehistoric times.[^s3][^s12]

## Meaning and practice

Chunkey was never only a pastime. Each Mississippian town kept a carefully maintained chunkey field, and at Cahokia the field sat at the heart of the city, in a Grand Plaza begun around 975.[^s3][^s1] Pauketat describes crowds of about a thousand cheering as players ran across the plaza.[^s2] Fine stones were often owned by clans or whole towns rather than individuals, and in the Mississippian period may have belonged only to chiefly lineages; chunkey players are the only athletes shown in Mississippian imagery.[^s3] Players appear on shell gorgets with chiefly symbols, and finely made stones were placed in elite burials.[^s4] In Cahokia's Mound 72, excavated by Melvin Fowler beginning in 1967, one burial held a cache of 15 polished, concave stones like those later used in the game.[^s6][^s5]

Games could last for hours, and spectators wagered heavily on them, sometimes staking everything they owned.[^s3][^s9] The game also carried ritual and religious meaning and may have been played at midsummer gatherings tied to the green-corn harvest.[^s3][^s4]

The game outlived Cahokia itself. On December 15, 1804, Sergeant John Ordway of the Lewis and Clark Expedition described Mandan men sliding sticks about four feet long after flattish stone rings; the journal's editors identify it as the Mandan hoop-and-pole game.[^s7] George Catlin sketched the same game at a Mandan village in 1832 and painted it in 1832–1833.[^s8] In the Southeast, chunkey declined after European arrival, perhaps because tribal political structures changed, while stickball grew more popular.[^s3] Revival is under way: Cherokees in North Carolina play a reconstructed form, the Cherokee Nation held chunkey contests in its Traditional Native Games competition in Tahlequah on April 13, 2024, and the Chickasaw Nation teaches the game as heritage nearly taken away.[^s3][^s11][^s12]

## Debates and interpretations

**When and where did it begin?** One view, cited by Maryland's archaeology lab from Pauketat's 2009 book, places chunkey's origin around 600 at Cahokia.[^s9] Gregory's review puts the common use of chunkey stones after 900 and credits Cahokia above all with the refined, standardized stone that spread after 1050, so the claim that Cahokia "invented" the game is better read as the region where it took its classic form.[^s1]

**What did it do?** Pauketat argues that chunkey helped organize Cahokian social and political life and that envoys carrying chunkey stones and war clubs spread a regional peace he calls the "Pax Cahokiana."[^s2] Bowne stresses instead its role in an ideology that legitimized ruling elites.[^s3] Thomas Zych reads each game as making social memory, binding communities to shared or rival histories of Cahokia.[^s10] Gregory goes further, suggesting chunkey mediated tensions and may have lowered violence — an interpretation she presents as her own.[^s1]

## Connections

The classic chunkey stone appears to have originated at Cahokia (`originatedIn`), the great Mississippian city whose plaza hosted the game, which is why Cahokia is background for this article.[^s1] Chunkey was part of Mississippian culture (`partOf`), whose elites it served.[^s3] Its history is reconstructed largely through archaeology (`studiedIn`), from Mound 72's cache to stones found across the continent.[^s2][^s5] It is related to the Mandan, observed playing it during the Lewis and Clark Expedition, and to the Cherokee and Chickasaw nations reviving it today, as well as to stickball, which rose as chunkey declined.[^s7][^s11][^s12][^s3]
