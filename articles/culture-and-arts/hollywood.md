# Hollywood

Hollywood is a district of Los Angeles, but for more than a century its name has stood for the whole American film industry. That industry did not begin there. It grew out of East Coast inventions and companies, moved west in the 1910s, and by the 1930s was dominated by a few studios that made, distributed and exhibited films. Law, politics and new technology have repeatedly remade it since.[^s1][^s2]

## History

**East Coast beginnings.** At Thomas Edison's laboratory in New Jersey, his assistant W. K. L. Dickson did most of the work on the Kinetograph camera and the Kinetoscope viewer; the Kinetoscope was first shown publicly in Brooklyn on May 9, 1893.[^s9] As movies became a business, patent fights followed. In late December 1908 the Edison and Biograph groups pooled their patents in the Motion Picture Patents Company, licensing nine producers and one importer, and for a few months a monopoly seemed possible before independent competitors appeared outside the "trust."[^s3]

**The move west.** Independents and licensed companies alike began wintering in Southern California. The New York Motion Picture Company reached Los Angeles in November 1909 and never went back; Selig set up a studio in Edendale in 1910; and in the 1911–1912 season the Nestor company built what is regarded as the first studio in Hollywood itself, at Sunset and Gower.[^s2] Hollywood, a small town whose streets the Wilcox family laid out in the 1880s, had merged with Los Angeles in 1910 to secure water; Cecil B. DeMille made it his permanent base in 1914.[^s1] Carl Laemmle, a German-Jewish immigrant who had battled the Patents Company, founded Universal in 1912 and opened Universal City in 1915; the Leo Baeck Institute notes that many of the major studios were founded by Jewish immigrants of that first wave.[^s10] Historian Steven J. Ross argues that Hollywood became the industry's capital after World War I began, when American films replaced European ones, and that only then did the place name come to mean the business.[^s11]

**The studio era.** By the 1930s a handful of companies dominated. In the Paramount case the Supreme Court described five majors—Paramount, Loew's, RKO, Warner Bros. and Twentieth Century-Fox—that produced films and, through subsidiaries, distributed and exhibited them, alongside Columbia, Universal and United Artists.[^s4] In 1945 the five majors had interests in about 17 percent of U.S. theaters, but in the 92 cities with more than 100,000 people at least 70 percent of first-run theaters were affiliated with them.[^s4] Sound transformed the screen in the late 1920s and ended many silent careers.[^s1]

**Censorship and self-regulation.** In 1922, with twenty-two state legislatures weighing censorship bills, producers formed a trade association under Will H. Hays to promise self-regulation. The industry adopted the Production Code in 1930; after the Catholic Legion of Decency threatened boycotts, it created the Production Code Administration under Joseph Breen in 1934, and the code shaped films into the 1950s.[^s5]

**Exclusion and alternatives.** Early American films often showed Black people in stereotyped roles that film historian Jacqueline Stewart traces to the minstrel tradition.[^s12] The work of Black filmmakers and performers, among them Oscar Micheaux, and of "race films" such as Richard Norman's *Regeneration* (1923) has long gone unrecognized; an Academy Museum of Motion Pictures exhibition that ran into 2023 set out to restore it to the story.[^s12] In the 1930s Hollywood also became a refuge for émigrés fleeing Nazism, among them Billy Wilder, Fritz Lang and Peter Lorre.[^s10]

**Blacklist and antitrust.** In October 1947 the House Un-American Activities Committee questioned Hollywood figures about Communist activity. Screenwriter Ring Lardner Jr. was removed from the stand on October 30 after refusing to say whether he was a party member; he and nine others, the Hollywood Ten, were convicted of contempt of Congress and blacklisted, and Lardner served nearly ten months in prison.[^s6] On May 3, 1948, in *United States v. Paramount Pictures*, the Court held block booking and price-fixing illegal, ruled that vertical integration was not illegal in itself, and sent theater ownership back to the lower court.[^s4] The consent decrees that followed made the studios separate distribution from exhibition.[^s7] Television studios moved into Hollywood in the 1950s and 1960s, and by the 1970s rising costs and departing companies weakened the district's role in production.[^s1]

## Today

On August 7, 2020, a federal court in New York ended the Paramount decrees, with a two-year sunset for the bans on block booking and circuit dealing.[^s7] The industry is now part of multinational media companies.[^s1] The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 425,020 jobs in the motion picture and sound recording industries in May 2023, with a mean annual wage of $90,450.[^s8]

## Debates and interpretations

Why the industry settled in Los Angeles is debated in emphasis. A popular explanation stresses flight from Edison's trust. The *History of the American Cinema* account gives more weight to climate, light and scenery: producer Fred Balshofer called the sunshine the stronger reason, Patents Company spies found his company in California soon after it arrived, and distance merely delayed lawsuits.[^s2] Historians cited by PBS SoCal add economic motives—Ross stresses Los Angeles's open-shop, nonunion labor market, DeMille reported crew costs well below East Coast rates, and Robert Sklar notes that a run to the Mexican border would have cost a day of shooting.[^s11]

## Connections

Hollywood **originated in** Los Angeles, which annexed the town in 1910.[^s1] It was **influenced by** the Kinetoscope and Edison's early film work, by the Motion Picture Patents Company whose litigation shaped where independents worked, and by the Production Code that governed what its films could show.[^s9][^s2][^s5] Related nodes include *United States v. Paramount Pictures*, the Hollywood blacklist and McCarthyism, and Universal Pictures.[^s4][^s6][^s10]
