# Battle of the Virginia Capes

The Battle of the Virginia Capes — usually called the Battle of the Capes in the United States and the Battle of the Chesapeake elsewhere — was fought on September 5, 1781, off the mouth of Chesapeake Bay near Cape Henry, Virginia, between a French fleet under François Joseph Paul, comte de Grasse, and a British fleet under Admiral Thomas Graves.[^s1][^s4] No Americans took part.[^s1] The brief fight settled little on its own, but it left France in command of the bay while Lord Cornwallis's army waited at Yorktown for help by sea, which is why the National Park Service treats it as a deciding factor in American independence.[^s1]

## Background

By the summer of 1781 Cornwallis had marched north from the Carolinas into Virginia and settled with about 7,000 troops at Yorktown, on the York River, to await supplies by sea.[^s5] George Washington at first favoured attacking British-held New York with his French allies, but the French commander, the comte de Rochambeau, judged its defences too strong, and Washington came round to a Virginia campaign largely because of de Grasse's naval plans.[^s2] When de Grasse wrote in late July that he would bring his fleet from the West Indies to the Chesapeake, the whole allied strategy came to depend on it; Washington learned of the plan on August 14 and turned the American and French armies south.[^s1][^s2]

The expedition also depended on Spain. De Grasse could raise only about 50,000 livres at Cap-Français in Saint-Domingue, so the Spanish official Francisco de Saavedra arranged for the money to come from Havana, and Spanish warships guarded Saint-Domingue so that de Grasse could take his whole fleet north.[^s8] The historian James A. Lewis has argued that Spain's part in Yorktown has often been belittled or ignored, and traced the funds to a loan arranged by Spanish officials rather than to the private gifts of later legend.[^s7] On August 15 Havana residents pledged about 500,000 pesos in a single day, and the frigate *Aigrette* delivered the gold and silver to de Grasse off Matanzas on August 18.[^s8]

## What happened

De Grasse left the Caribbean on August 5 with 28 ships of the line and about 3,000 troops, avoiding British fleets by way of the Bahama passages.[^s1][^s5] A British squadron under Samuel Hood reached the Chesapeake first, on August 25, found it empty and sailed on to New York, where it joined Graves.[^s1] The French entered the bay at the end of August and landed their troops near Jamestown; Graves left New York on August 31 with 19 ships of the line, expecting to arrive ahead of the French.[^s1][^s2]

On the morning of September 5 the British sighted the French fleet inside the capes. Some French officers were ashore and crews were unloading supplies, so de Grasse's ships went out to sea in a confused rush, some of them short-handed; Graves held back rather than strike while the French were disorganised, giving them time to form a line of battle.[^s1][^s4] De Grasse took 24 ships of the line into action.[^s2] Firing began in the late afternoon and ended at sunset. Because of the wind and Graves's unclear signals, the rear of the British line, under Hood, never closed with the enemy, so only the leading squadrons fought.[^s1][^s5] The French aimed high at sails and rigging and left at least two British ships unmanageable.[^s2] The National Park Service counts 90 British killed and 246 wounded, with six British ships damaged, against about 209 French casualties and two French ships damaged.[^s1]

For several days the fleets drifted at sea, out of sight of the bay. De Grasse slipped back into the bay first, on September 9–10, and found that the comte de Barras had arrived from Newport with eight more ships of the line, giving the French about 36.[^s1][^s5] Outnumbered, with a battered fleet, Graves returned to New York.[^s1]

## Aftermath and legacy

With the bay closed, Cornwallis could be neither reinforced nor rescued, while Washington used the Chesapeake to move troops and supplies to Yorktown.[^s1][^s2] When de Grasse proposed in late September to take his fleet out of the bay to meet British reinforcements, Washington wrote on September 25 that the siege was now an enterprise "reducible to calculation" under French naval protection, and warned that any absence could open a way to relieve Yorktown, leave the army without supplies and put peace further off than ever.[^s3] De Grasse stayed.[^s2] Repairs and weather held the British relief fleet in New York until October 19 — the day Cornwallis surrendered.[^s1][^s5]

The battle is remembered at the Cape Henry Memorial, part of Colonial National Historical Park, where a statue of de Grasse looks out over the water where it was fought.[^s6]

## Debates and interpretations

Writers agree on what the battle achieved but differ on how to describe the fight itself. Mount Vernon's encyclopedia calls it a tactical French victory, pointing to heavier British losses and Graves's retreat.[^s2] The American Battlefield Trust records it as a French victory but notes that the fleets pulled apart at sunset with no decisive result.[^s4] Its strategic weight is less disputed; Britannica quotes the British naval historian William M. James calling it "the decisive battle of the war."[^s5] A second debate concerns responsibility for the British failure. The Park Service stresses Graves's hesitation and unclear signals; Britannica describes a poorly managed attack.[^s1][^s5] A third concerns credit: scholars of Spanish participation argue that Spain's financing and naval cover, not only French ships and American armies, belong in the story.[^s7][^s8]

## Connections

- **Part of the American Revolutionary War**: the battle was the naval turning point of the war's final campaign.[^s1]
- **Occurred in Chesapeake Bay**: it was fought near the bay's mouth, outside Cape Henry, for control of the bay.[^s4]
- **Led by the comte de Grasse and Thomas Graves**: de Grasse commanded the French fleet; Graves commanded the British fleet, which included Hood's squadron.[^s1]
- **Led to the Siege of Yorktown**: French command of the bay cut Cornwallis off from relief by sea and enabled the allied siege that ended in his surrender on October 19, 1781.[^s2]
